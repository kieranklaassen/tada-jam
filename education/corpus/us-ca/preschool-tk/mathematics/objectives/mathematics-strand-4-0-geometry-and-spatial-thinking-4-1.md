## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years), a child identifies well-known flat shapes; circle, square, triangle and rectangle are the ones named. A note for this age says the child may fail to identify an unusual version of a shape, for instance a square rotated to stand on a corner. At the later age (4 to 5½ years), the child identifies, describes and makes a range of shapes, covering varied forms of those four and other shapes too, and uses informal words for the properties that define a shape (sides, corners and round are the examples).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child points at a plate and says circle, or picks the triangle out of a set of shape blocks. An older child recognises a long thin triangle or a tilted square, makes a triangle from three sticks or a rectangle from clay, and says things like "it has three sides and three pointy corners".

### Limits

Flat shapes only; solid shapes are foundation 4.2. Earlier: familiar shapes in their usual form; the note excuses a child who does not recognise an unusual version. Later: variations of the four named shapes and other shapes, which are not listed; the describing is in informal language, not formal terms or definitions. What changes with age: from naming typical examples to recognising varied ones, making shapes, and talking about their properties.

### Common mistakes

Rejecting a triangle that is not point-up with equal sides, calling it upside down or not a real one. Treating a tilted square as a different shape. Calling anything pointy a triangle, including shapes with curved sides or a gap in the outline.
