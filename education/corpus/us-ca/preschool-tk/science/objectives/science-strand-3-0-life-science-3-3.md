## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child expects people and animals to start moving by themselves, and to have insides and body processes unlike those of lifeless objects, which is why they behave differently from such objects. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child shows they know how living and nonliving things differ. The older child knows that biological changes, such as dying, healing, getting ill and growing, happen to living things alone: people, animals and plants.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child is not surprised when the cat walks off by itself but looks for who pushed the ball, and says the dog has bones and a tummy inside while the toy dog has stuffing. An older child sorts a stone, a toy car, a tree, a bird and a child into living and not living, and says the tree grows and can die and the cut on a knee heals, while the broken toy has to be mended by someone.

### Limits

Earlier: animate things are people and animals only; plants are not named at this age. The earlier statement is about what the child expects, so it shows in surprise and in predictions more than in words. Later: living things are people, animals and plants; the biological changes named (growing, getting ill, healing, dying) are examples. The statement does not ask for a definition of life. What changes with age: plants join the living things, and the child goes from expecting self-movement to knowing that growing, falling ill, healing and dying happen to living things alone.

### Common mistakes

Counting anything that moves as alive: clouds, a rolling ball, a wind-up toy, the sun. Counting plants as not alive because they do not move from place to place. Thinking dead means asleep or broken and can be undone.
