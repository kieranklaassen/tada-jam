## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

This takes place while the child plays and investigates together with others. At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child watches and tells how the direction and speed of things change, and explores what their own actions, for example dropping, rolling, pulling or pushing, do to start or stop a thing moving. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child predicts how a thing's speed, direction or distance traveled will change and tests the prediction. The older child then uses what was observed to explain why things start moving, stop, or change speed or direction.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child pushes a toy truck softly and then hard, rolls a ball into a wall and watches it come back, and says "it went fast" or "it stopped". An older child says "the car will go further if the ramp is higher", tries it, marks where the car stopped, and says "it stopped because the carpet is rough".

### Limits

The actions listed for the earlier age (dropping, rolling, pulling, pushing) are examples. Earlier: observing, describing, and exploring the effect of the child's own actions; no prediction is asked. Later: predicting, testing and explaining; distance is named only at the later age. The statement does not use the words force, friction or gravity, and asks for no measuring with units. What changes with age: from describing what happened to predicting it beforehand and explaining why.

### Common mistakes

Believing a moving thing stops by itself because it "gets tired", with no thought of the floor rubbing against it. Expecting a heavier ball to fall or roll faster in every case. Believing a thing moves only while it is being pushed, so a rolling ball must still have a push inside it.
