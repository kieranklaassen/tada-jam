## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

A child picks out and describes what various plants and animals are like, and sorts them into groups. At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) this includes how they behave and how they look, outside and inside, and the ability to sort is just beginning. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child does this for a wider range of plants and animals and is better at sorting them.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child looks at a snail and says it has a shell and goes slowly, opens a pea pod or cuts an apple with an adult to see the seeds inside, and puts toy animals with wings in one pile. An older child describes more kinds: a fish has fins and scales and lives in water, a cactus has spines and no leaves. The older child sorts pictures into birds, fish and insects, or into plants with flowers and plants without.

### Limits

Earlier: appearance, both outside and inside, and behavior are named as characteristics; sorting is called emerging. Later: a greater variety and a stronger ability to sort; the later wording does not repeat the list of characteristics. The statement names no particular animals, plants or categories and sets no number. What changes with age: more kinds of living things and more reliable sorting.

### Common mistakes

Sorting by one visible feature or by where a thing lives: a bat is a bird because it flies, a whale is a fish because it swims. Not counting people as animals, or trees as plants. Imagining the inside of an animal as empty or as filled with what it last ate.
