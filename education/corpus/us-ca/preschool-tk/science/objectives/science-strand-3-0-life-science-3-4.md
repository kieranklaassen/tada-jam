## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child names and describes the ways in which adult animals, people included, are like and unlike their young, as the child observes them. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child makes known that they expect the young of a kind of animal or plant to have characteristics like those of grown ones of that same kind.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child looks at a hen and a chick and says "both have beaks; the chick is yellow and fluffy and little", or says "I have brown eyes like Daddy but I'm small". An older child sees a puppy and says it will be a big dog with a long tail like its mother, or plants a bean and says a bean plant will come up, not a flower.

### Limits

Earlier: animals, humans included; plants are not named at this age. The child describes likenesses and differences they can observe. Later: animals and plants; the child expresses an expectation, and the statement does not ask why offspring resemble their parents. The words heredity and trait are in the title only, not in the age statements. What changes with age: from describing a grown animal and its young side by side to expecting what the young will be like, and plants are added.

### Common mistakes

Expecting a baby animal to look like a small copy of the adult, and so not linking a caterpillar to a butterfly or a tadpole to a frog. Thinking a puppy raised by a cat will grow up to be a cat. Matching young to parent by color alone.
