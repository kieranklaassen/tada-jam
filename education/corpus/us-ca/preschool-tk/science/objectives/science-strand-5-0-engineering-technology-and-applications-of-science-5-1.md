## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At both ages a child takes part in engineering design together with other children and grown-ups: finding a problem in play or daily activities, planning and making a solution to it, then testing the solution and improving it. At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the solutions are simple, and the testing and improving happen with adult support. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the solutions are more detailed, and the child tests and improves them with less adult support and over longer stretches of time.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child wants a bridge for toy cars across two chairs, lays a strip of card across with a friend, sees it sag, and with an adult's help puts a block under the middle. An older child draws or tells a plan for a marble run, builds it from tubes and tape, tests where the marble falls out, fixes that spot, and comes back the next day to make the run longer.

### Limits

The work is collaborative with peers and adults at both ages. The problems come from play and everyday activities; none is named. Earlier: simple solutions; adult support is stated for testing and refining. Later: more detailed solutions, less adult support, longer periods; no length is given. The statement does not ask for drawings or for particular materials. What changes with age: more detail in the solution, less adult help in testing and improving, and work that lasts longer.

### Common mistakes

Building at once with no plan and then being unable to say what the thing is for. Treating the first build as finished and taking a failed test as the end, not as a reason to change it. Knocking the whole thing down and starting over when one part fails, instead of changing that part.
