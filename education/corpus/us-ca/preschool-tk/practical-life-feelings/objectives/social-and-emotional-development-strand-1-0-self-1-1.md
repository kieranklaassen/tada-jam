## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years), children notice, and communicate to others, features of how they look that go with particular identities (ethnicity, race and gender are the examples), and they are starting to prefer certain activities, for example in how they dress or what they play. At the later age (4 to 5½ years), they express who they are (the same kinds of identity are given as examples) and show pride in it. They also let others know what they like about how they look or what they enjoy doing, for example by sharing what their family does or their own likes.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child looks in a mirror and says "I have brown skin like my dad" or "I'm a girl", picks the same dress-up cape each day, and asks for the sandbox first. An older child draws a self-portrait with matching skin and hair colours, says "I'm proud of my curly hair", and tells the group how the family keeps a holiday or which clothes they like to wear.

### Limits

The identities and preferences listed are examples; the statements do not say which a child should express. Pride in identity appears only at the later age. Neither statement mentions adult support. What changes with age: noticing and communicating about appearance becomes expressing an identity with pride, and emerging preferences become preferences the child lets others know about, family practices among them.

### Common mistakes

Children treat a changeable feature, such as hair length or clothing, as the thing that decides whether someone is a boy or a girl. A child assumes that every family does what their own family does. Children state a preference as a rule for others ("pink is only for girls").
