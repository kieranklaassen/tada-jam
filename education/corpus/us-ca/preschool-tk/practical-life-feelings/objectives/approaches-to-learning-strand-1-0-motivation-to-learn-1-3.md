## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years), children keep their attention and effort on what they are doing for a short while when an adult supports them. At the later age (4 to 5½ years), they stay focused for longer and need less of that support.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child sits with a puzzle or a tray of beads for a few minutes while an adult sits near, names what the child is doing and hands over the next piece. An older child works through the whole puzzle, or builds for a longer stretch, and looks up for an adult only now and then.

### Limits

The statements give no length of time in minutes; they say only "brief" at the earlier age and "longer" at the later age. The foundations document notes, beneath the two statements, that as a general guide a child stays with one activity for roughly 3 to 12 minutes at the earlier age and for up to roughly 8 to 20 minutes at the later age, and that how long depends, among other things, on the child, on how much the activity interests them, on the setting and on the support adults give. Adult support is part of both statements. What changes with age: the time a child stays focused grows, and less adult support is needed.

### Common mistakes

A child handles the materials without a purpose, which can look like concentration. Attention often ends when the task becomes hard or when the adult walks away. A child shows long focus on one favourite activity and very short focus elsewhere, so a single observation says little.
