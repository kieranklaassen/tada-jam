## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years), children keep going with what they are doing through setbacks or boredom when an adult supports them. With that support they also try to handle the feelings that come up (sadness, anger, excitement and frustration are the examples given), though they may move to something else after a short time. At the later age (4 to 5½ years), they keep going for longer, handle those feelings more consistently, and can stay with the activity with less adult support.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child whose tower falls cries or shouts, accepts a hug or a calm word from an adult, tries again once or twice, and then may walk away to something else. An older child says "this is hard", takes a breath or asks for one hint, and rebuilds the tower several times.

### Limits

The statements set no length of time and no number of attempts. The feelings named are examples. At the earlier age adult support is stated for the persisting and for the coping, and the statement itself allows that the child may change activity after a short while. What changes with age: persistence lasts longer, coping with feelings is more consistent, and less adult support is needed.

### Common mistakes

A young child takes one failure as proof that the task cannot be done, and stops. Strong feelings come out as throwing or knocking down the materials, not as words. A child repeats the exact action that failed instead of changing it.
