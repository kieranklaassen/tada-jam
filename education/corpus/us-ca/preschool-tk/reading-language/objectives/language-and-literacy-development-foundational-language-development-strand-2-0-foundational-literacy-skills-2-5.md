## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child is aware that a sound belongs to a written character or letter. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child, with adult support, correctly picks out or says the sounds of several letters or common characters. A separate sentence, for a child whose alphabet is the English or Spanish one, or one like it (Tagalog is the example given), asks for the sounds of around half the letters, correctly picked out or said.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child points at a letter and asks "What does that one say?", or makes a sound while pointing at the first letter of their name, even if it is the wrong sound. An older child, with an adult beside them, picks up the S and says "sss", or is shown the letter M and picks /m/ when the adult asks "Is it /m/ or /s/?".

### Limits

Earlier: only the idea that written symbols stand for sounds; the child is not asked to know any particular sound. Later: with an adult's support, the sounds of several letters or of common characters; either identifying a sound or saying it is enough. For English, Spanish and alphabets like them (Tagalog is named), about half of the letters; the statement does not say which letters, or whether uppercase or lowercase. Adult support is stated in the general sentence and is not repeated in the sentence about those alphabets. As worded, the child goes from a letter to its sound; writing a letter for a sound heard is foundation 4.2. Sounding out whole words is not mentioned. What changes with age: from knowing that a letter has a sound to giving the right sound for several letters, or about half of them in an alphabet like the English one.

### Common mistakes

Giving the letter's name when asked for its sound, most often for letters whose name does not begin with their sound, such as W, H and Y. Thinking a letter "says" a whole word, such as M "says Mommy". Adding a vowel after a consonant sound ("muh" for /m/), which makes blending harder later.
