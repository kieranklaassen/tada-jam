## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

A child connects the name of a letter with how that letter looks in print. At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) this holds for some letters, usually ones from the child's own first name; for a child whose alphabet is the English or Spanish one, or one like it (Tagalog is the example given), that means roughly three to eight uppercase letters. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) it holds for many letters; with that same kind of alphabet, most of the uppercase letters (roughly 15 to 20) and around half of the lowercase ones (roughly 12 to 16).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child finds the first letter of their own name on a block or a sign and says its name, or picks the M card when an adult asks "Which one is M?". An older child names most uppercase letters on magnetic letters or in a book title, and many lowercase ones, including letters that are not in their name.

### Limits

Earlier: some letters, usually those of the child's first name. For English, Spanish, and alphabets like them (Tagalog is named), about three to eight uppercase letters; lowercase is not mentioned at this age. Later: many letters; for those alphabets about 15 to 20 uppercase, which the statement calls most, and about 12 to 16 lowercase, which it calls approximately half. Every count is given as "about", not as an exact score, and the statement does not say which letters. For other alphabets only "some" and "many" are stated. The foundations document notes, beneath the two statements, that this foundation is for languages written with an alphabet only (it gives Arabic, Vietnamese, Spanish and English as examples), and that Chinese characters, used for Mandarin and Cantonese, are not an alphabet. This statement is about letter names and print: letter sounds are foundation 2.5, and writing letters is in strand 4. What changes with age: from some letters, usually from the child's own name, to many; for English, Spanish and alphabets like them, from about three to eight uppercase letters to most uppercase and about half of lowercase.

### Common mistakes

Mixing up letters that differ only by direction or a small stroke: b and d, p and q, M and W, E and F. Knowing a letter only as "my letter" in the child's own name and not knowing it in another word or another typeface. Knowing the uppercase form but not the lowercase one where the two look different, such as G and g, or R and r.
