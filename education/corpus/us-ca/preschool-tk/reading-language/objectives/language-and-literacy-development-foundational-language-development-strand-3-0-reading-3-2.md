## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child, once they have met a story a few times, shows a basic grasp of who it is mainly about or what mainly happens. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the child shows a grasp of the story's details: who is in it, what happens, and in what order. The older child also draws on a growing sense of how stories are built to say, when asked, what may happen next.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child, after the third reading, points to the wolf and says "he blows the house down", or acts out the main event with toys. An older child puts three or four picture cards of the story in order, tells who did what, and when the adult stops and asks "What will happen now?" gives a guess that fits the story.

### Limits

Earlier: main characters or main events (either is enough), at a basic level, and only after the child has met the story a few times; a first hearing is not what is asked. Later: details, including characters, events and their order, and a prediction when asked; the child is not expected to offer one unprompted. The later statement does not repeat the condition of having met the story several times. The statement does not ask the child to read the story themselves. What changes with age: from the main points of a well-known story to its details, its order, and a guess at what comes next.

### Common mistakes

Retelling the single most exciting event and leaving out how the story began or ended. Mixing up the order of events, most often the middle ones. Predicting from their own wishes or from another story they know, not from what has happened so far.
