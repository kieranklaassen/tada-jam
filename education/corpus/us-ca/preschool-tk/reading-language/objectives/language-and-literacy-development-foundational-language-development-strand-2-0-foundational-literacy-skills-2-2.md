## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

This is about putting spoken parts together into a word, with help from an adult or from objects or pictures. At the earlier age (3 to 4½ years) the child hears two words of one syllable each, for instance the two halves of "sandbox", and says the compound word they make. At the later age (4 to 5½ years) the parts given are syllables and single sounds, and the child joins them into a spoken word.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child hears an adult say "rain" and then "bow" while holding up two pictures, and says "rainbow". An older child hears "ta" and "ble" and says "table", or hears /m/, /a/, /p/ said slowly and says "map". All of it is listening and speaking; no letters are involved.

### Limits

Earlier: only two words of one syllable each, joined into a compound word. Later: syllables and individual sounds, joined into words. The statement sets no number of syllables or sounds per word. All blending is in speech; letters and print are not mentioned. At both ages the child has support from an adult, pictures or objects. The statement covers blending only; taking a word apart is not mentioned here. The foundations document notes, beneath the two statements, that languages differ in how long their compound words are, so a child whose language has no short compounds may not show the earlier-age skill; it says that compounds of two syllables are common in Chinese and English, while Spanish compounds mostly have three syllables or more. What changes with age: the parts get smaller, from whole words to syllables and single sounds.

### Common mistakes

Repeating the parts as given, with the pause still between them, instead of saying one word. Saying only the last part heard ("box" for "sand" and "box"). With single sounds, guessing a known word from the first sound alone ("mum" for /m/, /a/, /p/).
