## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child gives meaning to a fraction divided by a fraction, works out such quotients, and solves word problems that call for this division, for example by representing the problem with equations and visual fraction models. As examples, the child invents a story for (2/3) ÷ (3/4), shows the quotient with a visual fraction model, and explains from the link between division and multiplication that the answer is 8/9, since 2/3 is 3/4 of 8/9; the general rule given is that (a/b) ÷ (c/d) equals ad/bc. The sample problems ask how much each of 3 people gets when 1/2 lb of chocolate is shared equally, how many servings of 3/4 cup fit in 2/3 cup of yogurt, and how wide a rectangular strip of land is when its area is 1/2 square mile and its length 3/4 mile.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child takes a strip that stands for 2/3 of a cup and measures it against a strip for one 3/4-cup serving, finding that less than one full serving fits. The child draws both fractions on bars cut into twelfths, 8 twelfths and 9 twelfths, and reads the quotient 8/9 from the two counts. The child tells a story for the division, writes the matching equation, and checks the quotient by multiplying it by the divisor.

### Limits

Both the number divided and the divisor may be fractions; one of the sample problems shares a fraction among a whole number of people. The statement gives no list of denominators and does not mention mixed numbers. Equations and visual fraction models are named as examples of ways to represent a problem. The cluster heading adds that dividing a fraction by a fraction grows out of what the child already understands about multiplication and division.

### Common mistakes

Turning the wrong fraction upside down, or both fractions, when using the invert-and-multiply rule. Expecting a quotient to be smaller than the number divided, so that 8/9 looks wrong as an answer for 2/3 divided by something. Setting the division up the wrong way round: writing 3/4 ÷ 2/3 for "how many 3/4 are in 2/3".
