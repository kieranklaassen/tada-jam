## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child tells whether two expressions are equivalent, which means that both give the same number whatever value is put in for the letter. As an example, 3y and y + y + y are equivalent, since the two always come out equal no matter what number y is.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child takes two expression cards, tries several numbers in both, and records the results in two columns. When one number gives different results, the child says the pair is not equivalent. When every number tried gives matching results, the child looks for a property that explains why the two must always match. The child sorts a set of cards into piles of equivalent expressions.

### Limits

Equivalent here means equal for every value of the letter, not for one chosen value. The statement sets no limit on the kinds of expressions; its one example has a single letter and a whole-number coefficient. The cluster heading adds that this work takes the arithmetic the child already knows and carries it over to algebraic expressions.

### Common mistakes

Deciding that two expressions are equivalent because one tested number gives the same result (2x and x² when x is 2). Judging by appearance and taking 2 (x + 3) and 2x + 3 for the same. Thinking that x + x and x² mean the same thing.
