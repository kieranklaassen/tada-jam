## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child shows a three-dimensional figure as a net, a flat pattern built from rectangles and triangles, and finds the figure's surface area from the net. The child uses this on problems from real life and from mathematics.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child cuts a cereal box along its edges and lays it flat to see six rectangles. The child draws a net for a square pyramid on grid paper, cuts it out, and folds it to check that it closes. The child unfolds it again, finds the area of each face, and adds the areas, marking each face so that none is counted twice or left out.

### Limits

The nets are made of rectangles and triangles only, so every face of the solid is a rectangle or a triangle; a solid with a curved surface cannot be shown this way. The statement gives no formulas for surface area and sets no limit on edge lengths. The cluster heading names three subjects for the problems of this group: area, surface area and volume. This statement is the one about surface area.

### Common mistakes

Leaving out a face, often the base or a face at the back. Mixing up surface area and volume and multiplying three edge lengths. Drawing a net that has the right faces but cannot fold shut because two faces land on the same place.
