## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child recognises what makes a question statistical: it expects the data collected for it to vary, and its answer takes that variation into account. As an example, asking the ages of the students in a school is a statistical question because their ages will differ, while asking one person's own age is not.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child sorts question cards into two piles: questions with one fixed answer ("How tall is our teacher?") and questions whose answer needs data from many people or things ("How tall are the children in our class?"). The child rewrites a one-answer question so that it becomes statistical. Before collecting anything, the child predicts what different answers might come in.

### Limits

The statement is about recognising such questions; displaying and summarising data are covered by other statements of this grade (6.SP.4, 6.SP.5). It sets no limit on what a question is about. The cluster heading names the aim of this group: a growing understanding of variability in statistics.

### Common mistakes

Calling every question with a number as its answer statistical. Thinking a question is statistical because it is asked of many people, even when everyone gives the same answer. Expecting a statistical question to have one exact answer instead of a description of how the data vary.
