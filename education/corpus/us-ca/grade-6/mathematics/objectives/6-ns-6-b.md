## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child understands that each rational number is one point of the number line, and stretches the number lines and coordinate axes known from earlier grades so that points on a line and in the plane can have negative coordinates. In this part the child understands that the signs of the two numbers in an ordered pair tell which quadrant of the coordinate plane the point is in. The child also recognises that two ordered pairs that are the same except for signs give points that are reflections of each other across one axis or both.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child names the quadrant of (3, 2), (−3, 2), (−3, −2) and (3, −2) from their signs and then plots the four points on a four-quadrant grid. The child folds the paper along the y-axis and sees (3, 2) land on (−3, 2), then folds along the x-axis as well. Given a point, the child predicts where its mirror image across each axis lies and writes its coordinates.

### Limits

The stem speaks of rational numbers, so coordinates may be fractions or decimals of either sign. This part deals with signs, quadrants and reflection across the x-axis, the y-axis or both; other reflections and other movements of a figure are not mentioned. The statement sets no number range. The cluster heading adds that the numbers the child already knows are being extended to the whole system of rational numbers.

### Common mistakes

Changing the sign of the wrong coordinate, for example reflecting across the x-axis by changing the x-coordinate. Mixing up which pattern of signs goes with which quadrant. Thinking that a point on an axis, such as (0, 4), lies in a quadrant.
