## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student argues, from empirical evidence and scientific reasoning, for an explanation of how typical animal behaviours and specialised plant structures change the chance that animals and plants reproduce successfully. The clarification's examples for animal reproduction are calls and bright feathers that draw mates, herding that shields the young from predators, and nests built to keep the young from the cold. Its examples for plant reproduction are animals that carry pollen or seeds or make conditions in which seeds sprout and grow, and plant structures such as hard nutshells that squirrels bury, and nectar, scent and bright flowers that draw insects, butterflies among them, which carry pollen.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The student watches flowers in the schoolyard for ten minutes and tallies how many insects visit bright flowers and how many visit dull ones. The student handles seeds with hooks, wings and hard shells and notes how each could be moved. The student writes a claim, the tallies that support it, and the reasoning that links more visits to more pollen carried and more seeds.

### Limits

The record prints no assessment boundary.

### Common mistakes

Saying that an animal or a plant acts "in order to" reproduce, as if it planned to. Thinking flowers are decoration and that plants do not reproduce sexually. Saying a behaviour guarantees offspring, when it changes how likely they are.
