## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child writes texts that inform or explain; these include accounts of technical processes, of scientific experiments or procedures, and of events in history. The piece introduces its topic clearly with a preview of what comes next, sorts its information, concepts and ideas into broader categories that suit its purpose, and uses multimedia, graphics (tables and charts are the examples) and formatting (headings are the example) where they help the reader understand. The topic is built up with well-chosen, relevant material: examples, quotations, concrete details, definitions, facts or other information. Varied and fitting transitions hold the piece together and show how concepts and ideas relate, and exact wording and the subject's own vocabulary do the informing or explaining. The style is formal and the tone objective throughout, and a closing section or statement follows from what was explained or presented and supports it.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child writes up an event in history, such as the building of a railway, or the steps of an experiment on dissolving salt. The child starts with two sentences that say what the piece covers, groups the notes under headings, and adds a table of results or a labelled timeline. The child uses the subject's terms ("solute", "solvent"), joins the parts with words such as "next" and "as a result", and ends with a paragraph that sums up what was explained.

### Limits

The standard is for the band of grades 6 to 8 and is recorded here at grade 6; it sets no separate level for grade 6. Three kinds of account are named as included: historical events, scientific procedures or experiments, technical processes. Headings, charts and tables are examples, and formatting, graphics and multimedia are for use when they help understanding, not always. The lane's frame records that WHST.6-8.3 is a placeholder and not a separate requirement; this stem places narration inside informative writing. A note printed under this writing table in the adopted document, which belongs to that placeholder row and not to this record, says that students must be able to bring narrative elements into arguments and into texts that inform or explain: in history and social studies as narrative accounts inside analyses of people or events that matter historically, and in science and technical subjects as descriptions of the steps of a procedure exact enough for someone else to repeat the work and perhaps get the same results. No length is set.

### Common mistakes

Children write down everything they know in the order they think of it, with no grouping. They add opinion ("this was a great invention") where an objective tone is asked for. They use everyday words where the subject has an exact term, and they stop without a closing section.
