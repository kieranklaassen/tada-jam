## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student presents claims and findings aloud, in forms such as an informative talk, a narrative, an argument or a response to literature. The ideas come in a logical order, and the main ideas or themes are brought out with fitting facts, descriptions and details and with nonverbal elements. The student looks at the audience appropriately, speaks loudly enough and pronounces words clearly. The student also plans and gives an informative or explanatory presentation: it builds a topic from relevant definitions, facts and concrete details, links its points with suitable transitions, uses exact wording and the vocabulary of the subject, and closes with a strong conclusion.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The student prepares a four-minute talk explaining how a volcano erupts: writes an outline, defines "magma" and "vent", and picks three concrete details. Practising in front of a partner, the student looks up from the notes, speaks so that the back row can hear, and uses a hand gesture or a pause before the main point. The student says "first", "as a result" and "in the end" to move between parts and finishes with a sentence that sums up the explanation.

### Limits

Four kinds of presentation are given as examples: argument, narrative, informative, and response to literature. Three delivery features are named: eye contact, volume and pronunciation. Part a applies to one kind, the informative or explanatory presentation, and lists what it must contain. No length or audience size is set. California adds to the main statement and to part a, and the record does not show which words are the addition.

### Common mistakes

Students read the whole talk from a script and never look up. They speak too fast or too quietly when nervous. They give facts in the order they found them, with no transitions, and stop without a conclusion ("that's it").
