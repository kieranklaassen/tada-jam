## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student applies what they know about language and its conventions in all four modes: reading, listening, speaking and writing. The student changes the patterns of their sentences to serve the meaning, to suit the style and to hold a reader's or listener's interest. The student keeps tone and style the same throughout a piece.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The student reads a draft aloud, hears five short sentences in a row that all start with "I", and joins two of them or opens one with a time phrase. The student places a short sentence after several long ones so that one point stands out. In a report, the student finds a chatty line such as "it was super cool" and rewrites it to match the rest. Listening to a talk, the student says where the speaker's manner changed.

### Limits

The stem names all four modes (writing, speaking, reading, listening); parts a and b are about what the student produces. Part a gives three purposes for changing sentence patterns: style, meaning, and keeping a reader or listener interested. It names no sentence types. Part b does not say which tone or style, only that it stays the same. A footnote explains the asterisk on both parts: it is used from grade 3 onward for skills that students will probably have to keep working on in later grades, as their speech and writing become more demanding. The document's table of the language skills that need this continued attention gives part a one more mark, and the note to that mark says the skill is taken up into a part of a Language standard for grades 11 and 12.

### Common mistakes

Students write every sentence in the same subject-then-verb pattern, or vary by joining many clauses with "and" until the sentence runs on. They change from formal wording to slang, or from a serious tone to a joke, partway through a piece without noticing. Some change sentences only for variety and make the meaning harder to follow.
