## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

In writing and in speech, the student shows control of standard English grammar and usage. The student puts each pronoun in the right case: subject, object or possessive. The student handles every kind of pronoun without error, intensive pronouns such as ourselves and myself among them. The student notices and fixes a pronoun that changes number or person where it should not, and a pronoun whose antecedent is unclear or could be more than one thing. The student also notices where their own or other people's speech and writing departs from standard English, and finds and applies ways to express the same thing better in conventional language.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The student reads a classmate's paragraph, circles "Me and him went", and rewrites it as "He and I went". The student finds a paragraph that starts with "we" and changes to "you" halfway, and makes the pronouns match. Reading "When Maya called Rosa, she was upset", the student asks who "she" is and rewrites the sentence so that only one reading is left. The student says a sentence the way they would to a friend and then the way they would write it in a report.

### Limits

Both writing and speaking count. Three pronoun cases are named: subject, object and possessive. Intensive pronouns are shown by two examples only, and the statement gives no full list of pronouns. Part e names no particular strategies. California adds to part b, and the record does not show which words are the addition. A footnote explains the asterisk on parts c, d and e: from grade 3 on, it marks a skill that will probably keep needing work in later grades, because what students write and say becomes more demanding.

### Common mistakes

Students use an object pronoun in a compound subject ("Me and Sam went") and then overcorrect to a subject pronoun after a preposition ("between you and I"). They use a "-self" form where a plain pronoun belongs ("Give it to myself") or invent forms such as "theirselves". They write "he" or "it" when two earlier nouns could be meant and do not see the problem, because they know whom they mean.
