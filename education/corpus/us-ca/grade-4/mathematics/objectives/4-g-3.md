## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child knows what a line of symmetry of a two-dimensional figure is: a line through the figure along which it could be folded so that the two parts match. The child picks out figures that have line symmetry and draws their lines of symmetry.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child folds a paper shape so that one half lies exactly on the other, opens it, and draws along the crease. The child tries other folds of that shape to find every line of symmetry (four for a square) and puts aside the shapes for which no fold works. For a printed figure that cannot be folded, the child stands a small mirror on the line and compares the reflection with the hidden half.

### Limits

Line symmetry only, defined by folding; the statement does not mention turning (rotational) symmetry. Figures are flat. No limit is set on how many lines of symmetry a figure has. The cluster heading speaks of lines, angles and sorting shapes; it adds no limit on symmetry.

### Common mistakes

Taking any line that cuts a shape into two equal halves for a line of symmetry, such as the diagonal of a rectangle that is not a square. Finding the vertical line only and missing horizontal and slanted ones. Saying that a parallelogram has a line of symmetry because its two halves are alike.
