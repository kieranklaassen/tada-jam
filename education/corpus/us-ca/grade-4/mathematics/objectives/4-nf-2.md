## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child compares two fractions whose numerators differ and whose denominators differ. Ways named as examples are giving both fractions one denominator or one numerator, and judging each against a benchmark such as 1/2. The child knows that such a comparison only holds when both fractions are parts of one same whole, writes the outcome with <, = or >, and gives a reason for it, for example with a visual fraction model.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

To compare 3/8 and 4/6 the child says "3/8 is less than a half and 4/6 is more than a half" and places the < card between them. For 2/3 and 3/4 the child renames both as twelfths (8/12 and 9/12), or lays two fraction strips of equal length one under the other. Before comparing, the child checks that both fractions are of wholes of one size.

### Limits

A footnote to the fractions domain (recorded with 4.NF.1) allows only these denominators in grade 4: 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, then 8, 10 and 12, and also 100. Sevenths, ninths and elevenths are not used. A pair such as thirds and fifths has a common denominator (15) that is not on that list. Two fractions are compared at a time, and the result is written with <, = or >. The strategies in the statement are given as examples, not as required methods.

### Common mistakes

Taking the fraction with the larger denominator for the larger fraction (1/8 more than 1/6). Comparing numerators and denominators separately as whole numbers (4/10 more than 3/5 because 4 > 3 and 10 > 5). Calling 3/4 and 5/6 equal because each is one piece short of a whole.
