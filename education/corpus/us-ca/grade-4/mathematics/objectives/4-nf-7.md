## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child compares two decimals that go as far as hundredths by thinking about how large each one is. The child knows that the comparison only holds when both decimals are parts of one same whole, writes the outcome with <, = or >, and gives a reason, for example on a number line or with a different visual model.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child shades 0.4 on one hundred grid and 0.27 on a second grid of equal size, sees that 40 squares cover more than 27 squares, and places the > card between the two numbers. The child also marks both decimals on a number line from 0 to 1 and says which one is further to the right.

### Limits

Decimals have at most two digits after the point, and two decimals are compared at a time. Both refer to one same whole. The statement is marked as carrying a California addition. The number line and other visual models are named as examples of how to give the reason. A footnote in the adopted document permits just 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, then 8, 10 or 12, or else 100 as the denominator of a grade 4 fraction, and it stands on the whole domain, so it covers this statement.

### Common mistakes

Treating the digits after the point as a whole number, so that 0.27 is called larger than 0.4 because 27 is more than 4. The opposite rule: a decimal with more digits is always smaller, because hundredths are small (0.27 less than 0.2). Thinking that 0.4 and 0.40 are different amounts.
