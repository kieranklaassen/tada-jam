## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child divides a whole number of at most four digits (the dividend) by a single-digit number (the divisor) and gives the whole-number quotient and the remainder. The child may reason from place value, from the properties of operations, from the way multiplication and division undo each other, or from more than one of these. The child shows and explains the calculation with area models, rectangular arrays or equations, or with more than one of these.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

For 1,356 ÷ 4 the child shares out base-ten blocks into 4 groups: the thousand is traded for 10 hundreds, then hundreds, tens and ones are shared in turn, with a trade whenever some are left over. Or the child builds up multiples of 4 (4 × 300 = 1,200, leaving 156; 4 × 30 = 120, leaving 36; 4 × 9 = 36) and adds 300, 30 and 9 to get 339.

### Limits

The dividend has at most four digits (up to 9,999) and the divisor has one digit; a two-digit divisor is outside the statement. A footnote in the adopted document makes 1,000,000 the largest whole number expected in grade 4, and it stands on the whole domain, so it covers this statement. The answer is a whole-number quotient with a remainder, not a fraction or a decimal. The statement does not name the standard algorithm for long division.

### Common mistakes

Leaving out a zero in the quotient when a place cannot be shared (13 for 824 ÷ 8, which is 103). Stopping with a remainder that is as large as the divisor or larger. Losing the place of a quotient digit, so that 300, 30 and 9 are not kept apart as hundreds, tens and ones.
