## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

With visual fraction models, the child explains why multiplying both the numerator and the denominator of a fraction a/b by one number n gives an equivalent fraction, (n × a)/(n × b): there are more parts and each part is smaller, yet the amount is the same. The child uses this idea to spot equivalent fractions and to make new ones.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child folds a paper strip into 3 equal parts and shades 2 of them, then folds again so that every part is cut in two, and counts 4 shaded parts out of 6 while the shaded length stays as it was. The child says "twice as many parts, each half as big" and writes 2/3 = 4/6. With fraction tiles the child lines up pieces under one another to find other names for one length.

### Limits

A footnote limits the fractions of this domain in grade 4 to these denominators: 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, then 8, 10 and 12, and also 100. Sevenths, ninths and elevenths are therefore not used, and an equivalent fraction is in range only when its denominator is one of the nine listed. The explanation is made with visual fraction models. The statement is phrased as multiplying numerator and denominator by n; it does not ask for a fraction in lowest terms.

### Common mistakes

Adding one number to the numerator and the denominator where multiplying is meant (2/3 "equals" 3/4). Believing that the fraction written with larger numbers is the larger amount (4/6 more than 2/3). Multiplying only the numerator or only the denominator.
