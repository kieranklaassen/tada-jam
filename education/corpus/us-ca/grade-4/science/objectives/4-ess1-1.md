## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student picks out evidence, in the patterns of rock formations and of fossils found in layers of rock, that backs up an explanation of how a landscape has changed through time. The clarification's example is a layer holding sea-shell fossils that lies on top of layers holding plant fossils without shells, which indicates land that later came to be under water.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The student studies a photo or a layered clay-and-sand model of a cliff, sketches the layers, and labels which fossils sit in which layer. The student puts the layers in order from earliest to latest and says what the place was like at each stage. The student points to the fossil that supports each step of the account.

### Limits

The assessment boundary leaves out how rock forms and any memorising of particular formations or layers. It keeps time relative: earlier and later. The shells-above-plants case is an example. The department's grade document differs here: its clarification gives a second example, a canyon with a river at the bottom and walls that show different layers of rock, as a sign that the river wore down through the rock over a long time.

### Common mistakes

Reading the top layer as the oldest. Thinking a sea fossil found on a hill means the animal was carried there, not that the place was once under water. Describing the layers without using them as evidence for a change.
