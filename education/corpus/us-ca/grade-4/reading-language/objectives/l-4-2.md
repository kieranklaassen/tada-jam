## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

When writing, the child follows the standard English rules for capital letters, punctuation and spelling. Capital letters go where they belong. Direct speech and words quoted from a text are set off with quotation marks and commas, and in a compound sentence a comma comes ahead of the coordinating conjunction. Words at grade level are spelled right, with a reference checked when the child needs one.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child writes a short story in which two characters talk. The child puts a capital letter on each name and at the start of each sentence, puts each speaker's words inside quotation marks with a comma between the words and 'she said', and copies a line from a book with quotation marks around it. Where two full sentences are joined with 'and' or 'but', the child adds the comma. Unsure of a spelling, the child opens a dictionary or word list and checks.

### Limits

The standard is about writing only. Part a does not say which words take a capital letter. Commas are named for two jobs: with spoken or quoted words, and ahead of the conjunction in a compound sentence. Spelling is limited to words suited to the grade, which the statement does not list, and looking a word up is allowed.

### Common mistakes

Children leave out the comma between the spoken words and the speaker tag, or put the closing quotation mark ahead of the final punctuation of the speech. They put a comma before every 'and', including an 'and' that only joins two words. They capitalise common nouns that feel important and miss capitals inside titles.
