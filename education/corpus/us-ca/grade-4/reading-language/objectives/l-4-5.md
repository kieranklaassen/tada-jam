## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child shows that they understand figurative language, how words relate to one another, and fine shades of difference in what words mean. In context, the child says what a simple metaphor or simile means; the example given is the stock simile that likens something pretty to a picture. The child knows common proverbs, adages and idioms on meeting them and can say what each one means. The child also shows what a word means by connecting it to its antonyms (opposites) and to its synonyms (words close in meaning without being identical).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child reads 'her hands were ice' in a story and tells a partner that the girl's hands were very cold, not frozen water. The child hears 'don't count your chickens before they hatch' and explains it in their own words with a case from their own week. Given 'large', the child writes 'tiny' as an opposite, then puts 'big', 'huge' and 'enormous' in order of strength and says how they differ.

### Limits

Similes and metaphors are limited to simple ones, explained in context. The idioms, adages and proverbs are common ones; the statement lists none. Part c covers antonyms and synonyms only. The statement does not ask the child to write figurative language of their own.

### Common mistakes

Children read a figurative phrase word for word and picture the literal scene. They explain an idiom by repeating it. They treat synonyms as exact equals and swap one for another where the strength or tone is wrong.
