## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

In writing, speaking, reading and listening, the child applies what they know about language and how it is conventionally handled. The child picks words and phrases that say an idea exactly, and selects punctuation marks for the effect they have. The child also tells apart settings where formal English is called for, such as presenting ideas, from settings where informal talk fits, such as a discussion in a small group.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child revising a story swaps 'went' for 'crept' or 'raced' so that the sentence says exactly how the character moved. The child tries one sentence with a full stop, then an exclamation mark, then a dash, reads each version aloud, and keeps the one that sounds the way they intend. Presenting a report to the class, the child says 'Good morning, my topic is volcanoes'; talking it over afterwards with three classmates at a table, the child speaks in a relaxed, everyday way.

### Limits

The standard applies across writing, speaking, reading and listening. It names no list of words or punctuation marks. The two settings in part c, a small-group discussion and presenting ideas, are examples. A footnote explains the asterisk on parts a and b: starting in grade 3, an asterisk marks skills that will probably still need work in later grades, as children's writing and speaking grow more advanced.

### Common mistakes

Asked to be precise, children choose a long or unusual word that does not fit the meaning. They put exclamation marks on most sentences. They write a report in the casual words they speak with friends, or go stiff and quiet when a setting is formal.
