## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child explains the main ways in which prose, drama and poems differ. When speaking or writing about a text, the child refers to the structural parts of a poem, such as meter, rhythm and verse, and of a drama, such as stage directions, dialogue, descriptions, settings and the cast of characters.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child is given one scene three ways: as a page of a novel, a page of a play script and a poem. The child points to the stage directions and the list of characters in the script, claps the rhythm of two lines of the poem, and tells a partner what each form has that the others lack. In a written response the child puts the terms 'verse', 'dialogue' and 'stage direction' in the right places.

### Limits

Three forms are named: poems, drama and prose. The structural elements listed for poems and for drama are examples; none are listed for prose. The differences asked for are the major ones.

### Common mistakes

Children think a poem must rhyme. They read stage directions aloud as if a character spoke them. They name an element such as 'rhythm' and cannot show it in the text.
