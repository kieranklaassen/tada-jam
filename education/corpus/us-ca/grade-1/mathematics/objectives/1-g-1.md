## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child tells apart the features that make a shape the kind of shape it is from the features that do not matter for its kind. The example of a feature that defines is that a triangle is closed and has three sides; the examples of features that do not define are size, colour and which way the shape is turned. The child also builds shapes and draws shapes that have the defining features.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child sorts a pile of cut-out shapes into triangles and not-triangles, and keeps a long thin triangle, a tiny one and one that points downward in the triangle pile. Asked why, the child says "three straight sides and no opening". The child builds a rectangle from straws and clay balls, or draws one on dot paper, and then checks its sides and corners.

### Limits

The statement gives no list of shapes; the triangle is its only example. The cluster heading speaks of shapes and their attributes in general and names no shapes either. The neighbouring statement on joining shapes names triangles, squares, rectangles, trapezoids, half-circles and quarter-circles. The statement sets no number of shapes to build or draw.

### Common mistakes

Rejecting a triangle that is long and thin, or that rests on one corner, because it does not look like the usual one. Accepting a three-sided figure with a gap or a curved side as a triangle. Treating a square that has been turned as a different shape and calling it a "diamond".
