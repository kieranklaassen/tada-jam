## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child cuts circles and rectangles into two or into four shares of the same size. The child names the shares as halves, fourths or quarters, speaks of one share as a half of, a fourth of or a quarter of the whole, and says that the whole is two of those shares or four of them. For these cases the child understands that cutting into more equal shares makes each share smaller.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child folds a paper circle so that the edges meet, cuts along the fold and says "two halves", then folds and cuts again for fourths. The child lays the four pieces back together to show that four quarters make the whole circle. The child holds one half beside one fourth cut from a circle of the same size and sees that the fourth is smaller.

### Limits

Only circles and rectangles, and only two or four equal shares. The shares are named in words; the statement does not mention written fraction notation. Thirds are not mentioned. The idea that more shares means smaller shares is stated for these examples only.

### Common mistakes

Accepting unequal pieces as halves and speaking of "the bigger half". Thinking a fourth is bigger than a half because four is more than two. Calling any piece "a half" whenever a shape has been cut, whatever the number of pieces.
