## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child knows the phonics and word analysis of the grade and uses them to decode words, whether a word stands alone or sits in a text. The child knows which sounds go with the spellings of the common consonant digraphs, and decodes one-syllable words whose spelling is regular. For long vowel sounds, the child knows two spelling conventions: a final -e and the common vowel teams. Knowing that a syllable always holds a vowel sound, the child works out how many syllables a printed word has; two-syllable words built on basic patterns are divided into syllables and decoded that way. The child also reads a word when an inflectional ending has been added to it, and irregularly spelled words that suit the grade are ones the child recognizes and can read.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child reads printed words aloud from cards and in sentences. In "ship", "chat" and "thin" two letters are read as one sound; "cap" is read and then "cape", "rod" and then "road". The child marks each vowel sound in a printed word to find how many syllables it has, draws a line between the two syllables of "napkin", reads each half and joins them. The child reads "jump", "jumps", "jumped" and "jumping", and reads words such as "said" and "was" whole, without sounding out each letter.

### Limits

Digraphs are the common consonant ones. Words to decode have one syllable and a regular spelling, or two syllables and a basic pattern. Long vowels are covered through two spellings: final -e and the common vowel teams. Syllables are counted in print by finding the vowel sounds. Inflectional endings, and the irregularly spelled words suited to the grade, complete the parts. The statement gives no list of digraphs, vowel teams, syllable patterns, endings or irregular words, and no example words. The longest words named for decoding have two syllables. California adds to the opening line, which carries the state's mark straight after the words on decoding both singly and in text; the stored wording does not show where the addition starts.

### Common mistakes

Sounding the two letters of a digraph separately (/s/ /h/ for sh), or reading a final -e word with a short vowel ("cap" for cape). Counting vowel letters instead of vowel sounds, so that "cake" or "rain" is given two syllables. Sounding out an irregular word letter by letter, or misreading an ending, so that "jumped" becomes "jump" or a two-syllable "jump-ed".
