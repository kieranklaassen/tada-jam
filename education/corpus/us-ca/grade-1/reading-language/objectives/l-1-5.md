## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

With adult guidance and support, the child shows understanding of how words relate to one another and of small differences in what they mean. The child sorts words into categories (clothing and colors are the examples) and so gets a sense of the idea each category stands for, and defines a word by naming its category plus at least one key attribute (for example, a tiger as a big striped cat, or a duck as a swimming bird). The child links words to real life and to how they are used (for example, noting which places at home are cozy). The child also tells apart verbs that differ in manner (such as scowl, glare, stare, glance, peek, look) and adjectives that differ in strength (such as gigantic and large), which is done by defining them, by choosing among them or by acting them out.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child sorts word cards into piles such as things to wear and colors, then says what belongs in each pile and why. Asked what a word means, the child names the kind of thing it is and one feature that marks it out. The child acts out peeking, staring and glaring in turn, or places small, big and huge objects under matching word cards, and points to a spot at home that fits a word such as cozy.

### Limits

The whole standard is done with guidance and support from adults. The categories, definitions and word sets in brackets are examples, not required lists. Shades of meaning cover two word classes: verbs that differ in manner and adjectives that differ in intensity. The child may show the difference by defining, by choosing or by acting out. A definition needs the category and at least one key attribute.

### Common mistakes

Defining a word by an example or by what the thing does ("a duck goes quack") and leaving out the category. Sorting by something other than meaning, such as the first letter. Treating near-synonyms as the same, so that glance and stare are acted out alike.
