## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child looks closely at flat and solid shapes of various sizes, turned various ways, and compares them. In everyday words the child says how they are alike and how they differ, what parts they have (for example how many sides and how many vertices, called corners), and what other features they have (for example sides that are equally long).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child holds a square and a triangle, runs a finger along each side while counting, taps each corner, and says "this one has four, this one has three". The child rolls a ball and a can and says which only rolls and which can also stand. The child lays one side against another to see whether they are the same length.

### Limits

Both flat and solid shapes are in scope, at any size and any turn. The language is informal: "corners" is accepted for vertices, and no definitions are asked for. Sides, corners and equal side lengths are examples of what to notice, not a full list, and the statement names no particular shapes.

### Common mistakes

Losing track while counting sides or corners and counting one twice, especially on a shape that is being turned in the hand. Describing a shape only by likeness to a thing ("it looks like a door") without naming any part. Saying two shapes differ only because one is bigger or turned.
