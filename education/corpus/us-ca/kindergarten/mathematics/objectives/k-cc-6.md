## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

Looking at two groups of things, the child tells whether the first holds more, fewer, or just as many as the second. Pairing things off one against one and counting both groups are named as example ways to find out.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child sets cups against saucers one to one and sees which kind has some left over. Or the child counts both piles and says which number is bigger, using words such as more, fewer and the same.

### Limits

A footnote says that groups holding as many as ten things are part of what this statement covers; ten is the largest group size it mentions, and it is worded as what is included, not as a cap. The comparison is between groups of objects; comparing written numerals is the next statement. Matching and counting are named as examples, not as the only ways.

### Common mistakes

Judging by the space a group takes up, so that a spread-out row of five is called more than a tight row of seven. Judging by the size of the things, so that three big blocks are called more than five small ones. Counting both groups correctly and then not knowing which number means more.
