## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child shows a grasp of what spoken language is made of: single sounds (phonemes), syllables and whole words. The child can tell when words rhyme and can say rhyming words. With words said aloud, the child counts the syllables, says them, joins them into the word and breaks the word back into them; for a spoken word of one syllable, the child does the same joining and breaking with its onset and rime. In a word of three phonemes with the pattern consonant, vowel, consonant (CVC), the child picks out and says the first sound, the vowel in the middle and the last sound, except in CVC words that end in /l/, /r/ or /x/. The child makes a new word from a simple one-syllable word by putting in an extra sound or swapping one sound for another, and joins two or three phonemes into a word that can be recognised.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child claps once for each syllable of a classmate's name and says how many claps there were. Hearing /k/ and then "at", the child says "cat"; given "sun", the child says /s/ and then "un" (the onset is what comes before the vowel, the rime is the vowel and what follows). With three counters in a row for "map", the child touches the first, middle or last counter and says that sound alone. The child changes "map" to "mop" or "cap" by saying a different sound in one place, and says whether "map" and "tap" rhyme.

### Limits

Everything here is done with spoken words; the statement does not call for letters or print. Part b sets no limit on the length of the words whose syllables are counted, while onset and rime work (part c) is limited to one-syllable words. Part d is limited to words of three phonemes in the pattern consonant, vowel, consonant, and it leaves out words ending in /l/, /r/ or /x/; the vowel is the only middle sound named. A footnote says that slashes mark pronunciation, so a CVC word is counted by its three sounds whatever number of letters spells it. Part e covers putting a sound in or swapping one, within simple words of one syllable, and does not mention taking a sound away. California adds part f: blending, limited to two or three phonemes. The adopted document notes above the table that, for kindergarten, the expectation in the areas the table lists is that a child shows awareness and competence that are increasing.

### Common mistakes

Giving a word that starts with the same sound when asked for a rhyme ("cat, cup"). Saying a consonant with an extra vowel ("buh"), so that the blended sounds do not come out as the word. Finding the first sound but not the middle vowel, which is the hardest of the three positions to hear.
