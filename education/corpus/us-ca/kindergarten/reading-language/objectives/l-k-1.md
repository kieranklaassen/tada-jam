## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

In speech and in writing, the child follows the grammar and usage of standard English. The child can form a good number of the letters in print, capitals as well as small letters, and uses verbs and nouns that come up often. Speaking aloud, the child makes regular plurals with an added /s/ or /es/ (for example wishes from wish, dogs from dog). The child understands and uses the words that open a question, such as how, why, when, where, what and who, and the commonest prepositions, such as with, by, of, for, off, on, out, in, from and to. When language activities are done together with others, the child forms whole sentences and makes them fuller.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child writes the letters of their own name and other letters they know with a crayon or pencil, some as capitals and some small. Talking about a picture, the child says "two foxes" and "three cats", asks "Where is the ball?", and says the cup is on the table or in the box. When an adult says "The dog ran", the child adds to it: "The big dog ran to the gate."

### Limits

Part a asks for many letters, not all of them. Part c covers regular plurals only, made in speech; irregular plurals are not mentioned. The question words listed are given as examples, and the prepositions listed as examples of the most common ones; neither is a closed list. Part f is set in language activities done together, so the child is not asked to build and lengthen sentences alone. The statement applies to speaking as well as writing and sets no number of words or sentences.

### Common mistakes

Adding the regular ending to an irregular word ("foots", "mouses"). Writing letters reversed, such as b for d. Answering a "why" or "when" question as if it asked "what", and mixing up pairs of prepositions such as in and on.
