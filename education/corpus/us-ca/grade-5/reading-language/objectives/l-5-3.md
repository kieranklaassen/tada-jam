## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

Whether listening, reading, speaking or writing, the child applies what they know about how language works and about its conventions. The child makes sentences longer, joins them or cuts them down so that the meaning, the style and the interest of a reader or listener are served. The child sets side by side the kinds of English, such as registers and dialects, that appear in stories, plays or poems, and says how they are alike and how they differ.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child takes two short sentences from a draft and joins them with "because", then shortens a sentence that says the same thing twice. The child reads a story in which one character speaks in a regional dialect and another speaks formally. The child says how the two ways of talking differ and what each shows about the speaker.

### Limits

Three sentence changes are named (expanding, combining, reducing) and three reasons for them (meaning, interest, style). Comparing kinds of English is limited to literature: stories, dramas and poems. Dialects and registers are examples, not a full list, and no particular dialect is named. The statement sets no sentence length.

### Common mistakes

Children combine sentences with a string of "and" or "then" and produce a run-on sentence. They treat a dialect in a story as wrong English instead of a variety with its own rules. They shorten a sentence by cutting a detail that the meaning needs.
