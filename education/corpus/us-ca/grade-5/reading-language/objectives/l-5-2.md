## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child shows control of standard English capitalisation, punctuation and spelling in writing. The child puts punctuation between the members of a list inside a sentence and a comma after an introductory element. A comma also sets the words yes and no apart (as in "No, thank you"), stands between a tag question and the sentence it follows ("You saw it, didn't you?"), and shows that a person is addressed directly ("Steve, is that you?"). The child shows that something is the title of a work by underlining, italics or quotation marks. The child spells words suited to the grade correctly and looks them up in a reference when that is needed.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child writes a book report and underlines the book's title by hand, or sets it in italics when typing, and puts a poem's title in quotation marks. The child proofreads a draft, adds the missing comma after "After lunch" at the start of a sentence, and checks the spelling of "necessary" in a dictionary. The child writes a line of dialogue with a name in it and places the comma next to the name.

### Limits

The statement covers writing only. Punctuation is asked for in named cases only: some punctuation between the members of a list, and a comma for introductory elements, for yes and no, for tag questions and for direct address. The statement gives an example for each of the last three comma uses; the quoted examples in the Summary are the pack's own, of the same uses, not the official ones. Three ways of marking a title are named; the statement does not say which kind of work takes which mark. Spelling is bounded by words suited to the grade, with reference works allowed; no word list is given. A footnote says that, from grade 3 on, an asterisk marks a skill that will probably need more work in later grades as writing and speaking become more demanding; in this record the asterisk stands on punctuating a list.

### Common mistakes

Children leave out the comma after a long opening phrase, or put a comma after every short opening word. They forget the comma next to a name in direct address, which changes the meaning ("Let's eat Grandma"). They accept a spell checker's result for words that sound alike ("their" and "there") and do not look the word up.
