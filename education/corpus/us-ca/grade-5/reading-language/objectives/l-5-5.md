## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child shows that they understand three things: how words relate to one another, small differences between word meanings, and figurative language. The child reads similes, metaphors and other figurative language where it occurs in a text and says what is meant. The child knows common idioms, adages and proverbs when they meet them and can say what each means. Where particular words are related, as homographs, antonyms or synonyms are, the child uses the relationship to understand each of those words better.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child reads "the classroom was a zoo" in a story and says in plain words what the writer means. The child explains what "a stitch in time saves nine" advises, with an example from their own life. The child puts "cool", "cold" and "freezing" in order of strength, and reads "wind" aloud in two ways to show a homograph.

### Limits

Similes and metaphors are the two kinds of figurative language named; the word "including" does not exclude others. Idioms, adages and proverbs are limited to common ones, and no list is given. Synonyms, antonyms and homographs are examples of word relationships. Figurative language is interpreted in context, not as isolated phrases.

### Common mistakes

Children read an idiom or a metaphor literally ("it's raining cats and dogs" as animals falling). They can label a simile by spotting "like" or "as" but cannot say what is being compared or why. They confuse homographs with homophones.
