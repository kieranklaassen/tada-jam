## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

Given two rules, the child builds a number pattern from each and looks for how the terms in matching positions relate. The child pairs up matching terms as ordered pairs and plots them on a coordinate plane. As an example, starting both patterns at 0 with the rules 'add 6' and 'add 3', the child writes out the terms, notices that each term of one pattern is double the matching term of the other, and explains in an informal way why.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child writes two rows of numbers one under the other, one growing by 3 and one by 6, and draws lines between the terms that share a position. The child says 'the bottom one is always twice the top one, because each step adds twice as much and both begin at 0'. The child writes the pairs (3, 6), (6, 12), (9, 18), marks them on squared paper, and sees that they fall on a straight line.

### Limits

There are two patterns, each from a given rule, and the explanation is informal. The statement sets no number range and names no quadrant; its example starts at 0 and adds, so every term is zero or greater, and a geometry statement at this grade keeps plotted points to the first quadrant. The rules in the example add a fixed number; the statement does not say which kinds of rule are allowed.

### Common mistakes

Describing each pattern only on its own ('this one goes up by 3') and not comparing the matching terms. Pairing terms from different positions, for example the second term of one pattern with the third of the other. Swapping the order inside the pairs when plotting, so that the point for (3, 6) is marked at (6, 3).
