## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

Staying inside one system of measurement, the child changes a measurement from one standard unit to a larger or smaller one; for example, 0.05 m is what 5 cm becomes. The child uses such changes of unit when solving problems from real situations that take more than one step.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child measures a table in centimetres with a tape, then writes the same length in metres and in millimetres. Pouring water between a litre jug and a 250-millilitre cup, the child works out how many cups a 1.5-litre bottle fills. For a problem such as fencing a garden bed measured partly in feet and partly in inches, the child first brings all lengths to one unit and then adds.

### Limits

Conversions stay within one measurement system: metric to metric, or customary to customary. The statement does not ask for conversion between the two systems. The cluster heading speaks of like units, that is, units for the same kind of quantity, such as one unit of length changed to another unit of length. Neither the statement nor the heading lists the units; the one example goes from a smaller unit to a larger one and has a decimal answer. The problems take several steps and come from real situations.

### Common mistakes

Multiplying when going to a larger unit (writing 5 cm as 500 m) because 'converting means multiplying'. Moving the decimal point the wrong number of places, for example treating centimetres to metres as a shift of one place. Using 10 or 100 for customary units, such as 10 inches in a foot or 10 ounces in a pound.
