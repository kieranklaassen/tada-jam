## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child notices how strong a feeling is, not only which feeling it is, and has names for a growing range of more complicated feelings. The child can tell when what they are feeling is stress or anxiety.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of about nine marks on a scale from one to five how strong their anger was at recess and how strong it is now. The child chooses between close words such as annoyed, frustrated and furious, or says that they feel disappointed and relieved at once. Before a test the child notices a tight stomach and fast breathing and says "I am nervous about this." The document names no ages for this band; the child described here is in the later primary years, about 8 to 11.

### Limits

This statement belongs to the Late Elementary band, which the document ties to no grades. The statement is about noticing and naming; what a child does about a feeling is the subject of the Self-Management statements of this band (2.A.2, 2.B.2). Apart from stress and anxiety it lists no emotion words, and it sets no number of words a child should have. This is voluntary guidance, not an adopted standard.

### Common mistakes

Children use one word (mad, sad, fine) for feelings of very different strength. Many take stress felt in the body for illness (a stomach ache, a headache) and do not link it to worry. Mixed feelings are often reported as only the stronger one.
