## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

On their own initiative, the student in the Middle School band uses restorative approaches; restorative questions asked in a conflict are named as one of them. More and more, the student accepts that they are answerable for harm that may come from what they do, and looks for chances to mend the relationship. The student also practises forgiving.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A young person in the Middle School band, about 11 to 14 years old (the document itself names no ages), asks the other person and themselves after a quarrel: what happened, who was affected and how, and what would repair it. They say plainly what they did, without "but", and offer something that repairs it, such as replacing a broken item or correcting a rumour. When someone apologises to them, they say whether they accept it and stop raising the matter.

### Limits

This is voluntary guidance, not an adopted standard. Restorative questions are named as one restorative approach; the statement does not print the questions. Taking responsibility is described as increasing, and forgiveness as something practised. The use of restorative approaches is described as proactive, meaning without waiting to be told. Conflicts are named together with the questions; the wording does not settle whether the other restorative approaches are tied to conflicts too.

### Common mistakes

Young people often give an apology that shifts the blame ("sorry if you were upset") or say sorry and consider the matter closed without repairing anything. Many answer the restorative questions with what the other person did. Forgiveness is commonly confused with saying the harm did not matter or with having to be friends again.
