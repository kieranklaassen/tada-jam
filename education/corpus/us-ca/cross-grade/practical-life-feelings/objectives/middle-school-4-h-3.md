## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

In the Middle School band the student looks at what a microaggression is and thinks it over. The student understands what the bystander effect is and what being an ally means. The student knows that being an ally is not done once: it is a process that continues and that includes repeatedly looking at oneself.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A young person in the Middle School band, about 11 to 14 years old (the document itself names no ages), discusses small everyday remarks, such as "Where are you really from?" or praise for "speaking so well", and says how they are received by a person who hears them often. They describe a time when several people saw someone treated badly and nobody acted, and say why each person waited. They practise what an ally can do: speak to the person targeted, say "that's not okay", or fetch an adult, and afterwards ask themselves what they missed.

### Limits

This is voluntary guidance, not an adopted standard. The statement asks for understanding and reflection on three ideas: microaggression, allyship and the bystander effect; it defines none of them. It does not say the student must act as an ally; it says they understand allyship as something that continues and that involves reflecting on themselves.

### Common mistakes

Young people often judge a remark only by whether it was meant kindly and dismiss its effect when it is repeated many times. In a group, most assume someone else will act. Some think calling themselves an ally, or acting once, is enough.
