## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

In the Middle School band the student looks closely at what they believe about themselves and what they believe about learning, and at how the two sets of beliefs meet. The brackets name the contrast between aiming to master something and aiming to perform well. The student thinks about what these beliefs do to their confidence and their hopefulness.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A young person in the Middle School band, about 11 to 14 years old (the document itself names no ages), sorts their own recent statements about schoolwork into two piles: ones about getting better at something ("I want to understand fractions") and ones about looking good or avoiding looking bad ("I need an A", "I don't want to seem stupid"). They recall a task they avoided and say which belief was behind it. They describe how they felt about the next attempt after a failure in a subject they call themselves "bad at".

### Limits

This is voluntary guidance, not an adopted standard. The statement asks for examination and reflection; it does not say the student must hold a mastery orientation. The two orientations are named in brackets without definition. It names two effects to consider, on optimism and on confidence, and no others.

### Common mistakes

Many young people believe ability in a subject is fixed ("I am not a maths person") and treat that as a fact, not a belief. They often read effort as a sign of low ability. Some repeat growth-mindset phrases they have been taught without connecting them to a real task they gave up on.
