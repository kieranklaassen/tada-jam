## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child draws on ways that fit them personally to become motivated and to stay so; talking to oneself is the example given. The child can spot what takes their attention away and knows easy ways to deal with it, which the child actually uses.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of about nine tells themselves "one more page, then a break" while reading, or "I did this last time, I can do it again" before a hard sum. The child notices that the window seat or a talkative neighbour takes their attention, and moves seats, turns the toy face down, or puts on ear defenders. The document names no ages for this band; the child described here is in the later primary years, about 8 to 11.

### Limits

This statement belongs to the Late Elementary band, which the document ties to no grades. Self-talk is given as an example of a strategy, not as the required one. The solutions to distraction are described as simple. Both getting motivated and staying motivated are named. This is voluntary guidance, not an adopted standard.

### Common mistakes

Children's self-talk is often negative ("I'm so bad at this"), which lowers effort. Many blame the distraction ("he keeps talking") and do not see a step they could take themselves. Children overrate how well they can work with a distraction present.
