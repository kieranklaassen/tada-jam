## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student uses known mathematics to solve problems that come from daily life, society and work. This means picking out the quantities that matter, simplifying a complicated situation with assumptions and approximations that may be revised later, showing relationships with tools such as diagrams, tables, graphs, flowcharts and formulas, drawing conclusions, and checking the results against the situation and improving the model if it proves inadequate. The text's examples by age: an addition equation written for a situation in the early grades, a school event planned with proportional reasoning in the middle grades, and, in high school, geometry used for a design problem or a function that tells how one quantity depends on another.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child sees three children at a table and two more arriving, writes 3 + 2 = 5 to find how many cups to set out, and checks by handing out the cups. An older child plans drinks for a class party: decides that each person drinks about two cups and that one bottle fills about eight, makes a table, works out the bottles for 28 people, and changes the plan on realising that some will want more.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content. What the text says about early grades, middle grades and high school is given as examples.

### Common mistakes

Writing a number sentence that uses the numbers in the order they are heard and does not match the situation. Reporting a result the situation cannot have, such as three and a half bottles to buy, without adjusting it. Making an assumption without noticing it or saying it.
