## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student considers which tools are available for a problem and chooses among them with judgement. The tools named range from pencil and paper, concrete models, ruler and protractor to calculator, spreadsheet and mathematical software. The student knows the tools of the grade well enough to tell when each helps and what it cannot do. The text's one worked example is about high school students: they study graphs of functions and solutions that a graphing calculator produces, catch likely errors by estimating and by other mathematical knowledge, and know that in modelling, technology lets them see what follows when assumptions are changed, explore consequences and set predictions against data. Mathematical resources from outside that are relevant, digital material found on a website being one example, are something students across a range of grade levels can pick out and then use for setting problems or solving them, and they use technology to explore ideas and understand them more deeply.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child who wants to know which pile of cubes has more chooses to line the two piles up side by side, and picks up a train of cubes to measure a book and does not guess. An older child estimates 198 times 5 mentally without a calculator, takes a protractor for an angle and grid paper for an area, and on a long sum with a calculator estimates first and so notices a wrongly pressed key.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content. It does not list which tools belong to which grade. Its one worked example is about high school students.

### Common mistakes

Using one tool for everything, such as always counting on fingers or always taking the calculator. Accepting whatever a tool displays without an estimate to compare it with. Using a tool wrongly, such as starting a ruler at the 1 mark.
