## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student builds an argument for a mathematical claim from stated assumptions, definitions and results already established, makes conjectures and tests them in logical steps, splits a situation into cases, uses counterexamples, reasons from data with their context in mind, and explains and defends conclusions. The student also follows other people's arguments by listening or reading, decides whether they hold, compares two arguments, says what is wrong in a faulty one, and asks questions that make an argument clearer or better. The text says elementary students can argue with objects, drawings, diagrams and actions, and that such arguments can be correct before they are made general or formal in later grades.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child claims that seven is odd and shows it by pairing seven cubes and pointing to the one left without a partner. An older child claims that two odd numbers always add to an even number and explains it with a drawing of pairs and two leftover ones that join; when a classmate says "multiplying always makes a number bigger", the older child answers with six times one half.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content. The text says that making arguments general or formal comes in later grades, and that judging where an argument applies is learned later. Directly after this practice the adopted California document adds one further practice of its own, marked for higher mathematics only; the pack does not record it.

### Common mistakes

Repeating the answer, or saying "because I know", in place of a reason. Treating a few examples that work as proof for every case. Judging an argument by who said it, or by whether its answer matches one's own, and not by its reasoning.
