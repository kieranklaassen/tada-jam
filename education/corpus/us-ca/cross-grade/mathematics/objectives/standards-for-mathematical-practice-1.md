## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student makes sense of a problem and keeps working at solving it. Before attempting an answer, the student begins by making clear in their own mind what the problem means, looks for a way in, studies what is given, what is wanted, which conditions hold and how these relate, forms a guess about what kind of answer to expect and what it will mean, and plans a route to it. While working, the student thinks of similar problems, tries special cases and simpler versions to learn more about how the problem can be solved, keeps judging how the work is going, and takes another route when needed. A proficient student can explain how tables, graphs, equations and descriptions in words correspond, or draws a diagram of what matters and how it relates, plots data, and looks for regularities or trends. Answers are checked by a second method, and the student keeps asking, again in their own mind, whether it makes sense. The student can also follow how other people go about solving hard problems and see where different ways of solving correspond. The text gives two examples by age: younger students might lean on objects or pictures to grasp and solve a problem, and older students might, where the problem calls for it, rework algebraic expressions or change the window of a graphing calculator to find out what they need to know.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child, given a story about sharing eight crackers between two plates, retells the story, sets out eight counters, deals them onto the plates, and starts again another way when the plates come out uneven. An older child, given a problem about fencing a garden, says what is known and what is wanted, tries a smaller garden first, draws a diagram, and checks the result with a second calculation.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content, number range or topic. The text gives only examples of what younger and older students might do.

### Common mistakes

Starting to calculate with the numbers in the problem before knowing what is asked. Stopping when the first attempt fails, or accepting the first answer without checking it. Believing that a problem which cannot be answered at once cannot be solved at all.
