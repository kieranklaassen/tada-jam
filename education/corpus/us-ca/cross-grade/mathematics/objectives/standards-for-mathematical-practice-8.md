## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student notices when a calculation or a line of reasoning keeps recurring and looks for a general method or a shortcut. The text's examples: upper elementary students who divide 25 by 11 may see the same steps return and conclude that the decimal repeats; middle school students may arrive at the equation of a line, or at a general formula, after doing similar calculations several times. While working, the student keeps track of the whole process, minds the details, and keeps judging whether the results reached so far are reasonable.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child adds 9 to several numbers with counters, sees each time that it is ten more and one back, and starts doing that without the counters. An older child divides 1 by 3 on paper, sees the same remainder return and says the threes continue without end; after finding the perimeter of several rectangles the child states a rule: twice the length and twice the width.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content. The youngest students in its examples are in the upper elementary grades; it gives no example for young children.

### Common mistakes

Carrying out a long procedure many times without noticing that the steps repeat. Stating a rule after one or two cases without testing another. Using a shortcut such as "add a zero to multiply by ten" where it no longer works, as with decimals.
