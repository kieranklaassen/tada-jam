## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The student looks closely for a pattern or a structure and puts it to use. The text's examples: young students may see that seven and three more equals three and seven more, or sort shapes by their number of sides; later, students see 7 × 8 as 7 × 5 plus 7 × 3, which prepares for the distributive property; older students read the numbers in an algebraic expression as a sum and a product of two simpler numbers, or add a helping line to a geometric figure. They can also take a wider view, change viewpoint, and treat something complicated as one object or as made of several.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A younger child shows with cubes that three and seven fill a ten-frame just as seven and three do, sorts blocks into three-sided and four-sided ones, and notices that every number in one column of a hundred chart ends in the same digit. An older child works out 6 × 14 as 6 × 10 and 6 × 4, and finds the area of an L-shaped room by drawing one line that splits it into two rectangles.

### Limits

This practice holds for every grade from kindergarten up and sets no grade-specific content. The text ties its examples to rough ages (young, later, older) and gives them as examples.

### Common mistakes

Working every item out from the beginning without noticing that it resembles the one before. Seeing a pattern in a few cases and applying it where it does not hold. Reading an expression only as a list of steps to carry out, not as a thing that has parts.
