## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

Faced with a word or phrase that is unfamiliar or has more than one meaning, the child works out or checks what it means. Three ways are named, each used where it suits: looking it up in general or specialised reference materials, breaking the word into its meaningful parts, and reading the clues in the surrounding text.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A young child (about 5 to 7) hears "enormous" in a story, looks at the picture of the elephant and the words around it, and says it means very big; the child sees "un" at the start of "unhappy" and says it means not happy. An older child (about 9 to 12) meets "bank" in a passage about a river and picks the meaning that fits, uses the parts of "transport" to guess at "transatlantic", and confirms a science term in a glossary.

### Limits

This is an anchor standard that holds from kindergarten to grade 12: it sets no grade-specific content, and each grade's own standards give the detail. Three strategies are named: context clues, meaningful word parts, reference materials (general and specialised). They are to be used as appropriate, not all every time. No word list and no list of word parts is given.

### Common mistakes

Young children guess a word from its first letter or from the picture alone. Older children take the first meaning listed in a dictionary without testing it in the sentence. They trust a familiar-looking word part that misleads ("invaluable" read as "not valuable").
