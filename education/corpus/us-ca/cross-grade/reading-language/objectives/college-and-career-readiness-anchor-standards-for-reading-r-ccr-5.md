## Summary

_The pack's summary, not the official text._

The child examines how texts are built. This includes how particular sentences, paragraphs and larger parts connect with one another and with the text as a whole; a stanza, scene, chapter or section is given as an example of a larger part.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A young child (about 5 to 7) points to the title, the front cover and the last page of a book, and says which part of a story comes first, next and last. An older child (about 9 to 12) explains why a chapter that looks back in time is placed where it is, says what the opening paragraph of an article prepares the reader for, and shows how the last stanza of a poem answers the first.

### Limits

This is an anchor standard that holds from kindergarten to grade 12: it sets no grade-specific content, and each grade's own standards give the detail. Units of three sizes are named: sentences, paragraphs and larger portions. Four larger portions are given as examples (section, chapter, scene, stanza). No kind of text is named.

### Common mistakes

Young children mix up the order of events when retelling. Older children describe what a section says and do not say what it does for the whole text. They name a structure ("it has chapters") without explaining how the parts connect.
