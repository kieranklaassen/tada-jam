## Official wording

vertellen voor veelal bekend publiek: - een zelfbedacht verhaal vertellen; - een boekbespreking doen; - iets beschrijven of uitleggen; - een voorbereid praatje/spreekbeurt houden (bijv. over een sport/hobby); - verslag uitbrengen over een gebeurtenis of activiteit

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

telling to a mostly familiar audience: - telling a story one has made up oneself; - giving a book talk (*boekbespreking*, presenting a book one has read); - describing or explaining something; - giving a prepared short talk/class presentation (*praatje/spreekbeurt*) (e.g. about a sport/hobby); - reporting on an event or activity

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child stands in front of the class with a book they have read, shows the cover, tells what it is about without telling the ending and says what they thought of it. On another day the child gives a prepared talk about judo with the judo suit at hand, or tells a story they have made up. After the school trip the child reports to another class what was done and seen. A child of 7 tells the class about an object they hold in their hands; a child of 10 gives a prepared talk about their hobby.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The audience is "mostly familiar" (*veelal bekend*), so it is not always one the child knows. Five kinds of telling are listed: telling a self-made story, giving a book talk, describing or explaining something, giving a prepared short talk or class presentation, and reporting on an event or activity; a sport or hobby follows "e.g." and is an example of a subject for the prepared talk. The statement sets no length and no size of audience, and does not mention help. The open data lists it under sub-cluster NE/MT/03/01, titled *Een monoloog houden* (holding a monologue).

### Common mistakes

Assuming that the listeners know what the child knows: saying "he" and "there" without saying who and where. In a book talk, retelling the whole book event by event and not saying what the child thought of it. Reading a written-out text aloud word for word without looking at the audience.
