## Official wording

een formele bewerking geven bij een optel- of aftreksituatie tot ten minste 1000 en omgekeerd: bij een formele bewerking onder 1000 passende optel- of aftrek situaties bedenken

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

giving a formal operation (*formele bewerking*, the calculation written in symbols) for an adding or subtracting situation up to at least 1000 and the reverse: thinking up fitting adding or subtracting situations for a formal operation below 1000

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child hears a situation, such as a bus with 48 people from which 19 get off, and writes the formal operation that fits it, 48 − 19. The other way round, the child gets a written operation such as 350 + 125 and makes up a story that fits it, and tells or draws the story. The child checks whether the story of another child fits the operation. A child of 7 acts a situation out with objects before writing the operation; a child of 10 makes up a story about a difference between two amounts for a written subtraction.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. Two ranges are stated, one for each direction. The adding or subtracting situations go "up to at least 1000" (*tot ten minste 1000*): no upper bound is set, and the Dutch *tot* leaves open whether 1000 itself is included. The formal operations for which situations are thought up are "below 1000" (*onder 1000*), without "at least"; read strictly, *onder* (below) leaves 1000 itself out. The wording does not say whether these bounds hold for the numbers in the operation, for the outcome, or for both. The goal links a situation and an operation in both directions; the wording does not mention working out the outcome.

### Common mistakes

The child makes up a story that uses the right numbers but the wrong operation, such as an adding story for 48 − 19. The child's subtraction stories are always about taking away, never about comparing two amounts or filling up to an amount. The child writes a "how many more" situation as an addition because the word "more" is in it.
