## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

exploring the standard measure 'metre' and the measures for length derived from it, discovering the relations between them and converting (*herleiden*) the length measures: 1 m = 10 dm, 1 dm = 10 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 m = 1000 mm, 1 km = 1000 m

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child lays ten strips of a decimetre along a metre rule and ten centimetre cubes along one strip, and counts the ten millimetre marks in one centimetre on a ruler. The child then writes a length in another unit, for example 3 m as 30 dm, 4 cm as 40 mm and 2 km as 2000 m. A child of 7 finds the relations by laying and counting; a child of 10 converts between the named units in both directions without the material.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names the metre as the standard measure and prints five relations: 1 m = 10 dm, 1 dm = 10 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 m = 1000 mm and 1 km = 1000 m. The units in these relations are the kilometre, metre, decimetre, centimetre and millimetre. The relation 1 m = 100 cm is not among the five, although it follows from the first two. The decametre and the hectometre are not in the printed relations. All five relations are in whole numbers; the goal's own wording shows no decimal numbers and states no number range for the conversions. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: 2 m 40 and 240 centimetres are equal to 2 metres and 40 centimetres; it is an example, not a limit. The example rests on 1 m = 100 cm and writes the length as "2 m 40", without a decimal comma.

### Common mistakes

The child multiplies where it has to divide, or the reverse, so that a length gets a smaller number in a smaller unit. The child uses the same step for every pair of units, for example takes 1 km for 100 m or 1 cm for 100 mm. The child skips the decimetre, which is little used in daily life, and takes 1 m for 10 cm.
