## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

knowing the standard measures kilogram and gram and knowing that 1 kg = 1000 grams;

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child holds a pack of one kilogram and a thing of about one gram and says which of the two units suits a thing that is to be weighed. The child says and writes that 1 kilogram is 1000 grams and uses this, for example to say that half a kilogram is 500 grams. A child of 7 knows both words and knows which is the heavier unit; a child of 10 uses the relation in both directions, from kilograms to grams and from grams to kilograms.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names two units, kilogram and gram, and one relation: 1 kg = 1000 gram. It names no other unit of weight: not the milligram or the tonne, and not the everyday Dutch words *pond* (500 grams) and *ons* (100 grams). This goal states no number range beyond the relation. The source prints the word *en* (and) once too often ("kilogram en gram en kennen") and ends with a semicolon; the gloss leaves out the extra word and keeps the semicolon.

### Common mistakes

The child takes 1 kilogram to be 100 grams, by analogy with 1 metre and 100 centimetres. The child thinks that 1000 grams is more than 1 kilogram because the number is larger. The child uses a unit that does not suit the thing, for example gives the weight of a person in grams.
