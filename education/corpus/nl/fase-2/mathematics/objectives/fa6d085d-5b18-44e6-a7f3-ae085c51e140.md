## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

multiplying with insight below at least 1000 in context situations and formal mathematical language according to the splitting or distributing strategy (*splits- of verdeelstrategie*, splitting one number and multiplying each part) (e.g. 8 x 36 = 8 x 30 + 8 x 6). The pupil explains how he/she arrives at a solution.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child works out 6 × 34 by splitting 34 into 30 and 4: 6 × 30 = 180, 6 × 4 = 24, together 204. In a situation, such as 4 boxes with 125 screws each, the child splits 125 into 100, 20 and 5 and joins 400, 80 and 20 into 500. The child tells how the outcome was found and why the part outcomes are added, and shows it with a rectangle of squares cut into two parts when that helps. A child of 7 lays a multiplication as a rectangle of squares, cuts the rectangle apart and adds the outcomes of the parts; a child of 10 writes the split as sums and explains why the outcomes of the parts are added.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The range is stated as "below at least 1000" (*onder ten minste 1000*): numbers below 1000 are the least that is offered, and no upper bound is set; read strictly, *onder* (below) leaves the bound itself out. The wording does not say whether the bound holds for the numbers in the calculation, for the outcome, or for both. The work is in context situations and in formal mathematical language. One strategy is named, the splitting or distributing strategy, used "with insight" (*met inzicht*). The statement prints one example in brackets, after "bijv.": 8 x 36 = 8 x 30 + 8 x 6, a one-digit number times a two-digit number, with the two-digit number split into its tens and ones; it is an example, not a limit. A second sentence is about the pupil (*de leerling*) and says that the pupil explains how he or she arrives at a solution; it is worded as something the pupil does, and the statement as a whole stays guidance about what a school offers. The wording does not say how many digits the numbers have, and names no written algorithm for multiplying. The open data stops before the example; the record holds the whole statement as the content-line document prints it.

### Common mistakes

The child multiplies only one of the parts: gives 6 × 34 as 6 × 30 + 4 = 184. The child loses the zero in the tens part and gives 6 × 30 as 18. Asked how the outcome was found, the child gives the outcome again or says "I just knew it".
