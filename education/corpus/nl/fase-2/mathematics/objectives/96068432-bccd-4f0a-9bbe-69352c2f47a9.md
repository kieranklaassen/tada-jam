## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

weighing persons, objects and quantities with different digital and analogue scales (scales for persons (*personen weegschaal*, a bathroom scale), kitchen scales) and weighing out quantities to the nearest gram

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child stands on a bathroom scale and reads its own weight, and puts an object on kitchen scales and reads the number on the display or the place of the pointer between the marks. The child weighs out a quantity, for example flour for a recipe, by adding a little and taking a little away until the scale shows exactly the number of grams asked for. A child of 7 weighs its school bag on a bathroom scale and reads the weight; a child of 10 weighs a bag of apples on kitchen scales and reads the weight.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names what is weighed: persons, objects and quantities. It names different digital and analogue scales, and the brackets name the scale for persons and the kitchen scale. The precision "to the nearest gram" is attached to weighing out quantities; the goal states no precision for weighing persons and objects. The gram is the only unit this goal names; `rw/m/4/05/fase2` names kilogram and gram. This goal states no number range.

### Common mistakes

On an analogue scale the child reads the nearest numbered mark and ignores the small marks between, or counts each small mark as one gram without checking what it stands for. The child includes the weight of the bowl, because it does not set the scale to zero first. The child reads grams for kilograms or the reverse, or leaves out the unit.
