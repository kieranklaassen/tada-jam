## English gloss

thinking critically and reasoning about length and perimeter in simple problem situations

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child thinks about a question on length or perimeter and gives reasons: whether a ribbon is long enough to go round a box, whether two different routes are equally long, how much fence a garden needs. The child says beforehand what it expects and checks by measuring or by laying a string along the edge. A child of 7 compares two lengths by laying them side by side; a child of 10 reasons with metres and centimetres and explains that two figures made of the same number of squares can have different perimeters.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The problem situations are "simple", and they are about length and perimeter. The goal's own wording names no situations, no unit and no measuring instrument, and states no number range. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: "A rectangle has a perimeter of 24 cm. How long can the length and the width then be? Think up several ways."; it is an example, not a limit, and it uses the centimetre and the number 24.

### Common mistakes

The child thinks that a figure that looks bigger always has the longer perimeter. The child compares a bent line and a straight line by where their ends lie, and says that the straight line is longer. The child compares two numbers that have different units, for example says that 90 cm is more than 1 m.
