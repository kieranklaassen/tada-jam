## English gloss

investigating how day and night come about (position of the sun during the day and the course of the shadow)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child stands a stick upright in the playground and marks the tip of its shadow with chalk at several times in the school day, then compares the length and direction of the marks. The child notes where the sun stands each time, without looking straight at it. With a ball and a lamp the child turns the ball and sees one half lit and one half dark. A child of 7 marks and compares the shadows; a child of 10 can link the shadow record to the turning of the earth.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The brackets name two things to look at: where the sun stands during the day and how the shadow changes. The statement names no particular times of day, does not mention seasons or time zones, and names no instrument.

### Common mistakes

Believing that night comes because the sun goes out, goes behind hills or clouds, or is covered by the moon. Expecting the longest shadow at midday, when the sun is highest. Expecting the shadow of a fixed object to point the same way all day.
