## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child sorts toys, colours, jobs and household tasks from a catalogue or picture set into "for boys", "for girls" and "for everyone", and then looks at who in the group does or likes each. The child looks in picture books, advertisements and shop shelves at what boys and girls are shown doing, and asks a grandparent what was expected of boys and girls when he or she was young. A child of 7 notices that "for boys" and "for girls" do not fit what the children in the group like; a child of 10 says where such expectations come from, such as home, friends, media and shops, and that they change over time.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 2 (*groep* 4 to 6, about ages 7 to 10), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The wording ends with the printed marker (b), the curriculum institute's sign for an aspect of citizenship (*burgerschap*). The verb is seeing in the sense of understanding (*inzien*), and its object is how gender roles arise and how they are learned. The Dutch *sekserollen* is literally "sex roles": the behaviour expected of someone as a boy or a girl, a man or a woman. The statement names no roles and nothing from which they are learned, and does not say whether the roles are good or bad.

### Common mistakes

Children think that what boys and girls usually do is fixed by the body and cannot be otherwise. They keep to a rule ("boys do not cry", "pink is for girls") and correct or tease a child who does not follow it. They think they chose every preference themselves and that nobody influenced them.
