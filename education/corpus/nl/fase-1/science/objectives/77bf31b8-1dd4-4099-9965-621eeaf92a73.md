## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

realising that physical phenomena offer a lot, but can also be dangerous

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child tells what light, heat, electricity, water and sound give: a lamp to see by, a heater to get warm, water to wash with, music to listen to. The child also tells where the same thing can hurt: a hot pan or radiator, a candle flame, a socket, deep water, a very loud noise, looking into the sun. The child points out in the classroom or at home what may be touched and what may not, and says what to do: keep a distance, ask an adult. A child of 4 knows a few fixed rules such as "hot, do not touch"; a child of 7 can give for one phenomenon both a use and a danger, and the reason for the rule.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1 (*groep* 1 to 3, about ages 4 to 7), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The wording states two sides of physical phenomena: that they offer a lot, and that they can also be dangerous. It names no phenomenon and no danger, and it asks for a realisation, not for handling a risk. It states no safety condition and no rule of conduct. A neighbouring statement, `ojw/nattech/2/02/fase1`, names light, sound, temperature, force and electricity as its own subjects; this statement does not say that it means the same five.

### Common mistakes

Seeing a thing as only good or only dangerous, not both: fire is bad, water is safe. Judging danger by what can be seen, so that a hot plate that does not glow, a socket or a very loud sound is taken as harmless. Being able to repeat a rule without knowing the reason, and not applying it to a new case.
