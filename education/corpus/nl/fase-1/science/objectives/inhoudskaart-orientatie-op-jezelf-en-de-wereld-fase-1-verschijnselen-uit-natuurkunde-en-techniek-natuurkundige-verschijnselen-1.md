## Official wording

verkennen en benoemen van dingen en verschijnselen in de natuurkunde met karakteristieke eigenschappen als resultaat van: ik zie, ik hoor, ik voel, ik ruik, ik proef

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Oriëntatie op jezelf en de wereld - kleuters (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

exploring and naming things and phenomena in physics with characteristic properties as a result of: I see, I hear, I feel, I smell, I taste

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child holds an ice cube and says "I feel that it is cold" and "I see that it gets smaller", strikes a triangle and says "I hear it ring", and shines a torch on the wall and says "I see light". The child smells and tastes only what an adult offers, such as sugar and salt that look the same and taste different. The child learns the word for what was noticed: cold, warm, loud, soft, light, dark. A child of 4 says what is seen or felt in one or two words; a child of 6 uses the sentence "I see", "I hear" or "I feel" and names the thing with its property.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1, for the youngest children (*groep* 1 and 2, about ages 4 to 6), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The wording ties the exploring and naming to five kinds of observation, written from the child's side: I see, I hear, I feel, I smell, I taste. What is named are things and phenomena in physics with their characteristic properties; the wording gives no example of a thing, a phenomenon or a property. It does not mention measuring or instruments, and it states no condition for smelling or tasting.

### Common mistakes

Saying what a thing is or what it is for instead of what is seen, heard or felt ("it is a bell" instead of "I hear a high sound"). Mixing what is observed with what is thought, or naming the wrong sense ("I see that it is cold"). Using the eyes only and leaving out the other senses.
