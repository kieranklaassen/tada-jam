## Official wording

bewust worden van verschillen tussen gesprekspartners en van omgangstaal (zoals reacties van anderen en non-verbale reacties van anderen én van gebaren en mimiek om gespreksbijdragen te ondersteunen)

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Nederlandse taal: ontluikende en beginnende geletterdheid - jonge kind (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

becoming aware of differences between conversation partners and of everyday language (*omgangstaal*, the language people use with each other) (such as reactions of others and non-verbal reactions of others, and also of gestures and facial expression to support contributions to a conversation)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child notices that a friend laughs at a joke that the teacher answers with a question, and that a classmate frowns or turns away when they do not understand. The child hears that the teacher speaks to a younger child in another way than to the head teacher, and sees a classmate show with the hands how big the fish was while telling about it. A child of 4 reacts to a clear signal such as crying or laughing; a child of 6 says "she looks like she doesn't get it" when a classmate frowns.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1, for the youngest children (*groep* 1 and 2, about ages 4 to 6), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The wording speaks of becoming aware, not of adapting one's speech. What follows "such as" are examples: reactions of others, non-verbal reactions of others, and gestures and facial expression that support what someone contributes. The last part of the bracket ("and also of gestures and facial expression") reads on from "becoming aware of"; the source prints it inside the bracket. The statement does not say which conversation partners or which differences between them.

### Common mistakes

Not looking at the listener while talking, so that a puzzled or bored face goes unnoticed. Not noticing that people speak differently to different partners, for example that a classmate speaks to an unfamiliar adult in another way than to a friend. Reading a facial expression only when it is strong (crying, laughing) and missing a frown.
