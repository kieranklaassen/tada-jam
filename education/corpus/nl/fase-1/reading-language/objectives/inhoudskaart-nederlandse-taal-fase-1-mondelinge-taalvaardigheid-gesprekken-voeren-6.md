## Official wording

oriënteren op een gespreksonderwerp (bijv. door te zoeken naar antwoorden op vragen zoals: waar gaat het over? wat weet ik ervan? wat wil ik vertellen/weten?)

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Nederlandse taal: ontluikende en beginnende geletterdheid - jonge kind (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

getting an orientation on a topic of conversation (e.g. by looking for answers to questions such as: what is it about? what do I know about it? what do I want to tell/know?)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

Before a talk about the dentist, the child looks at a toothbrush and a picture and says what they think the talk will be about. The child tells something they already know and something they want to ask or tell. A child of 4 names the object and an experience of their own ("I went to the dentist"); a child of 6 puts a question of their own before the talk starts.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1, for the youngest children (*groep* 1 and 2, about ages 4 to 6), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. Looking for answers to questions is given as an example of how to orient, and the three questions (what is it about? what do I know about it? what do I want to tell or know?) are examples of such questions; they are not a fixed list. The statement does not say whether the child does this alone or with help. It names no topics.

### Common mistakes

Starting to tell a whole story at once when asked what the talk will be about. Saying "I don't know anything" about a topic the child has experience of, because the word for it is unfamiliar. Taking "what do you want to know?" as an invitation to tell something.
