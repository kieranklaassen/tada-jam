## Official wording

interpreteren van wat wordt verteld (relateren aan eigen kennis en ervaring; betekenis afleiden uit intonatie en stemgebruik)

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Nederlandse taal: ontluikende en beginnende geletterdheid - jonge kind (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

interpreting what is told (relating it to one's own knowledge and experience; deriving meaning from intonation and use of voice)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

Hearing a story about a child who is afraid in the dark, the child says "I have a night light too" and so links the story to their own life. The child hears from the reader's low, slow voice that something exciting is coming, and from a raised voice that a character is angry. A child of 4 reacts to the voice with face and body; a child of 6 says how a character feels and what in the voice told them so.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1, for the youngest children (*groep* 1 and 2, about ages 4 to 6), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The brackets name two ways of interpreting: relating what is told to one's own knowledge and experience, and deriving meaning from intonation and use of voice. The statement names no kind of text and sets no difficulty. It does not mention pictures or facial expression as sources of meaning.

### Common mistakes

Moving from the story into their own experience and not returning to what was told. Taking the words literally when the tone shows a joke or the opposite meaning (a teasing "well done" after a spill). Replacing what the story said with what they already knew, for example calling the wolf bad in a story in which the wolf is kind.
