## Official wording

kennismaken met en lezen van: - verhalende teksten, vooral expressief (bijv. sprookjes, dierenverhalen, realistische verhalen, fantasieverhalen, voorleesverhalen, prentenboeken); - poëzie, simpele rijmpjes, versjes en liedjes die vooral vormvast zijn (bijv. wiegeliedjes, slaapliedjes, dansliedjes, kringliedjes, aftelversjes, springliedjes, sinterklaasversjes, raadselrijmen, spotversjes)

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

getting to know and reading: - narrative texts, mainly expressive (e.g. fairy tales, animal stories, realistic stories, fantasy stories, read-aloud stories, picture books); - poetry, simple rhymes, verses and songs that mainly have a fixed form (e.g. cradle songs, lullabies, dance songs, circle songs, counting-out rhymes, skipping songs, *Sinterklaas* verses (for the feast of St Nicholas), riddle rhymes, mocking verses)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child listens to picture books and stories read aloud, looks through them alone, and later reads short ones. The child sings and says songs and verses with the group: a song for dancing in a circle, a rhyme for counting out who is "it", a verse while skipping rope. A child of 4 hears these texts and joins in from memory; a child of 7 also reads short stories and verses alone.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1 (*groep* 1 to 3, about ages 4 to 7), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names two groups of fiction: narrative texts that are mainly expressive, and poetry, simple rhymes, verses and songs that mainly have a fixed form. All the kinds in brackets are examples, introduced by "bijv."; none is required. The two verbs are getting to know and reading: the wording does not say which kinds the child reads alone and which are read or sung to the child, and it sets no length or difficulty.

### Common mistakes

This is not a skill with a typical error. Children who say a verse or song from memory often join words together or replace a word they do not know with one that sounds like it. A child may believe they are reading when they say a known verse from memory with a finger on the page, while the finger does not match the words.
