## Official wording

ervaring opdoen met het schrijven van korte teksten met meestal één inhoudselement, vaak met een chronologische opbouw: - verhalende teksten (bijv. briefje, kaartje) - eenvoudige informatieve teksten (bijv. bericht of antwoorden op vragen) - eenvoudige instructieve teksten (bijv. recept of korte gebruiksaanwijzing)

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Nederlandse taal: ontluikende en beginnende geletterdheid - jonge kind (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

gaining experience with writing short texts with mostly one content element, often with a chronological structure: - narrative texts (e.g. a note, a card) - simple informative texts (e.g. a message or answers to questions) - simple instructional texts (e.g. a recipe or short instructions for use)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child in *groep* 3 (about 6 or 7) writes a note or a card to someone they know. The child writes a few sentences that answer questions about an animal, or writes down in order the steps of something the class made. The child reads the text to a classmate, who says whether they can follow it.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1 (*groep* 1 to 3, about ages 4 to 7), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The card prints this statement under a heading that says from *groep* 3 on (about ages 6 to 7). The wording says gaining experience. The texts are short, have "mostly one content element" and are "often" built in chronological order; the statement sets no number of sentences. Three kinds are named as sub-points: narrative texts, simple informative texts and simple instructional texts. What stands in brackets after each kind is an example; the card gives a note and a card as its examples of narrative texts. Under Common mistakes, *en toen* is the pack's example.

### Common mistakes

Starting in the middle, as if the reader already knows who and where. Joining every sentence to the one before with *en toen* (and then). Putting the steps of an instruction in the wrong order, or leaving one out.
