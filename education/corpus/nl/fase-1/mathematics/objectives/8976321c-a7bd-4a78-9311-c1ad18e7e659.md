## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

investigating and naming differences between three-dimensional figures and the two-dimensional figures that belong to them

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child holds a ball and a paper circle, a cube and a paper square, and says what differs: the ball rolls and can be held all round, the circle lies flat. The child presses a block into sand or clay, or draws round it, and sees which flat shape each side leaves. A child of 4 feels and says "this one is flat" and "this one rolls"; a child of 7 names both the solid and its flat shape and says that a cube has square faces.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1 (*groep* 1 to 3, about ages 4 to 7), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names no figures; examples of shapes are in rw/mk/3/03/fase1. It does not say which two-dimensional figure belongs to which three-dimensional one.

### Common mistakes

The child uses the name of the flat shape for the solid, and calls a ball a circle and a cube a square. The child thinks a solid has only the flat shape that is seen from the front. The child does not expect a tin (a cylinder) to leave a circle when it is pressed on its end.
