## Official wording

verschillen onderzoeken en benoemen tussen driedimensionale figuren en daarbij behorende tweedimensionale figuren

Source: SLO (Stichting Leerplanontwikkeling), Inhoudskaart Rekenen-wiskunde - kleuters (fase 1), page 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

investigating and naming differences between three-dimensional figures and the two-dimensional figures that belong to them

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child holds a ball and a paper circle, a cube and a paper square, and says what differs: the ball can roll and can be held all round, the circle lies flat. The child presses a block into clay or sand, or draws around it, and sees which flat shape each face leaves. The child feels a block in a bag and picks the drawn shape that matches one of its faces. A child of 4 says that one is "flat" and the other "a real block"; a child of 6 says that a cube has a square on every side and that a tin leaves a circle as its print.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 1, for the youngest children (*groep* 1 and 2, about ages 4 to 6), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The statement is about differences, which the child investigates and names. It names no figures and does not say which three-dimensional and two-dimensional figures belong together. It does not mention the formal names of solids.

### Common mistakes

The child calls a ball a circle and a cube a square. The child expects one solid to give one flat shape and is surprised that a tin gives a circle from its end and a rectangle when its side is rolled out. The child counts only the faces that can be seen from one side.
