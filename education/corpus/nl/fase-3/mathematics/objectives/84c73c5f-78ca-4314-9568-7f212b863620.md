## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

investigating the structure of the counting sequence (*telrij*) up to at least 1 billion (*miljard*, 1,000,000,000) and reasoning about it

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child writes 1,000, 1,000,000 and 1,000,000,000 under each other in a place-value chart and sees that each group of three digits is worth 1,000 times the group to its right. The child works out that 1,000 thousands make a million and 1,000 millions make a billion (in Dutch a *miljard*), and says which number follows 999,999 and which follows 999,999,999. A child of 10 writes the numbers in a place-value chart and points out the pattern in it; a child of 12 explains why the pattern of ones, tens and hundreds returns in each group of three digits and reasons about how it goes on.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal states its range as "tot ten minste 1 miljard" (up to at least 1 billion): the offer reaches 1,000,000,000 at the least, no upper limit is set, and the wording does not say whether 1,000,000,000 itself is included. The Dutch *miljard* is 1,000,000,000, a thousand millions, which English calls a billion; the Dutch word *biljoen* is another number, a million millions. The range belongs to investigating the structure of the counting sequence and reasoning about it; the goal does not say that pupils calculate with numbers of this size. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: "Explain why there is no 'last number' in the counting sequence. Is there a first number?"; it is an example, not a limit.

### Common mistakes

The child thinks that a million comes right after 100,000, or that a billion is 100 millions, and so loses part of a group of three digits. The child writes one million with five or seven zeros. The child reads a long number from the left digit by digit without first splitting it into groups of three, and gives it the wrong name.
