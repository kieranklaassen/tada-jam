thinking critically and reasoning about weight in problem situations

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child works on a question about weight and gives reasons: whether a group of people stays under the maximum load of a lift, whether a car may be filled to the roof with paper, whether a stated weight can be right. The child estimates with weights it knows, calculates, and says which data it still needs. A child of 10 compares and adds weights that are given; a child of 12 also reasons about what is not given, for example estimates the weight of one pack of paper and works out the weight of a full load from it.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal's own wording says "problem situations" and names none. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: a car may be loaded only up to a certain weight, and the question is whether it may then be loaded full of paper, with the request to explain why or why not; it is an example, not a limit. The car full of paper in the paragraph above comes from that example; the lift and the stated weight are the pack's. Neither the wording nor the example names a unit, a weighing instrument, a conversion or a formula, and the goal states no number range. The goals of this kind for length and perimeter, area and *inhoud* are `rw/m/1/12/fase3`, `rw/m/2/10/fase3` and `rw/m/3/10/fase3`.

### Common mistakes

The child judges the weight of a load by one piece of it and takes a car full of paper to be light, because one sheet is light. The child compares numbers that have different measures without converting. The child accepts a result that cannot be right and does not check it against a weight it knows.
