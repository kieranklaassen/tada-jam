## English gloss

comparing (standardised) ratios (*verhoudingen*, a word that covers both ratio and proportion)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child compares two players, one who scores 3 times in 4 shots and one who scores 7 times in 10 shots, by rewriting both to 100 shots: 75 in 100 and 70 in 100. The child compares two packs of cheese, 250 grams for 2.00 euro and 400 grams for 3.00 euro, by working out the price per 100 grams, 0.80 euro and 0.75 euro. A child of 10 compares ratios after bringing them to a standard that is given, such as per 100; a child of 12 compares them after choosing the standard, such as per 100, per 1 or per kilogram.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal is about comparing ratios; "gestandaardiseerde" (standardised) is in brackets, so ratios as they are given and ratios brought to a standard are both covered. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: "Which is more, 1 in 5 or 1 in 4?" (Wat is meer, 1 op de 5 of 1 op de 4?); it is an example, not a limit. The text does not say what the standard is; per 100, per 1 and per kilogram in the examples above are the pack's. The goal's own wording states no number range and names no ratios; the example compares 1 in 5 with 1 in 4.

### Common mistakes

The child compares only the first numbers and calls 7 in 10 better than 3 in 4 because 7 is more than 3. The child brings only one of the two ratios to the standard. The child changes one number of a ratio to reach the standard and leaves the other number as it was.
