## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

saying aloud and writing down the various weight measures, including the concept '*ton*' (as a weight, the tonne) (not to be confused with the concept '*ton*' in the context of money)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child reads weights aloud from labels and lists, for example "three hundred and fifty grams" for 350 g and "two and a half kilograms" for 2.5 kg, and writes a weight it hears with the right abbreviation. The child says that the 3 *ton* of a lorry load is a weight and that the 3 *ton* of a house price is an amount of money, and tells from the situation which of the two is meant. A child of 10 says and writes the measures and tells from the situation which *ton* is meant; a child of 12 does the same, so the two differ little here.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal speaks of "the various weight measures" and names one of them, the *ton*; it lists no others. The measures kg, hg, dag, g, dg, cg and mg are listed in `rw/m/4/05/fase3` and `rw/m/4/06/fase3`. The goal does not print the size of a *ton*: as a weight a *ton* is 1000 kg, and in the context of money a *ton* is 100,000 euros; the wording only warns against confusing the two. The goal is about saying and writing: it names no conversion and no formula, and it states no number range.

### Common mistakes

The child confuses abbreviations that look alike, for example dag (decagram) and dg (decigram), or mg and g. The child takes a *ton* to be 100 kg, or uses the number 100,000 of the money sense for a weight. The child writes a weight as a number without a measure.
