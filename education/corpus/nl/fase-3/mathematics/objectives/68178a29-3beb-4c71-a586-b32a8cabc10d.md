## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

constructing block constructions and matching ground plans (*plattegronden*, plans drawn as seen from above) with height numbers (*hoogtegetallen*, the number of blocks on each square) and three-dimensional objects with the help of a *bouwplaat* (a net to fold, or a sheet to build from) or on the basis of a complex step-by-step plan

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child builds a construction of cubes and draws its ground plan: a grid seen from above with in each square the number of cubes that stand on it. In the other direction the child reads a ground plan with height numbers and builds the construction, for example a row with the numbers 3, 1 and 2 as three stacks of three cubes, one cube and two cubes, six cubes in all. The child also cuts out and folds a *bouwplaat* into a three-dimensional object, or follows a plan of many steps to make one. A child of 10 builds from a given ground plan and writes the height numbers for a small construction; a child of 12 works out from the height numbers what the front and the side look like before building, and follows a long plan without skipping a step.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names three things to construct: block constructions, matching *plattegronden* with height numbers, and three-dimensional objects. *Plattegrond* is also the word for a floor plan or a street map; with height numbers it is the plan of a block construction seen from above, with in each square the number of blocks that stand there. Two means are named, joined by "or": a *bouwplaat* and a complex step-by-step plan; the sentence most naturally joins them to the three-dimensional objects, and it does not rule out that they belong to all three. *Bouwplaat* has two readings, a net that is cut out and folded into a solid, and a printed sheet with building instructions; the goal does not say which. The step-by-step plan is "complex"; the goal does not say what counts as complex, states no number of blocks and names no objects. The goal's own wording names no material. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: with *vouwblaadjes* (squares of folding paper), Lego, Knexx (two brands of building toys; the source prints "Knexx"); it is an example, not a limit. The example stands after the whole sentence and does not say to which of the things to construct each material belongs; the cubes in the paragraph above are the pack's.

### Common mistakes

The child writes in a square the number of blocks it can see from the side and not the number that stand on that square. The child builds the construction mirrored or a quarter turn round, because it does not hold the ground plan the same way as the construction. The child skips a step of a long plan and finds later that the parts do not fit.
