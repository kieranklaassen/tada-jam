## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

approximating lengths and perimeters (*omtrekken*, also circumferences) by making use of measuring off with a measure (*afpassen*, laying a measure end to end), reference measures (*referentiematen*, familiar things of a known size) and scale

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child estimates a length in three ways: by walking it in steps of about a metre, or laying a stick end to end, and counting; by comparing with something whose length it knows, for example a door of about 2 metres high; and by measuring on a map and using the scale, for example 4 cm on a map with the scale 1 : 10,000 is 40,000 cm, so 400 m. For a perimeter the child walks along the whole edge of a field and counts its steps. A child of 10 approximates a length or a perimeter in these ways, for example the height of something in a photo from a person who stands beside it; a child of 12 does the same, so the two differ little here.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names three ways of approximating: measuring off with a measure, reference measures and scale. Its own wording names no measure to measure off with, no reference measure and no way of writing a scale. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: the question how high the tower is, about (*ongeveer*), when one looks at this photo, and how high when the height of a person can be used as a reference; it is an example, not a limit. The person in a photo in the paragraph above comes from that example; the steps, the stick, the door and the scale 1 : 10,000 are the pack's. The goal does not say how close the approximation has to be. Neither the wording nor the example names a unit, a conversion or a formula, and the goal states no number range.

### Common mistakes

The child takes steps that are shorter than a metre and counts each as a metre. Working with a scale, the child converts wrongly from centimetres on the map to metres or kilometres in reality, for example makes 40,000 cm into 40 m or 4 km. For a perimeter the child estimates one side, or leaves out sides.
