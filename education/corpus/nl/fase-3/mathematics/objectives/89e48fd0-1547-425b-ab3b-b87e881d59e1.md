## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

working with and reasoning about line symmetry and point symmetry in shapes and figures

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child folds a cut-out figure, or holds a mirror on it, to find every line that divides it into two halves that are each other's mirror image. The child turns a figure half a turn around its centre, for example on a pin, and sees whether it lies exactly on its own outline again. The child sorts figures into those with line symmetry, with point symmetry, with both and with neither, and says why; a parallelogram with unequal sides and no right angles, for example, has point symmetry and no line symmetry. A child of 10 tests a figure by folding it and by turning it; a child of 12 reasons without folding or turning which symmetry a figure has.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names two kinds of symmetry and no others: line symmetry (a figure has a line that divides it into two halves that are each other's mirror image) and point symmetry (a figure lies on itself again after half a turn around a point). The two explanations are the pack's; the goal gives none. Both are "in shapes and figures"; the goal names no particular shape or figure and does not say whether they are flat or three-dimensional. It does not name symmetry by a turn other than half a turn.

### Common mistakes

The child takes the diagonal of a rectangle for a mirror line, because it divides the rectangle into two equal halves. The child thinks that a figure with point symmetry must also have a mirror line. The child stops after finding one mirror line and misses the others.
