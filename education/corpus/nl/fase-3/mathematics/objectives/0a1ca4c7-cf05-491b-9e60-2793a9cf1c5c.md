interpreting, comparing and calculating (converting, *herleiden*) with population density (number of inhabitants per square kilometre)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child says what a population density means: at 500 inhabitants per square kilometre, 500 people live on average on each square of 1 kilometre by 1 kilometre. The child works out a density from a number of inhabitants and an area, for example 12,000 inhabitants on 30 square kilometres gives 400 inhabitants per square kilometre, and compares two regions: the one with more inhabitants can have the lower density when its area is larger. In the other direction the child works out the number of inhabitants from a density and an area: 250 inhabitants per square kilometre on 40 square kilometres is 10,000 inhabitants. A child of 10 compares densities that are given and says where people live closer together; a child of 12 calculates the density, the number of inhabitants or the area from the other two.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names three activities, interpreting, comparing and calculating (with *herleiden*, converting, in brackets), with one compound quantity: population density. The brackets say what it is and name its unit: the number of inhabitants per square kilometre. The goal names no other unit of area and no formula. It names no countries or regions; the numbers in the paragraph above are made up by the pack. This goal states no number range.

### Common mistakes

The child takes the region with the most inhabitants for the region with the highest density. The child divides the area by the number of inhabitants. The child reads the density as the number of people who live on every single square kilometre, and does not see that it is an average over the whole area.
