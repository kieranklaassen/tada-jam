## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

investigating and reasoning about sight lines (*kijklijnen*, lines from an eye to what is seen) in a space (*ruimte*, also: a room)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child stretches a string or lays a ruler from the eye of a viewer past the edge of an object, to find what the viewer can and cannot see behind it. The child draws sight lines on a plan seen from above or on a side view, for example from a person past the corner of a building, and shades the part that stays hidden. The child reasons with the lines: whether a person behind a wall can be seen from a window, and how that changes when the viewer comes closer or stands higher. A child of 10 tries it with real objects and a string; a child of 12 draws the lines on paper and predicts before checking.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names two activities, investigating and reasoning, about sight lines "in een ruimte", which can be read as in a space or in a room. A sight line is a line from the eye of a viewer to what the viewer sees; the explanation is the pack's. The goal's own wording names no kind of drawing (a plan from above, a side view), no objects and no measuring. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: the question whether, if I see you in the mirror, you can then also see me, and when you can and when not; it is an example, not a limit. The example names one object, a mirror; with a mirror a sight line runs straight to the mirror and straight on from it, which is the pack's explanation too. The paragraph above has no mirror; its string, wall and window are the pack's. Sight lines used for shadows are named in `rw/mk/3/04/fase3`.

### Common mistakes

The child draws a sight line that bends around an object. The child starts the sight line at the feet of the viewer, or at the object, and not at the viewer's eye. The child thinks that a driver can see it because it can see the vehicle, and does not ask whether a straight line runs from the driver's eyes to itself.
