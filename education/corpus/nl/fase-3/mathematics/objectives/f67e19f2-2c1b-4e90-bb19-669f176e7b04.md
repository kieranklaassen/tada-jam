## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

thinking critically and reasoning about area (*oppervlakte*) and about the relation between perimeter (*omtrek*) and area

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child works on a question such as: a square has a perimeter of 100 cm, what is its area? Each side is 100 : 4 = 25 cm, so the area is 25 x 25 = 625 cm²; the child then says why this cannot be worked out for a rectangle from its perimeter alone. With a loop of string of a fixed length the child makes different rectangles and finds that the area changes while the perimeter stays the same. A child of 10 finds by trying that the perimeter of a figure does not fix its area; a child of 12 explains why and gives examples of its own.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal has two subjects: area, and the relation between perimeter and area. Unlike the goals of this kind for length and perimeter, *inhoud* and weight (`rw/m/1/12/fase3`, `rw/m/3/10/fase3`, `rw/m/4/07/fase3`), this wording does not have the words "in problem situations". That a different perimeter is possible with an equal area is the subject of `rw/m/1/11/fase3`. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: three questions, how the area can be worked out when the perimeter of a square is 100 cm, why that cannot be done for a rectangle, and whether a square metre can also be round; it is an example, not a limit. The square with a perimeter of 100 cm and the question about the rectangle in the paragraph above come from that example; the loop of string is the pack's. The goal's own wording names no figure, no unit, no conversion and no formula, and it states no number range; the example names the square and the rectangle and uses the centimetre and the square metre.

### Common mistakes

The child thinks that a longer perimeter always goes with a larger area. The child thinks that figures with the same perimeter have the same area. The child confuses the two calculations and adds the sides for the area or multiplies them for the perimeter.
