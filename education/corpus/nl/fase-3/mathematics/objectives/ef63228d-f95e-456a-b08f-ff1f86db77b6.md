## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child lays out how the units of time build on each other: 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, 24 hours in a day, 7 days in a week, 12 months in a year, 100 years in a century. The child says which steps are fixed and which are not, for example that a month has 28 to 31 days, and compares this with the steps of ten in the numbers. The child reads a timeline: which way time runs, what one section stands for, and where a given year or period lies. A child of 10 names the steps between neighbouring units; a child of 12 explains the whole structure, including that the calendar and the clock do not go in steps of ten.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The verb is understanding, and it has two objects: the structure of the time system, which the brackets say includes the timeline (*tijdbalk*, a bar or line on which years or periods lie in order), and the connection between the different units of time. The goal names no unit of time and prints no relation between units; the relations in the paragraph above are the pack's examples. The units are listed in `rw/m/6/05/fase3`. This goal states no number range.

### Common mistakes

The child treats time as a system of tens, for example takes 1.5 hours for 1 hour and 50 minutes or expects 100 minutes in an hour. The child thinks that every month has 30 days or exactly 4 weeks. The child reads a timeline without looking at what one section stands for, and takes sections of 10 years for sections of 100 years.
