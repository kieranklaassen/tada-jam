## Official wording

met elkaar in verband brengen van gegevens uit tabellen of grafische voorstellingen en op basis hiervan trends herkennen, conclusies trekken en voorspellingen doen, ook met gegevens die binnen één grafische voorstelling worden gecombineerd

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

relating to each other data from tables or graphic representations and on the basis of this recognising trends, drawing conclusions and making predictions, also with data that are combined within one graphic representation

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child lays two displays next to each other, for example a table of ice-cream sales per month and a line graph of the temperature per month, and says how the two go together. The child describes a trend, such as a number that rises each year, draws a conclusion from it, predicts the next value and says how sure the prediction is. The child reads a display that combines two sets of data, for example a graph with two lines and a legend, and compares the lines: where they cross, and where one rises while the other falls. A child of 10 reads off and compares values from two displays; a child of 12 names the trend, predicts beyond the data and says what the data cannot show.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal names relating data from tables or graphic representations to each other, and three things done on that basis: recognising trends, drawing conclusions and making predictions. The last part adds data that are combined within one graphic representation. The goal names no kind of graphic representation, no kind of data, no formula and no number range; the displays in the paragraph above are the pack's examples.

### Common mistakes

The child concludes that one thing causes the other because two lines rise together. The child extends a trend without end, for example predicts that a child who grew 6 centimetres a year keeps doing so as an adult. The child compares two lines in one graph that have different units or different axes as if the numbers meant the same.
