## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child reads a measure that is written as a decimal number and says what the digits after the decimal point stand for in a smaller measure: 2.5 kg is 2 kg and 500 g, so 2500 g; 1.5 l is 1 l and 5 dl, so 15 dl. In the other direction the child writes 750 g as 0.75 kg. A child of 10 checks on scales or on a measuring jug what the decimals stand for; a child of 12 says from the number alone which measure belongs to each decimal place.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal stands in the cluster for weight (rw/m/4). Its wording says *inhoudsmaten* (measures of volume or capacity) and does not say *gewichtsmaten* (weight measures); the gloss follows the wording. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: 2.5 kg of potatoes, that is 2500 grams (printed "2,5 kg aardappels, dat is 2500 gram"); it is an example, not a limit. So the wording names measures of volume or capacity, and the example shows two weight measures, the kilogram and the gram, with a number that has one decimal place. Neither says which kind of measure the goal is meant for, and the pack does not settle it: the paragraph above gives the official example, one more with weight and one with litres, and the last two are the pack's. The goal's own wording names no unit, no conversion and no formula, and it states no number range and no number of decimal places.

### Common mistakes

The child reads the digits after the decimal point as a whole number of the smaller measure, for example reads 2.5 kg as 2 kg and 5 g, or as 2 kg and 50 g in the way it reads euros and cents. The child thinks that 2.25 kg is more than 2.5 kg, because 25 is more than 5. The child moves the decimal point the wrong number of places, for example writes 750 g as 7.5 kg.
