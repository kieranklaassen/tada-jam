## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child lays 4 rows of 6 counters, turns the tray a quarter turn, sees 6 rows of 4 and says that 4 × 6 and 6 × 4 are the same amount. The child checks 156 ÷ 12 = 13 by multiplying 13 × 12 = 156, and works 6 × 98 as 6 × 100 − 6 × 2 = 588. The child tries whether changing the order also works for subtracting and finds that 8 − 3 and 3 − 8 are not the same. A child of 10 shows a property with counters or on a number line; a child of 12 puts it into words and uses it to shorten a calculation.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The goal's own wording names properties of operations and relationships between operations and lists none; rw/bew/1/06/fase3, rw/bew/3/06/fase3 and rw/bew/3/17/fase3 name examples for their own content. The content-line document prints an example with this goal: "Why can you swap the numbers in adding and multiplying (e.g. 3 + 5 = 5 + 3; 3 × 5 = 5 × 3), but not in subtracting and dividing (e.g. 100 − 99 ≠ 99 − 100 and 24 ÷ 3 ≠ 3 ÷ 24)" (in Dutch notation 3 x 5 = 5 x 3 and 24 : 3 ≠ 3 : 24); it is an example, not a limit. The document opens three brackets in this example and closes two, and prints no question mark. The goal's own wording states no number range and does not say which operations or which kinds of number; the example is about one property, swapping the two numbers, in all four operations, with whole numbers up to 100.

### Common mistakes

Changing the order in a subtraction or a division, as if 3 − 8 were the same as 8 − 3. Multiplying only one part when a multiplication is split, so that 6 × 98 becomes 6 × 100 − 2. Knowing a rule as a trick without being able to show why it works.
