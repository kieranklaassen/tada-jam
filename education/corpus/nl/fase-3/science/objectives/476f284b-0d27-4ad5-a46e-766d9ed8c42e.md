## English gloss

investigating what material or what combinations of materials an object consists of (e.g. metal, (natural) stone, glass, plastic, wood, paper, textile, concrete, polyester)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child takes an object apart, such as a pen, a shoe, a drinks carton or a bicycle lamp, lays out the parts and names the material of each. Where looking is not enough the child tests: tapping, scratching, weighing in the hand, holding a magnet to the part, looking at a torn or cut edge. The child records which materials are combined, such as the layers of card, plastic and aluminium foil in a drinks carton. A child of 10 names the materials of the separate parts; a child of 12 finds materials that are layered or mixed and names the test that showed it.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The nine materials in brackets follow "bijv." and are examples. The statement speaks of one material or combinations of materials in an object; it names no object and no way of testing. Changes to the properties of materials are in `ojw/nattech/1/02/fase3`.

### Common mistakes

Naming only the material that shows on the outside and missing a coating, a core or a layer. Calling every metal iron. Taking concrete for a natural stone, or painted metal for plastic.
