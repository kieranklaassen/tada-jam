## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

using joins in carrying out a design (fixed join such as pin/hole, sewn, welded; loose/fixed join such as *knoop* (button or knot), zip, bolt and nut)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

While building a design the child chooses how each pair of parts is joined: a peg pressed into a hole or a seam sewn by hand where the parts must stay together, and a bolt and nut, a button, a knot or a zip where they must come apart again. The child makes the joins, pulls and twists the object to test them and replaces a join that fails. The child finds the same kinds of join in a chair, a jacket and a bicycle, and finds the welded joins in a metal frame. A child of 10 picks a join that holds; a child of 12 picks the join by the material, the load and whether the part must come apart again, and gives the reason.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The joins are used in carrying out a design. The brackets name two groups, each with examples after "zoals": fixed joins (pin and hole, sewn, welded) and loose/fixed joins (*los/vaste verbinding*, joins that can be undone and fastened again: *knoop*, zip, bolt and nut). *Knoop* means button or knot, and the wording does not say which. Welding is named as an example of a fixed join without any condition; the text states no safety condition and says nothing about tools or supervision. Gluing, nailing and screwing are not in the lists.

### Common mistakes

Making a fixed join where the part must come apart or move later. Choosing a join that does not suit the material, such as a bolt through thin paper, which tears out. Placing a join so close to the edge that the material tears or splits there.
