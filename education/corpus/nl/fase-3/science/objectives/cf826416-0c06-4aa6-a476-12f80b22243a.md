## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

realising that the characteristics of a kind or species (*soortkenmerken*; *soort* means both) are passed on to offspring and what the consequence of that is

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child compares photographs of a litter of puppies or kittens with both parents and marks which features every young shares with the parents and which differ among the young. The child sows seeds taken from one sunflower and sees that only sunflowers come up. The child says what follows from this: a dog has puppies and never kittens, and young look like their parents without being copies of them. A child of 10 says that young look like their parents; a child of 12 says that characteristics come from both parents at reproduction, and that a species stays recognisable from one generation to the next for that reason.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The verb is realising. *Soortkenmerken* are the characteristics of a *soort*, a word that means both kind and species. The statement says that these are passed on to offspring and asks for the consequence, without saying what the consequence is. It sets no level of detail on how characteristics are passed on, and it names no plants or animals. Heredity in humans is named in `ojw/pdm/3/15/fase3`.

### Common mistakes

Thinking features gained during life are passed on, such as a scar, a docked tail or muscles made strong by training. Thinking a young animal gets its features from one parent only, for example females from the mother and males from the father. Thinking that young are exact copies of a parent.
