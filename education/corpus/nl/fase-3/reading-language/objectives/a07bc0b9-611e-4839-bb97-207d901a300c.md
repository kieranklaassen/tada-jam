## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

using abstract school language and subject language (*school- en vaktaal*: the language of lessons and the terms of subjects)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

Presenting the results of a survey, the child says "the majority", "on average", "compared with" and "we conclude that". In a talk about the Second World War the child says "occupation", "resistance" and "liberation" and uses each in a full sentence that shows its meaning. The child replaces "there were more and more" with "the number increased". A child of 10 uses abstract terms that were practised for this talk; a child of 12 uses abstract school language through a whole presentation, including sentence patterns such as "this leads to" and "in contrast to".

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The statement names school language and subject language in one compound and defines neither: *schooltaal* is commonly understood as the language of lessons and tasks across subjects, and *vaktaal* as the terms of one subject. Both are called abstract; the statement does not say what makes language abstract. It speaks of language (*taal*), where NE/MT/01/05/01/fase3, the matching goal for conversations, speaks of words (*woorden*). It names no words, no subjects and no number, and does not mention help. The open data lists it under speaking, in sub-cluster NE/MT/03/05, titled *Woordgebruik en woordenschat* (word use and vocabulary).

### Common mistakes

Using an abstract term learned by heart for the talk and being unable to say what it means when asked. Changing back to everyday wording as soon as the prepared part is over, for example when answering questions. Using an abstract word too widely, such as "average" for "most".
