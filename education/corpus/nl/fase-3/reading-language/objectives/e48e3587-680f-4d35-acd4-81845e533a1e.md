## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

recognising the difference between figurative and literal language use

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The child hears the Dutch saying *hij heeft een gat in zijn hand* (literally "he has a hole in his hand") and says that it is not meant literally and means that he spends money easily. From two spoken sentences, "the river froze" and "she froze when she saw the dog", the child says which use of "froze" is literal and which is figurative. The child hears a joke that depends on taking a saying literally and explains why it is funny. A child of 10 recognises common sayings and comparisons as figurative and explains them; a child of 12 also recognises a figure that the speaker has made up, or an exaggeration, and says how the context shows that it is not meant literally.

### Limits

This statement describes what a school offers in fase 3 (*groep* 7 and 8, about ages 10 to 12), not what a child must be able to do, and nothing says in which year. The verb is "recognising": the statement does not ask the child to use figurative language. It names no kinds of figurative language (comparison, metaphor, saying) and gives no examples. The open data lists it under listening, in sub-cluster NE/MT/02/06, titled *Woordenschat en -strategieën* (vocabulary and vocabulary strategies). The source prints the verb without *van* ("of") after it, unlike most neighbouring goals; the gloss reads it in the same form as theirs. The statement does not mention help.

### Common mistakes

Taking a figure literally, most often one the child has not heard before. Recognising that a sentence is not meant literally and giving it a wrong meaning from one of its words. Calling any unusual or difficult wording figurative.
