## Official wording

f) wel/niet -n in samengestelde woorden

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 4.4 Moeilijkheid, niveau 2F, Spelling / 3. Morfologische spelling / Moeilijke gevallen / f).

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

f) -n or no -n (*wel/niet -n*) in compound words

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 at this level writes a compound word whose two parts are joined by a sound written *-e-* or *-en-* and decides which it is, although both sound the same in everyday speech. The child writes *pannenkoek*, *boekenkast* and *hondenhok* (pancake, bookcase, kennel) with *-en-*, and *zonnebloem* and *rijstepap* (sunflower, rice pudding) with *-e-*.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, terms and language care, table of difficulty (*Moeilijkheid*), row f) of the difficult cases (*Moeilijke gevallen*) under *Spelling*, 3. *Morfologische spelling* (morphological spelling, the spelling of word forms such as plurals and diminutives). In this table (Table 2 of the decree) the row has a plus sign in the column for level 2F. The table's caption describes its levels as mastery: 75% of all pupils in the age group or level group have an 80% chance of getting it right. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The row is short: it names the question of -n or no -n in compound words, states no rule and gives no example. The pack reads it as the choice between *-e-* and *-en-* where the parts of a compound meet; the row itself does not say this. The pack's examples, all correct Dutch under the official spelling: with -n, *pannenkoek* (pancake), *boekenkast* (bookcase), *hondenhok* (kennel); without -n, *zonnebloem* (sunflower), *rijstepap* (rice pudding), *groentesoep* (vegetable soup).

### Common mistakes

A child writes what is heard and leaves the *n* out everywhere (the wrong *pannekoek* and *boekekast*). A child who has learned the rule adds the *n* everywhere, also in *zonnebloem* (the wrong *zonnenbloem*). A child decides by meaning ("one dog or several dogs?") instead of by the form of the first word, which gives a wrong answer for a word such as *hondenhok*, a kennel for one dog.
