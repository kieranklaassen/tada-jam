## Official wording

Legt relaties tussen tekstuele informatie en meer algemene kennis. Kan de bedoeling van tekstgedeeltes en/of specifieke formuleringen duiden.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 2.1 Zakelijke teksten, niveau 2F, Kenmerken van de taakuitvoering / Interpreteren / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Makes connections between textual information and more general knowledge. Can interpret (*duiden*, saying what something means) the intention of parts of the text and/or specific wordings.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 reads in a text about a dry summer that farmers may not spray their fields, and connects this with what the child already knows: plants need water, so the harvest will be smaller. The child says why the writer put in a paragraph with a farmer's own words (to show what the rule means for one person) and why the writer chose the word "only" in "only two days of rain".

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, reading factual texts (*Lezen zakelijke teksten*), row *Interpreteren* (interpreting). The row stands under *Kenmerken van de taakuitvoering* (characteristics of task performance); this is the first of the two paragraphs in its cell. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The first sentence speaks of "more general" knowledge and does not say which knowledge or where it comes from. The second names two things whose intention the pupil interprets: parts of the text and specific wordings, joined by "and/or". The intention of the writer is a separate statement in the same row. The statement names no kind of text, no length and no title.

### Common mistakes

A child answers from own knowledge alone and loses what the text says. A child retells a passage where its purpose is asked: says what the paragraph says, not why it is there. A child does not notice that a single word carries a judgement, such as "only" or "even".
