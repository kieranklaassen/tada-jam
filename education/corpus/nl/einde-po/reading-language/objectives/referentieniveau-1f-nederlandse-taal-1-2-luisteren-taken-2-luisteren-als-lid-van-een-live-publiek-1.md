## Official wording

Kan de hoofdlijn begrijpen van korte informatieve, instructieve en betogende teksten met een duidelijke structuur en voldoende herhaling.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 1.2 Luisteren, niveau 1F, Taken / 2. Luisteren als lid van een live publiek / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Can understand the main line of short informative, *instructieve* (instructing: how to do something, or teaching) and *betogende* (arguing a case: argumentative or persuasive) texts with a clear structure and enough repetition.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The pack's illustration: a pupil of about 12 sits in the school hall while a fire officer tells what to do when the alarm sounds, names three steps and repeats them at the end. Afterwards the pupil can say what the three steps are. In class the pupil listens to a classmate's short talk that argues for a later start of the school day, and can say what the classmate wants and the main reason given.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1F for Dutch, *Luisteren* (listening), task 2, *Luisteren als lid van een live publiek* (listening as a member of a live audience). It stands under *Taken* (tasks). The task has one other statement at 1F, on understanding a story. 1F is the level every pupil should reach at the end of primary school. The statement asks for the main line (*hoofdlijn*) only. It names three kinds of text and sets three conditions on them: short, with a clear structure, and with enough repetition. *Instructieve* texts tell how something is done or teach something, and *betogende* texts argue a case, which an English reader may take as argumentative or as persuasive; the statement does not choose. "Short" is given no number here; the text-feature statement *Lengte* (length) of *Luisteren* at 1F, another record of this lane, gives a listening time. The statement names no topics.

### Common mistakes

A pupil remembers an example or an anecdote from the talk and not the point it was given for. In a text that argues a case, a pupil can say the subject but not what the speaker wants the audience to think or do. A pupil no longer follows when the speaker leaves the order that was announced.
