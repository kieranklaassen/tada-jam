## Official wording

Kan teksten zodanig vloeiend lezen dat woordherkenning tekstbegrip niet in de weg staat. Kent de meest alledaagse (frequente) woorden, of kan de betekenis van een enkel onbekend woord uit de context afleiden.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 2.1 Zakelijke teksten, niveau 1F, Kenmerken van de taakuitvoering / Techniek en woordenschat / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Can read texts so fluently that word recognition does not get in the way of understanding the text. Knows the most everyday (frequent) words, or can derive the meaning of *een enkel* unknown word (a single one, or one here and there) from the context.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 reads a page at a steady pace without sounding out the words, and then tells what the page said. In a text about a summer without rain the child meets a word it does not know, reads the sentences around it again (the grass turned yellow, the ditches were empty), and says what the word must mean.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1F for Dutch, *Zakelijke teksten* (reading factual texts), *Techniek en woordenschat* (technique and vocabulary). It stands under *Kenmerken van de taakuitvoering* (features of task performance). 1F is the level every pupil should reach at the end of primary school. Fluency is bounded by its effect: fluent enough that recognising the words does not hinder understanding. The statement gives no reading speed and no word list, and it does not say whether the reading is aloud or silent. For vocabulary it names the most everyday (frequent) words. *Een enkel onbekend woord* can be read as one single unknown word or as an unknown word here and there; in both readings the unknown words are few, and the statement joins knowing the words and deriving a meaning with "or".

### Common mistakes

A child reads quickly and passes over a short word such as *niet* (not) or *geen* (no), which reverses the meaning of the sentence. A child reads so slowly, word by word, that it has forgotten the start of the sentence by the end. A child guesses a word from its first letters and reads another word that looks like it.
