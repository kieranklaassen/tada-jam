## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The statement is a cross-reference and names no action of its own. As an illustration of what it refers to, a pupil of about 12 gives a talk to the class and is clearly intelligible although one long word is pronounced wrongly. Most sentences are built correctly, and when a sentence goes wrong the pupil starts it again where the class would otherwise not have understood it.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, *Spreken* (speaking), row *Vloeiendheid, verstaanbaarheid en grammaticale beheersing* (fluency, intelligibility and command of grammar). It stands under *Kenmerken van de taakuitvoering* (features of task performance) and is the first of the two records of its cell. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The whole statement is two words and a plus sign, "Zie Gesprekken +". The two words refer the reader to section 1.1 *Gesprekken* (conversations). They are read here as referring to the row with the same heading under *Gesprekken* in the same level column, whose three statements are other records of this lane; the source does not say this in words. The plus sign says that the statement that follows in this cell is added to what the cross-reference refers to; that statement, on being easy to follow and on fluency, is another record of this lane. The decree prints the plus sign as a paragraph of its own under the reference; the wording joins it to the reference and that paragraph is no record, so the records of this cell have the positions 1 and 3. The statement itself names no quality of performance, and the source prints no full stop after it.

### Common mistakes

This is not a skill with a typical error: the statement is a cross-reference with a plus sign. The typical errors belong to the statements it refers to: speech that is too fast or too soft to understand, errors in verb forms and word order, and sentences that go wrong and are left unrepaired. In a talk to an audience pupils most often leave out the repair, because nobody interrupts to ask what was meant.
