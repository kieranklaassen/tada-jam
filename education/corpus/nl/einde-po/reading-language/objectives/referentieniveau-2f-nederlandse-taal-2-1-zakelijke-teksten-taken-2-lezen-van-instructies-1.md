## Official wording

Kan instructieve teksten lezen, zoals recepten, veelvoorkomende aanwijzingen en gebruiksaanwijzingen en bijsluiters van medicijnen.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 2.1 Zakelijke teksten, niveau 2F, Taken / 2. Lezen van instructies / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Can read instructive texts, such as recipes, frequently occurring directions and instructions for use and package leaflets of medicines.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 reads a recipe from start to finish and then says what is needed, how much of each thing, and in what order the steps come. The child reads the instructions for use of a device, such as a bicycle lamp, and finds the step that says how to fit the batteries. In the leaflet from a box of medicine the child finds the heading that says how the medicine must be stored and reads out what stands under it.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, reading factual texts (*Lezen zakelijke teksten*), row *2. Lezen van instructies* (reading instructions). The row stands under *Taken* (tasks). 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The kinds of text follow *zoals* ("such as"), so they are examples of the level, not a closed list. *Veelvoorkomende* (frequently occurring) stands before "directions and instructions for use"; the wording leaves open whether it also covers the medicine leaflets, and the gloss leaves it open too. The statement is about reading these texts; it does not say that the pupil carries out the instructions. For the medicine leaflet it sets no level of detail and states no safety condition. It names no length and no number of steps.

### Common mistakes

A child reads the first step only and not the whole instruction, and so misses that something had to be prepared beforehand. A child reads over a step or does not notice that the order of the steps matters. A child misreads an amount or a unit, for example takes tablespoons for teaspoons.
