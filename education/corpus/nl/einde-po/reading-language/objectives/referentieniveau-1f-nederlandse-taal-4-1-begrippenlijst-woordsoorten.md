## Official wording

Zelfstandig naamwoord, werkwoord (klankvast, klankveranderend (zwak, sterk)), bijvoeglijk naamwoord.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 4.1 Begrippenlijst, niveau 1F, Woordsoorten.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Noun (*zelfstandig naamwoord*), verb (*werkwoord*) (sound-constant, *klankvast*; sound-changing, *klankveranderend* (weak, strong; *zwak*, *sterk*)), adjective (*bijvoeglijk naamwoord*).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 sorts the words of a sentence by word class. In the pack's example, *De kleine hond blaft* (The small dog barks), the child names *hond* as a noun, *kleine* as an adjective and *blaft* as a verb. The child sorts verbs into two kinds by saying their past tense: *werken, werkte, gewerkt* (to work) keeps its vowel and is *klankvast* or weak; *lopen, liep, gelopen* (to walk) changes its vowel and is *klankveranderend* or strong.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1F for Dutch, *Begrippenlijst en Taalverzorging* (list of terms and language care), the row *Woordsoorten* (word classes) of the list of terms (*Begrippenlijst*). It is the 1F cell of that row in the decree's table 1, which gives the terms about language by level. 1F is the level every pupil should reach at the end of primary school. The cell names three word classes, noun, verb and adjective, and nothing more: no definitions and no mark of how well a pupil must know them. The introduction to the list in the decree, which is in no record, says that a limited number of terms is needed for talking about language and that most of them have been dealt with by the end of primary school. No other word class, such as the article, the pronoun or the preposition, is in this cell. For the verb the brackets give two kinds, *klankvast* and *klankveranderend*, and then, in brackets within the brackets, *zwak* and *sterk*. As printed, "weak, strong" stands after *klankveranderend* only; it is plainly meant as the traditional pair of names for the two kinds in the same order: a weak verb keeps its vowel in the past tense, and a strong verb changes it.

### Common mistakes

A child knows a verb only as a "doing word" and so does not see *zijn* (to be) or *hebben* (to have) as verbs. A child does not recognise an adjective when it comes after the noun, as *klein* in *De hond is klein* (The dog is small). A child makes a weak past tense for a strong verb and writes *loopte*; correct is *liep*.
