## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

a) spelling-ambiguous words (*spelambigue woorden*)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 at this level writes words in which one sound can be written in two ways and the sound does not tell which: *ei* or *ij*, *au* or *ou*, *g* or *ch*, a final *d* or *t*, *c* or *k*. For some words the child uses a rule, such as making the word longer to hear the final letter (*hond*, dog, because of *honden*, dogs); for others the child knows the written word by sight (*klein*, *prijs*, *pauw*, *koud*: small, price, peacock, cold).

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, terms and language care, table of difficulty (*Moeilijkheid*), row a) under *Spelling*, 6. *Overige regels* (other rules). In this table (Table 2 of the decree) the row has a plus sign in the column for level 2F. The table's caption describes its levels as mastery: 75% of all pupils in the age group or level group have an 80% chance of getting it right. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The row is two words and gives no rule and no example. The same section of the decree, in its running list of spelling categories, describes spelling-ambiguous words as words that can be spelled in two ways because the sound does not decide, where two letter signs stand for one sound (au/ou, d/t, ei/ij, ch/g, c/k); that list carries no level and is not a record of the pack. The row does not say which words are meant or how many. The item in that running list gives examples of its own, each as a wrong spelling with the right one in brackets; among them are *klein* (small), *dacht* (thought, the past tense), *antwoord* (answer) and *direct* (direct). They are the decree's examples from a list that carries no level. The pack's examples, all correct Dutch: *prijs* (price), *pauw* (peacock), *koud* (cold).

### Common mistakes

A child writes the other possible letter for the sound (the wrong *klijn*, *dagt*, *antwoort* and *direkt*, which are the decree's own examples in that list). A child uses the lengthening rule for a final *d* or *t* in a single word but not inside a longer word (the wrong *hontje* where *hondje*, little dog, is right). A child spells a frequent word correctly and a rare word with the same sound wrongly, because the choice rests on remembering the written word.
