## Official wording

Kan betogende, vaak redundante teksten lezen, zoals reclameteksten, advertenties, folders, maar ook brochures van formele instanties of licht opiniërende artikelen uit tijdschriften.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 2.1 Zakelijke teksten, niveau 2F, Taken / 3. Lezen van betogende teksten / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Can read argumentative, often redundant texts, such as advertising texts, advertisements, leaflets, but also brochures from formal institutions or mildly opinion-giving articles from magazines.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 who reads at this level reads a leaflet from a sports club that wants new members and says what the leaflet wants the reader to do and which reasons it gives. The child notices that the same message returns several times in other words. The child also reads a brochure from an official body, such as a municipality's brochure about separating waste, or a magazine article in which the writer gives some opinion, and says what the writer's opinion is and which sentences give facts.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, reading factual texts (*Lezen zakelijke teksten*), row *3. Lezen van betogende teksten* (reading argumentative texts). The row stands under *Taken* (tasks). 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The texts are "often redundant": a redundant text says the same thing more than once, and "often" is the statement's own word. The kinds of text follow *zoals* ("such as") and are examples of the level. The words *maar ook* ("but also") add two kinds: brochures from formal institutions, and magazine articles that are *licht opiniërend*, mildly opinion-giving; "mildly" is part of the level. The statement names no titles and no length.

### Common mistakes

A child takes a claim in an advertisement for a fact. A child gives the topic of the text but not what the writer wants the reader to think or do. A child takes each repetition of the same message for a new argument.
