## Official wording

Kan een helder gestructureerde voordracht, toespraak of les begrijpen over vertrouwde onderwerpen binnen het eigen vak- of interessegebied.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 1.2 Luisteren, niveau 2F, Taken / 2. Luisteren als lid van een live publiek / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Can understand a clearly structured *voordracht* (a talk or lecture, or a recitation), speech or lesson about familiar subjects within one's own subject area or field of interest (*vak- of interessegebied*).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

The pack's illustration: a pupil of about 12 who keeps rabbits sits in the school hall while a vet gives a talk about caring for pets. The talk has a clear order: food, housing, illness. The pupil follows it to the end and tells a parent at home what the vet said about each of the three.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, *Luisteren* (listening), row *Luisteren als lid van een live publiek* (listening as a member of a live audience). It stands under *Taken* (tasks), as task 2, and is the first of two statements in its cell. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. *Voordracht* has two readings in Dutch: a talk or lecture, and a recitation of a text or poem before an audience; the gloss keeps the Dutch. The statement names three kinds of spoken text (*voordracht*, speech, lesson) and three conditions: the text is clearly structured, the subjects are familiar (*vertrouwde*), and they lie within the pupil's own *vak- of interessegebied*. *Vakgebied* is a field of study or of work; the statement does not say what it is for a pupil in primary school. The statement sets no length; length stands in the row *Lengte*, another record of this lane.

### Common mistakes

This is not a skill with a typical error. What pupils most often leave out is the structure: they remember separate facts from a talk and not the parts it consisted of or how these followed each other. Many stop listening when the talk goes beyond what they already know about the subject.
