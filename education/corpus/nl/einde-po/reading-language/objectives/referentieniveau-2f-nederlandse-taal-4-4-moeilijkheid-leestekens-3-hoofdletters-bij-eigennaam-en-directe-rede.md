## Official wording

3. Hoofdletters bij eigennaam en directe rede

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 4.4 Moeilijkheid, niveau 2F, Leestekens / 3. Hoofdletters bij eigennaam en directe rede.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

3. Capital letters with a proper name (*eigennaam*) and direct speech (*directe rede*)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 at this level writes a capital letter at the start of the names of persons and places in the child's own text, also in the middle of a sentence. When the child writes down word for word what someone says, the child puts a colon, opens the quotation marks and starts the spoken sentence with a capital letter.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, terms and language care, table of difficulty (*Moeilijkheid*), row 3 under *Leestekens* (punctuation marks). In this table (Table 2 of the decree) the row has a plus sign in the column for level 2F. The table's caption describes its levels as mastery: 75% of all pupils in the age group or level group have an 80% chance of getting it right. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The row names two uses of the capital letter: with a proper name and with direct speech (the words someone says, quoted exactly). It gives no examples, does not say which kinds of names are meant (for example persons, places or organisations), and states no rule for the colon and the quotation marks around direct speech. The pack's examples, both correct Dutch: *Sanne woont in Den Haag.* (Sanne lives in The Hague.) and *Vera zei: ‘Ik kom morgen.’* (Vera said: "I am coming tomorrow.").

### Common mistakes

A child starts the quoted sentence with a small letter after the colon. A child writes a capital letter at the start of a sentence but not in a name in the middle of it. A child gives days and months a capital letter (the wrong *Maandag*), where Dutch writes *maandag* (Monday) and *maart* (March).
