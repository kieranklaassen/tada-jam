## Official wording

Trema, accent.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 1, 4.1 Begrippenlijst, niveau 2F, Leestekens.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

Diaeresis (*trema*), accent (*accent*).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 at this level points to the two dots on the second *e* in *knieën* (knees), calls them a *trema*, and says what they do: they show that a new syllable starts there, so the word is read as *knie-en*. The child points to the mark on the *e* in *café*, calls it an *accent*, and says that it shows how the letter sounds or, in *één* (one), that the number is meant.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 2F for Dutch, terms and language care, list of terms (*Begrippenlijst*), row *Leestekens* (punctuation marks). It is the cell of this row in the column for level 2F of the decree's table of terms (Table 1); the table prints no sign, and the column gives the level. 2F is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The decree introduces the list as terms for talking about language: the point is that teachers (and pupils) can name certain phenomena in language situations with a rich context. The cell names two terms: *trema* (diaeresis, the two dots on a vowel) and *accent* (accent mark). It lists them as terms: it states no rule for when either mark is written and gives no examples. The pack's examples, all correct Dutch: with a diaeresis, *knieën* (knees), *ideeën* (ideas), *reëel* (real), *egoïstisch* (selfish); with an accent, *café* (café), *crème* (cream), *één* (one, the number).

### Common mistakes

A child calls every mark above a letter an accent, also the diaeresis. A child takes the Dutch diaeresis for the German umlaut and thinks that it changes the sound of the vowel, where in Dutch it marks the start of a new syllable. A child leaves the marks out when writing because the word can be read without them, for example writes the wrong *knieen* for *knieën*.
