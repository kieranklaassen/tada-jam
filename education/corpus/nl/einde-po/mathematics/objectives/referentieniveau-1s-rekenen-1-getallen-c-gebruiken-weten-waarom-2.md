## Official wording

decimale getallen als toepassing van (tiendelige) maatverfijning

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 2, 1 Getallen, niveau 1S, C Gebruiken / Weten waarom / 2.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

decimal numbers as an application of (tenfold) refinement of measures (*maatverfijning*, dividing a unit into smaller units)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 measures a table that is longer than 1 metre and shorter than 2 metres, uses the tenths of a metre and then the tenths of those, and writes 1.37 m: 1 metre, 3 tenths and 7 hundredths. The child explains that each further decimal place stands for a unit ten times as small (metre, decimetre, centimetre), and that this is why a more exact measurement has more decimals.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1S for arithmetic, domain Getallen (numbers), under *weten waarom* (understanding why). 1S is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. It stands under sub-heading C (using). The statement is a fragment without a verb: read with its kind, it concerns understanding why decimal numbers follow from dividing a unit of measure into ten smaller units, again and again. The word "tenfold" (*tiendelige*) stands in brackets. The statement names no quantities, no units and no number of decimals, and it sets no number range. Measures that are not divided in tens, such as hours and minutes, are not mentioned.

### Common mistakes

A child reads the decimal part as a count of a smaller unit and takes 1.5 m for 1 metre and 5 centimetres. A child thinks more decimals make a larger number and calls 1.37 larger than 1.4. A child carries the idea over to time and reads 1.30 hours as 1 hour and 30 minutes.
