## Official wording

are, hectare

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit referentieniveaus Nederlandse taal en rekenen, bijlage 2, 3 Meten en Meetkunde, niveau 1S, A Notatie, taal en betekenis / Paraat hebben / 1.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

are (*are*, a unit of area), hectare (*hectare*)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 says at once that an are is the area of a square of 10 m by 10 m, which is 100 m², and that a hectare is the area of a square of 100 m by 100 m, which is 10 000 m². The child also says at once that a hectare is 100 are. On a playground the child paces out a square of 10 m by 10 m to show one are.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1S for arithmetic, domain *Meten en Meetkunde* (measuring and geometry), under *paraat hebben* (knowing by heart, having ready). It stands in part A, *Notatie, taal en betekenis* (notation, language and meaning). 1S is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The statement names two units of area, the are and the hectare, and no others. It prints only the two names: no abbreviation, no size and no relation. The sizes (an are is 100 m², a hectare is 100 are) are not printed here; the relation 1 km² = 1 000 000 m² = 100 ha is a separate statement in part B under *paraat hebben*. The decree prints this as a statement of its own, apart from the other statements on measures under the same heading. Standing under knowing by heart, it asks for the two units as ready knowledge; it does not ask for an explanation.

### Common mistakes

A child takes the hectare for a length of 100 m, because *hecto-* means a hundred. A child says that a hectare is 100 m² or 1000 m². A child knows the hectare from daily life, has never met the are, and so cannot say that a hectare is 100 are.
