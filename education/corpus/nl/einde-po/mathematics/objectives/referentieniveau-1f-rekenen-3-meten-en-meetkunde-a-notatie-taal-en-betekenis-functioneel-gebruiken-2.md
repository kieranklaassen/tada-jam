## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

different units of time (hour, minute, second, century, year, month)

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 picks the unit that fits what is being told: seconds for a sprint across the playground, minutes for the walk to school, hours for a school day, months until a birthday, years for an age, and centuries for how long ago a castle was built. The child reads and says these units in a recipe, on a departure board at a station or in a history text, and says which of two units is the longer one.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1F for arithmetic, domain *Meten en Meetkunde* (measuring and geometry), under *functioneel gebruiken* (using in practical situations). It stands in part A, *Notatie, taal en betekenis* (notation, language and meaning). 1F is the level every pupil should reach at the end of primary school. The brackets name six units of time: hour, minute, second, century, year and month. Week and day are not in these brackets; they are named in the statement on time in part B, under *functioneel gebruiken*. The fragment has no verb; it stands under using in practical situations in the part on notation, language and meaning, so using these units and their names in practical situations is presumably meant. It states no relations between the units (such as how many minutes are in an hour) and no number range.

### Common mistakes

A child chooses a unit that does not fit, such as giving the length of a football match in seconds. A child has no sense of how long a century is and places something of 100 years ago and something of 1000 years ago equally far back. A child reads 1.5 hours as 1 hour and 5 minutes, as if time were written in tens.
