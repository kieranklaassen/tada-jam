## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 writes the units of length, of weight and of capacity in three rows, from kilo- down to milli-, and shows that the same prefixes stand at the same places in each row. The child explains that one step along a row is a factor of 10 for metres, grams and litres alike. The child then explains why the step is 100 for units of area and 1000 for units of volume: an area has a length and a width, and a volume also has a height.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1S for arithmetic, domain *Meten en Meetkunde* (measuring and geometry), under *weten waarom* (understanding why). It stands in part B, *Met elkaar in verband brengen* (relating things to each other). 1S is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The fragment names the structure and the coherence of the metric system and nothing else: no units, no prefixes and no relations. The source prints it in a shortened form, without the linking words (*structuur en samenhang metrieke stelsel*); the gloss supplies "of the". It has no verb; under this heading, understanding and explaining how the system is built and how its parts belong together is presumably meant. The decree prints "decimal structure of the metric system" as a separate statement directly before this one, under the same heading.

### Common mistakes

A child learns length, weight and capacity as three separate lists and does not see that the prefixes mean the same in each. A child knows single relations and cannot work out one that was not learned, such as the centilitres in a litre from the centimetres in a metre. A child uses the step of 10 for area and volume too.
