## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 folds a cut-out figure along a line to test whether the two halves cover each other, and says why the line is or is not a line of symmetry. The child says that a square has 4 lines of symmetry and shows each by folding. The child folds a rectangle that is not a square along its diagonal, sees that the halves do not cover each other, and explains why.

### Limits

This is a statement of reference level 1S for arithmetic, domain *Meten en Meetkunde* (measuring and geometry), under *weten waarom* (understanding why). It stands in part A, *Notatie, taal en betekenis* (notation, language and meaning). 1S is the higher level that many pupils reach at the end of primary school, above the basic level 1F. The statement asks for reasoning about symmetrical figures. It names no figures, no kind of symmetry (line symmetry or rotational symmetry) and no number of lines of symmetry. It stands under understanding why: the child gives reasons and does not only point out a line.

### Common mistakes

A child takes every line that divides a figure into two equal halves for a line of symmetry, such as the diagonal of a rectangle. A child looks only for a vertical line and misses horizontal or slanted ones. A child calls a parallelogram symmetrical in a line because it looks regular.
