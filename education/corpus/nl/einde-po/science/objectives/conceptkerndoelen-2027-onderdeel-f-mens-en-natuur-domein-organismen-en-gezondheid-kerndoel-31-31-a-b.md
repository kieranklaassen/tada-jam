## Official wording

verbanden leggen tussen de bouw van planten en mensen en hun vorm en functie;

Source: Ministerie van Onderwijs, Cultuur en Wetenschap (via internetconsultatie.nl), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen overige leergebieden (consultation draft), page 13.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

making connections between the structure (*bouw*, how something is built) of plants and humans and their form and function;

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 digs up a weed and says what the shape of each part has to do with its work: the branched roots hold the plant in the soil and take up water, the stem carries the water up, the flat leaves catch light. The child stands a stalk of celery in coloured water and sees the colour rise through the stalk. On the child's own body the child bends the elbow, which works like a hinge, turns the shoulder, which moves in many directions, and compares in a mirror the sharp front teeth for biting with the broad back teeth for grinding.

### Limits

This is an item under goal sentence 31 A, which says that the pupil shows insight into organisms; 31 A sits under core goal 31. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. The item names plants and humans; animals are not named in it. It names no part of a plant or of the body: no organ, tissue or cell is named, and no quantity. It sets no level of detail. "Their" (*hun*) form and function can be read as those of plants and humans or as those of their parts; the gloss leaves this as open as the Dutch.

### Common mistakes

Many children think a plant takes its food from the soil through its roots; the roots take up water and minerals, and the plant makes its food in the green leaves with light. A child names the parts and says what each does, and leaves out what the shape has to do with it. Many children think of bones as dead, dry material; in a living body bone is living tissue that grows.
