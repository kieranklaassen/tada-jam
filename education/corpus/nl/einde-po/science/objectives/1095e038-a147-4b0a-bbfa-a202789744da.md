## Official wording

De leerlingen leren over de bouw van planten, dieren en mensen en over de vorm en functie van hun onderdelen.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen WPO, bijlage, kerndoel 41.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

The pupils learn about the structure (*bouw*, how something is built) of plants, animals and humans and about the form and function of their parts.

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 digs up a weed and points to the roots, the stem, the leaves and the flower, and says that the roots hold the plant in the soil and take up water, and that the green leaves make the plant's food with light. The child bends an arm, feels the elbow joint and the muscle of the upper arm, and says that the bones give support and that a muscle moves a bone by pulling on it. The child compares the wide flat bill of a duck with the short thick beak of a sparrow and says which fits sieving food out of water and which fits cracking seeds.

### Limits

This 2006 goal is still in force. It is the second of seven goals under the heading *Natuur en techniek* (nature and *techniek*, a word read as technology or as engineering). The goal names three groups (plants, animals and humans) and two things about their parts: form and function. It names no part and no organ, and it sets no level of detail, also not for the human body.

### Common mistakes

Many children think that a plant takes its food from the soil through its roots; a plant makes its food in its green parts with light, and the roots take up water and minerals. A child names a part and cannot say what it does, or does not link its shape to its task. Many children think of the body as separate parts and do not connect them, for example the lungs that take in oxygen and the blood that carries it to the muscles.
