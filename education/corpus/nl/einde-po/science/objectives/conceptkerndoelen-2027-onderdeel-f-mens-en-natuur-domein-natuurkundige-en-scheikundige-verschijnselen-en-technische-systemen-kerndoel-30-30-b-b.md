benoemen van veel voorkomende stoffen en hun onderscheidende eigenschappen en gevaren;

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

naming common substances (*stoffen*; *stof* is read as substance or as material) and their distinguishing properties and dangers;

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 names substances it meets every day, such as water, table salt, sugar, iron, aluminium and vinegar, and says how to tell two that look alike apart: iron and aluminium are both grey metals, a magnet attracts iron and not aluminium, and a piece of aluminium is much lighter than a piece of iron of the same size. The child points to the hazard symbol on a closed bottle of cleaning product or methylated spirit and says what it warns of, such as "catches fire easily" or "burns the skin".

### Limits

This is an item under goal sentence 30 B, which says that the pupil shows insight into and experiments with substances and the changes they can undergo; 30 B sits under core goal 30. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. "Common" bounds the substances; the item names none, and no number of them. It names no property and no danger. It asks for naming dangers; it states no rules for handling substances and no safety condition, and hazard symbols are not mentioned in it.

### Common mistakes

Many children think that "chemical" substances are dangerous and "natural" ones are safe. A child assumes that a clear liquid without a smell is water. A child tells substances apart by colour alone, so two white powders or two clear liquids are called the same.
