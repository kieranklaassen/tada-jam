## Official wording

benoemen van soortnamen van planten en dieren in verschillende soorten ecosystemen en deze planten en dieren indelen in een taxonomie;

Source: Ministerie van Onderwijs, Cultuur en Wetenschap (via internetconsultatie.nl), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen overige leergebieden (consultation draft), page 14.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

naming species names (*soortnamen*; *soort* means both species and kind) of plants and animals in different kinds (*soorten*) of ecosystems and classifying these plants and animals in a taxonomy;

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

With an identification chart a child of 11 or 12 names what is found in a wood and in a ditch by its own name: blackbird and great tit, not "bird"; oak and beech, not "tree". The child sorts cards of the animals into groups by features of the body: animals with and without a backbone, then mammals, birds, fish, amphibians and reptiles, and insects with six legs apart from spiders with eight.

### Limits

This is an item under goal sentence 32 C, which says that the pupil explores different ecosystems and explains interactions within the system; 32 C sits under core goal 32. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. The item has two parts: naming and classifying. *Soortnamen* can be read as names of species or as names of kinds, and the item does not say whether Dutch names or scientific names are meant; in *soorten ecosystemen* the same word means kinds of ecosystems. The item names plants and animals; fungi are not named in it. It names no species, no number of names and no ecosystem. It says "a taxonomy" and does not say which groups or levels the taxonomy has.

### Common mistakes

A child sorts by where an animal lives or how it looks, so a whale or a dolphin is put with the fish and a bat with the birds; all three are mammals. A child counts a spider as an insect; an insect has six legs and a spider eight. A child gives the name of a group, such as "bird" or "tree", where the name of the species is asked.
