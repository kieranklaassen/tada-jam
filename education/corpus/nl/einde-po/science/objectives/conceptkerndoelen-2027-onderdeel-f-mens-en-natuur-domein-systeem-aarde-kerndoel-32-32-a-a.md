## Official wording

evalueren hoe planeten in ons zonnestelsel voldoen aan voorwaarden voor leven;

Source: Ministerie van Onderwijs, Cultuur en Wetenschap (via internetconsultatie.nl), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen overige leergebieden (consultation draft), page 13.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

evaluating how planets in our solar system meet conditions for life;

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 makes a table of the eight planets with a column for each condition the child looks at, such as liquid water, a temperature that is not too hot and not too cold, and an atmosphere. The child fills the table in from a book and judges each planet: Venus is far too hot, Mars is cold and has very thin air, Jupiter has no solid ground, and the earth is the only planet known to have life.

### Limits

This is an item under goal sentence 32 A, which says that the pupil shows insight into and explores changes to the earth's surface through natural phenomena and human action; 32 A sits under core goal 32. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. The item is about the planets and not about the earth's surface; the draft prints it under 32 A. The verb is evaluating (*evalueren*). The item does not name the conditions for life, the planets or their number, and it names no quantity or unit. It speaks of planets in our solar system: moons and planets of other stars are not named.

### Common mistakes

Many children think a planet is hotter the closer it is to the sun; Venus is hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the sun, because the thick atmosphere of Venus holds the heat. A child counts the sun, the moon or Pluto among the planets; the solar system has eight planets, and Pluto has been classed as a dwarf planet since 2006. A child takes "conditions for life" to mean whether people could live there, and answers yes or no without looking at each condition.
