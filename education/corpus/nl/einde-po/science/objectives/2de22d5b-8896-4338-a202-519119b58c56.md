## Official wording

De leerlingen leren in de eigen omgeving veel voorkomende planten en dieren onderscheiden en benoemen en leren hoe ze functioneren in hun leefomgeving.

Source: Rijksoverheid (Basiswettenbestand, KOOP), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen WPO, bijlage, kerndoel 40.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

The pupils learn to distinguish and name plants and animals that are common in their own surroundings, and learn how they function in their living environment (*leefomgeving*, the surroundings where they live).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 walks around the school grounds or a park, looks at the leaves and the bark of a tree and names it as an oak or a birch, and names a blackbird, an earthworm and a woodlouse. The child tells kinds that look alike apart by pointing to a feature, such as the shape of a leaf or the colour of a beak. The child says how one of them lives there: the woodlouse stays in damp places under stones and wood and eats rotting plant material, and the blackbird pulls earthworms out of the grass.

### Limits

This 2006 goal is still in force. It is the first of seven goals under the heading *Natuur en techniek* (nature and *techniek*, a word read as technology or as engineering). The goal bounds the plants and animals to those that are common in the pupils' own surroundings; it names no species and no number of species. It does not spell out what "how they function in their living environment" covers, and it gives no examples. In the second half, "they" and "their" most naturally point to the plants and animals; the Dutch is as open as the gloss.

### Common mistakes

Many children use the word animal only for mammals with four legs, and do not count insects, worms, birds or fish as animals. Many children do not count a tree or grass as a plant. A child calls every small crawling animal an insect, including spiders and woodlice, which are not insects.
