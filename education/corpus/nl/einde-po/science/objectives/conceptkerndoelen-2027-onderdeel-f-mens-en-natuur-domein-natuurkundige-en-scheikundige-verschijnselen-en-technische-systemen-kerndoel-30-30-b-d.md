## Official wording

experimenteren met natuurkundige veranderingen van stoffen;

Source: Ministerie van Onderwijs, Cultuur en Wetenschap (via internetconsultatie.nl), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen overige leergebieden (consultation draft), page 12.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

experimenting with physical (*natuurkundige*, of physics) changes of substances (*stoffen*; *stof* is read as substance or as material);

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 lets an ice cube melt in a closed jar, weighs the jar before and after, and finds the same weight. The child leaves a saucer of salt water on the windowsill for some days: the water evaporates and the salt stays behind as crystals. The child takes a cold glass out of the refrigerator and sees drops form on the outside from the water vapour in the air. Each time the child says that the substance has changed in form and is still the same substance.

### Limits

This is an item under goal sentence 30 B, which says that the pupil shows insight into and experiments with substances and the changes they can undergo; 30 B sits under core goal 30. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. The item names no particular physical change and no particular substance; melting, evaporating and condensing, and ice, salt water and water vapour, are the pack's examples. It states no temperature, no unit and no instrument, and it states no safety condition, also not for heating. Chemical changes are in item 30 B e.

### Common mistakes

Many children think that water that evaporates no longer exists. Many children think that the drops on a cold glass have come through the glass; they come from water vapour in the air. A child expects ice to weigh more or less than the water it melts into.
