## Official wording

De leerling toont inzicht in de waterkringloop, weer en klimaat en verkent deze in de eigen leefomgeving.

Source: Ministerie van Onderwijs, Cultuur en Wetenschap (via internetconsultatie.nl), Besluit vernieuwde kerndoelen overige leergebieden (consultation draft), page 14.

## English gloss

_The pack's gloss, not an official translation._

The pupil shows insight into the water cycle, weather and climate and explores these in the pupil's own living environment (*eigen leefomgeving*, the surroundings in which the pupil lives).

## Design notes

_The pack's inference, not the official text._

### In a child's hands

A child of 11 or 12 sets up a rain gauge and a thermometer outside, reads them each day for some weeks and notes the results in a table. After rain the child draws a chalk line around a puddle and sees the puddle shrink as the water evaporates. The child compares the measurements with the long-term averages for that month in the Netherlands and says what belongs to the weather of those weeks and what to the climate.

### Limits

This is a goal sentence (*doelzin*, the sentence printed under a core goal with a capital letter) under core goal 32, which is about the system earth. This is a draft goal, not yet in force. Its items 32 B a to 32 B e name the characteristics and distribution of climates on earth; how the water cycle is put together; analysing the weather from six named elements; the frequency and duration of extreme weather; and connections between climate change, natural processes and human action. The sentence has two parts: showing insight, and exploring in the pupil's own living environment. It does not say what that environment is (home, school, a wider region or all of these). The sentence names no quantity.

### Common mistakes

Many children mix up weather and climate, and take one cold week as a sign about the climate; weather is the state of the air at a place over hours or days, and climate is the average weather over a long period, usually thirty years. Many children think the water of a dried-up puddle has all gone into the ground, or no longer exists, and leave out that it has evaporated into the air.
