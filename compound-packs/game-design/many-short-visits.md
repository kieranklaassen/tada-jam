---
title: A learning game is built for many short visits spread over weeks, never to be finished or mastered in one sitting
applies_when:
  - planning a learning game for a given age
  - deciding how long a session of a game is and what a child does on a return visit
  - planning practice of number facts or letter sounds without timers
tags: [learning-games, spacing, sessions, practice, fluency]
---

Do:

- A visit of a few minutes is whole: something is begun and finished.
- A return visit meets the same skill again in a slightly different form.
- Once a mechanic is learned, kinds of problem are mixed, so the child has to decide what kind each one is.
- Fluency comes from many short, varied, untimed meetings with the same facts, and from play that goes more smoothly when the fact is known.
- The child can come back as often as they like and leave at any moment.

Do not:

- The game is not designed around long sessions, and minutes played are not counted as success. Total time did not predict learning.
- The game does not arrange the return. Nothing is offered for coming back and nothing is lost by staying away (`the-line-between-depth-and-manipulation.md`).
- No clock is used to build speed.

Exception: none.

Evidence: games played in one session taught no more than ordinary lessons (g 0.08), and games played over several sessions did (g 0.44), while total duration was unrelated to the effect [V, Clark et al. 2016]. In a later review of apps for ages 3 to 9, the number of sessions did not predict the effect [V, Gilbert's summary of Kim et al. 2021 and its replication], so not every review supports spacing. Timed practice does build fact fluency in the short term [S]; the jam rules it out, and the cost is set out in `research/open-questions.md`. Detail: `research/learning-games-that-work.md`, summary rule 9, sections 2 and 8.
