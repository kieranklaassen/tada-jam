---
title: Depth comes from a small set of parts that combine, never from more authored content, and a game names what a child can do on day 15 that they could not on day 1
applies_when:
  - designing a new game for the jam
  - deciding how much content, how many levels or how many objects a game needs
  - turning a lab demo into a real game
  - reviewing a game plan for whether children will come back to it for weeks
tags: [depth, replay, combinations, systems, week-three]
---

A game that lasts weeks is a few objects, each with a few properties, where any two do something together. The work goes into the table of interactions, not into more scenes, levels or art.

Do:

- The plan holds a grid of objects by actions (6 by 5 is enough to start), and every cell gives a result that looks and sounds different.
- The plan answers in one line: on day 15, what can the child do, find or make that they could not on day 1? Good answers are new combinations, a skill the child keeps getting better at with the gain visible in the world, secrets that work every time, and things of the child's own making.
- The same pieces can make things, not only solve things.
- The rules stay the same, so what a child discovers stays true.

Do not:

- "More levels", "more items" and "the same thing again" do not answer the day-15 question. A level or story seen once loses most of its value when repeated.
- Random variety is not counted as depth.

Exception: for ages 2 to 4, day 15 may look almost like day 1, with the child faster, surer and trying one new thing (`ages-2-to-4.md`).

Evidence: Cook on activities that keep their value when repeated and those that do not [V]; Zoombinis gets 625 individuals from four attributes of five values [V]. The grid and the day-15 question are proposals built on those [I], and no study tracks how long children keep returning to such games. This extends "Depth engine" in `CONCEPTS.md`.
