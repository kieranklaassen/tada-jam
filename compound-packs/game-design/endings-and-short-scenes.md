---
title: Every play cycle ends when the child ends it, and a short animated scene is its twist or its ending, never an interruption
applies_when:
  - adding an ending, a cutscene or a celebration
  - adding a short animation or movie to a game
  - deciding what happens after a round, a task or a build is finished
  - designing the shape of one play-through of a game
tags: [endings, cutscenes, animation, story, play-cycle, autoplay]
---

A cycle has four beats: an idea is introduced, developed, given a twist, and concluded. The conclusion is a finished scene that stays as long as the child likes. Beginning again is an obvious act the child performs in the scene.

Do:

- After the ending, if the child does nothing, nothing new starts.
- A scene plays as the consequence of what the child just did, as a secret that one combination always produces, or as the ending.
- A scene lasts 4 to 10 seconds, stars what the child made (the customer bites the pizza with exactly these toppings), and gives way to any touch.
- A scene is a list of timed beats over six to ten poses joined by springs, filled in from the state of play, so it differs each time.
- In a learning game the scene acts out what the child just made: the word, the sum, the bridge.

Do not:

- No automatic next round and no countdown to one.
- No scene before the action, and none played only sometimes for the same action.
- No fixed film, and no story that competes with the task.

Exception: open pretend play has no ending and has a calm way to tidy up. An ending may hold for 10 to 25 seconds, and for ages 9 to 12 a replay of the child's own machine for 30 to 45.

Evidence: automatic advance with no pause was found in 46% of the apps most used by children aged 3 to 5 [V, Radesky et al. 2022]. The four beats are Hayashida's structure for Mario levels [V]. Children grow impatient with what they cannot interrupt [V, Sesame Workshop]. The lengths and the pose method are inference [I].
