---
title: Characters have fixed opinions of their own, and their reactions to what the child did are the game's feedback
applies_when:
  - designing a new game for the jam
  - adding a character, a customer or a creature to a game
  - deciding how a game shows a child the result of what they did
  - a game feels flat or emotionally empty
tags: [characters, reactions, feedback, emotion, opinions]
---

The child's work is done for someone, or to someone, who has tastes. That character's face, body and voice show what they think of the result. The reaction is the feedback, and nothing else rates the result.

Do:

- Each character has one want that is always visible, and a few likes and dislikes that never change, so a child can learn them and test them on purpose.
- A dislike is as good to watch as a like. Disgust, surprise and fright are outcomes worth causing.
- A reaction starts within a few hundred milliseconds, can be read from across a room, and is to the exact thing the child made: these toppings, this haircut.

Do not:

- A character's feelings are never about the child: no disappointment aimed at the child, no thanks or praise as payment, no remark about the child stopping, staying, leaving or coming back.
- A want is about something in the scene (an empty plate) and may be shown to the child as the jam's "Scene want" describes. A waiting character never complains of being ignored.

Exception: a game with no character, where the material itself answers, says in its plan what gives the feedback.

Evidence: reviews describe the eaters in Toca Kitchen and the clients in Toca Hair Salon, whose faces are the only feedback [S]. The MDA paper's example for ages 3 to 7 tunes a game for a character's surprise, fear and anticipation, not for winning [V]. The owner marked five of the six demos built around a reacting customer or patient "build" (`lab/arcade/RATINGS.json`). Characters who press children to keep playing were found in 25% of the apps young children use most [V, Radesky et al. 2022].
