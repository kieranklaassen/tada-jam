---
title: An error shows as a consequence in the world that says where and why, and nothing gives a verdict
applies_when:
  - deciding what happens when a child gets something wrong
  - adding feedback for a right or wrong answer in a learning game
  - a child can get through a game by tapping everything until it works
tags: [learning-games, feedback, errors, consequences, control-of-error]
---

The jam already rules out ticks, crosses and wrong sounds ("Control of error" in `CONCEPTS.md`). This rule adds what makes a consequence readable, and why a silent retry is not enough. The game runs the child's answer and shows what it does.

Do:

- The consequence follows from the action by the world's own logic. A cake that is too salty makes the creature pull a face and reach for water.
- It shows where and why. The bridge breaks at the weak span. The rod is short by a gap the child can see.
- The state stays. The child changes one thing and tries again.
- It is as interesting as success, and it is about the thing, never about the child.
- Success is also a consequence: the monster eats, the boat floats.
- Feedback is fullest when a task is new and thins once the child can do it.

Do not:

- No buzzer, no cross, no sad face turned to the child, no "try again", no reset, no lost piece.
- No wrong try that costs nothing and tells nothing. When a wrong try gives no information, children stop reasoning and tap everything until something works.
- No cheer in place of showing why.

Exception: for the youngest, a material may simply not go together wrongly (a piece that only fits its own place).

Evidence: preschoolers given feedback that explained made fewer errors than those given cheering sounds, and "Great job!" added nothing [V via Outhwaite et al. 2023, reporting Callaghan and Reich 2021]. Practice with no feedback taught nothing in a fraction game where practice with feedback taught a lot [V, Fazio et al. 2016]. The list of what makes a consequence readable is inference [I].
