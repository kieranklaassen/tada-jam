---
title: A game for ages 9 to 12 has real difficulty, a system that behaves truly and more than one solution, and nothing that age rejects as babyish
applies_when:
  - designing a game for children aged 9 to 12
  - planning a learning game for a given age when the band reaches 9 or older
  - choosing the look, humor or characters for older children
  - deciding how much help or hinting an older child's game gives
tags: [ages-9-12, difficulty, systems, puzzles, tone, older-children]
---

Do:

- The system is real and consistent: a kit of physical parts, a small rule language, a model that is true wherever it claims to be science.
- Any working solution stands. A better one is visibly better in the world: taller, smoother, fewer parts.
- Failure is large, funny and free. The bridge sags and the truck slides off, nothing is lost, and one tap tries again.
- What the child builds stays and is worth showing to the person in the room: a machine left running, a replay.
- Humor is dry, a little absurd and not explained. Characters have flaws. Tools look like tools.
- The jam's idle guidance shows what can be touched or one possible move, never a solution. Further help is something the child goes and gets. Showing a neat way after the child's own attempt is covered in `guided-discovery.md`.

Do not:

- Nothing this age reads as babyish: praise for trivial actions, a mascot that explains, no way of being wrong, one right answer, pastel baby styling, animations that cannot be skipped, forced tutorials, school exercises with a game theme added.
- No competition and no stored best.

Exception: difficulty comes with a way back in. Children in grades 4 to 6 said they stopped when a game was too hard.

Open: whether a reading on the object itself (a height, a part count) may be shown, and whether written notation may be required, are the owner's decisions (`research/open-questions.md`, items 2 and 5).

Evidence: Papert on children calling something fun because it is hard [V]. In Olson's survey of 1,254 pupils aged 12 to 14, the top edge of this band, challenge, creativity and curiosity were leading motives [V]. Fifth-graders were moved more by challenge than by competition [V, Greenberg as cited by Olson]. The babyish list is inference and has not been checked with children [I].
