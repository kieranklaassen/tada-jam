# Open questions for the owner

These are decisions the research raises that belong to the owner. None of them is written as a rule in this pack. Each rule that touches one says so and points here. When the owner decides, the answer becomes a rule file at the top level of the pack (or a change to an existing one), and the item is removed from this file.

Evidence marks are the ones used in the two research files: [V] read in the source, [S] from a secondary summary, [I] inference.

On 2026-10-02 the owner decided that numerals and mathematics symbols may appear from about age 6 and that games for the youngest stay free of words and numerals. The rule that follows is in `fade-to-school-symbols.md`: a game whose age band starts at 6 or above may show numerals and mathematics symbols, each laid on or beside the quantity it stands for; a game whose band starts below 6 shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, optional or not; and no game shows a letter or a written word at any age. That decision closes none of the items below. Each item ends with what is done until he decides.

## Decisions

### 1. Bring back impact feedback while keeping approval feedback out

- **Where it comes from.** Rule 7 of `lab/arcade/GENTLE.md` bans, in one list, screen shake, the brief pause on impact, confetti, flashes, floating words and fanfares, and asks for low volumes with gaps of quiet. The owner then rated the calm demos "No" for being "too slow or too quiet".
- **What the research says.** The first research file reads the cause as this: calm was reached by turning down the size of the response, when the thing to remove was only the pressure [I]. It proposes splitting the list. Squash and stretch, anticipation, a camera kick in proportion to mass, a pause of a frame or two on a heavy landing, and dust where things hit say something about weight. Confetti, fanfares, floating words, flashes and stars say something about approval. Sources: Swink on polish [V]; Eiserloh on scaling camera shake so only large events move it [V]; Jonasson and Purho and Nijman, known from written notes only [S]. The split itself is an inference, and no source tests it with children.
- **A caution.** Nine minutes of a fast cartoon lowered four-year-olds' executive function straight afterwards [S, Lillard and Peterson 2011]. That finding is about pace imposed on a child who is watching, not about a response the child causes.
- **Options.** (a) Keep the ban as written and make responses bigger only through chains of consequences and sound. (b) Allow the weight effects (camera kick and impact pause, scaled so only heavy things trigger them) and keep the approval effects banned. (c) Allow them only above a certain age band.
- **Until decided.** Squash, stretch, anticipation and follow-through are already required by the jam's quality bar. Camera shake and impact pause are not used, in gentle games or in the learning games: the response is carried by chains of consequences, sound and squash. See `touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md`.

### 2. A reading shown on the thing itself, for ages 9 to 12

- **The question.** May a game for ages 9 to 12 show a property of the child's current object as a reading on the object: a height against a ruler, a count of parts on the workbench, a step count for a program?
- **What the research says.** Older children want to make a working thing better for their own satisfaction, and a better solution has to be visibly better [I]. The first research file calls such a reading an instrument: it describes the current object. It becomes a score when the game stores a best, sets a target, or celebrates a lower number. The file states that this is a judgment for the owner. Sources for the wish to optimize: Olson [V]; Zachtronics, where any working machine counts [S].
- **Against it.** `AGENTS.md` bans counters dangled at the child. The numeral itself is no longer the obstacle: a game whose band starts at 6 or above may show a numeral laid on or beside the quantity it stands for. What is open is whether a reading is an instrument or a counter.
- **Options.** (a) No readings; better is shown only by how the thing looks and behaves. (b) Readings allowed for ages 9 to 12 under three conditions: nothing stores a best, nothing sets a target, nothing reacts to the number. (c) Readings allowed only where the child picks up a measuring tool and applies it.
- **Until decided.** Option (a): no readings. Better is shown by how the thing looks and behaves.

### 3. A single letter that says its sound, for ages 4 to 6

- **The question.** May a single letter appear on the kid side for ages 4 to 6, as an object with a voice, when the jam's rule is no letter and no written word on the kid side at any age?
- **What the research says.** Training children to hear the sounds in words improved reading more when letters were used (d 0.67 against 0.38) [S, Ehri et al. 2001]. Kindergartners who built their own spellings learned to read more words than children trained on sounds alone [S, Ouellette and Sénéchal 2008]. The second research file concludes that a ban on letters across the whole band would leave the strongest effect unused, and that the moment to bring letters in is when a child is picking out first sounds and blending [I]. On the other side, eight months of sound games with no letters still improved reading through grade 2 [S, Lundberg et al. 1988], so games without letters are worth making either way.
- **Options.** (a) No letters at 4 to 6; reading games are sound play only. (b) A single letter may appear as an object that says its sound when touched or traced, never as a word to read. The wordless check has no exception for a letter on the kid side, so this would need one. (c) Letters only at the top of the band, after the child has shown reliable sorting by first sound.
- **Until decided.** Option (a): no letters. The owner's decision of 2026-10-02 names numerals and mathematics symbols only.
- **Note.** All of this evidence is marked [S]. Letter and sound map far more regularly in Dutch than in English, so letter games need a content pack for each language [I].

### 4. How the youngest reading games show letters at all

- **The question.** If letters are allowed at some age, in what form do they first appear, and what do games for ages 2 to 4 show?
- **What the research says.** For ages 2 to 4: no letters; listening, rhyme, syllables and first sounds need none [S, Lundberg et al. 1988; I for the age split]. After that the second research file lists four forms, in the order they would arrive [I, drawing on Montessori materials described from secondary sources]: a letter that rides on a creature as its badge once the child sorts by first sound reliably; a letter traced with the finger while it says its sound; letters on the tiles a child slides together to blend a word; loose letters the child builds words from before being asked to read any.
- **A known weak form.** Dragging letters into matching outlines lets a child match shapes without thinking about sounds [I, from the description of Endless Alphabet].
- **Options.** (a) Decide one first form for all reading games. (b) Allow all four in the order above. (c) Leave letters out of the jam's reading games and treat them as a matter for the grown-up.
- **Until decided.** No letter and no written word appears on the kid side in any band, ages 9 to 12 included. Reading and language games are sound play for the two younger bands, and the oldest band has none, because every school goal in reading for that age is about words, sentences or texts.

### 5. Written notation that is required, not optional, for ages 9 to 12; and a note for the grown-up

- **The question.** May a symbol stand alone in a game for ages 9 to 12, so that the child has to read numerals or fraction notation in order to play? May units be shown? And may the grown-up corner hold a short note on what to talk about?
- **What the research says.** Players who solved 140 equations in a game did not improve on paper equations in one study [V, Long and Aleven 2014], though a larger trial later found gains [V, Decker-Woodrow et al. 2023]. The second research file concludes that each strand for this band should end in the notation the child sees at school, and says this age is insulted when honest notation is missing [I]. Games taught about twice as much when paired with other teaching [V, Wouters et al. 2013], and another meta-analysis found no difference [V, Clark et al. 2016].
- **Against it.** The jam's rule lets a game whose band starts at 6 or above show numerals and mathematics symbols only laid on or beside the quantity each stands for, so notation is now seen at 9 to 12 and play does not depend on reading it. A unit abbreviation is a word, and no written word is shown at any age. The grown-up corner in `AGENTS.md` (Δ4) is described as holding settings a parent tunes.
- **Options.** (a) Notation is shown only on or beside its quantity, as the rule now has it; the school form with units lives off the kid side. (b) For bands whose youngest age is 9 or more, a symbol may stand alone and be required, as a recorded exception to the wordless rule. (c) The grown-up corner may hold a note with the school form and what to talk about, with no score, log or verdict.
- **Until decided.** Option (a): no symbol stands alone and no unit is shown. The question of the note is unchanged.

## Where the evidence does not support the jam's hard constraints

The jam's constraints stand. These are the places where the research does not back them, with what the research files say about getting the benefit another way.

### Praise looks close to neutral for young children

- In a study of 23 evaluated math apps, every highly effective one had both explanatory and motivational feedback, and the authors say praise "was not a hindrance" [V, Outhwaite et al. 2023]. No app had explanation without praise, so the sample cannot say whether praise is needed.
- Positive verbal feedback raised later interest overall (d 0.33) but not in children (d 0.11, not significant) [V, Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999].
- The reward experiment that underlies the ban is about promised rewards. Children given a surprise award did not lose interest [V, Lepper 1973].
- Mindset theory is contested and is not used to justify anything in this pack [S].
- **By other means.** The explanation does the work, so removing praise costs little as long as the consequence is clear and satisfying [I]. A character delighted by the pizza is a consequence inside the fiction and is acceptable under both readings.

### Points raise scores on the drilled skill, at a later cost to interest

- Points and badges added to school-like tasks gave g 0.53 on tests of the drilled skill, against 0.25 for more elaborate games; the difference was not significant [V, Clark et al. 2016]. Gamification shows short-term cognitive gains (g 0.49) with unstable effects on motivation [S, Sailer and Homner 2020].
- Those studies do not measure interest afterwards. Expected rewards lower later free-choice interest, more in children [V, Deci et al. 1999]; stars for redoing problems lowered learning [V, Long and Aleven 2014]; a course with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower motivation and exam scores [S, Hanus and Fox 2015].
- **By other means.** Simple repeated practice does work for a drilled skill. The practice is kept and the points are left out: the same facts met many times inside play that goes more smoothly when the fact is known [I].

### Timed practice builds short-term fluency

- Timed practice does build fact fluency in the short term, and the difference from untimed practice fades [S]. The common claim that timed tests cause math anxiety rests on weak evidence [S]. Under time pressure, anxious children do worse [S].
- So speed drills are not shown to be harmful in general. The jam rules them out, and the cost is some fluency.
- **By other means.** Many short, varied, untimed encounters spread over weeks, which the spacing evidence supports [V, Clark et al. 2016], and games in which knowing the fact makes play smoother without a clock [I]. See `many-short-visits.md`.

### The principle that the mechanic is the skill rests on thin evidence

- The one direct experiment had 58 and 16 children from one school, the learning advantage appeared only on a delayed test, and a teacher-led session was part of every condition [V, Habgood and Ainsworth 2011].
- A small study with adults went the other way [V, Nidd and Muldner]. A meta-analysis that coded 57 studies found no advantage for games coded as integrated [V, Clark et al. 2016].
- The cleanest result is about motivation: given a free choice, children chose the integrated game about seven to one [V].
- **By other means.** The second research file keeps the principle on the attention account: children learn what the task makes them look at and think about [S, Cutting and Iacovides 2022; I for the reading]. That gives the questions in `the-mechanic-is-the-school-skill.md`, and it also says when integration fails, which is when the game adds demands that compete with the idea.

### Free discovery is not supported for young children

- This is not a jam constraint, but an open-ended toy can be mistaken for a way of teaching. Discovery without help loses to explicit teaching (d -0.38) [S, Alfieri et al. 2011], and trying before being taught did not help grades 2 to 5 [V, Sinha and Kapur 2021].
- **By other means.** In a wordless game, teaching means a character shows it once. See `guided-discovery.md`.
