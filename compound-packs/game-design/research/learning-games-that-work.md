# Learning games that work

What the evidence says makes a learning game both fun and effective, and which existing games show how. Written for a small studio designing about 20 touch-screen learning games for ages 2 to 4, 4 to 6 and 9 to 12, in mathematics, early reading and language, science and nature, and practical life and feelings, for children in California and the Netherlands.

Date of research: 1 October 2026.

## How to read this

Every claim carries one of three marks.

- **[V]** verified: I read the source text itself (the paper, the report, or the developer's own page) and the number or statement is in it.
- **[S]** secondary: taken from an abstract shown in a search result, a summary by someone else, or a well-known finding I did not open in this pass. Treat numbers marked [S] as probably right but unchecked.
- **[I]** inference: my own reasoning from the evidence. Not a finding.

Two notes on method. First, an automated page summariser returned invented statistics for one paper early on, so every [V] number below was checked against text I extracted from the document myself. Second, several publishers blocked access (Taylor and Francis, Wiley, SAGE, ACM, ScienceDirect). Where I could not open something I say so. Nothing here is cited that I could not at least locate.

The hard constraints (no scores, coins, streaks, timers, praise, ticks or crosses; no words or numerals for the youngest; no network; put-away at any instant) are taken as given. Where the evidence does not support a constraint, or cuts against it, I say so plainly and then say how to get the benefit by other means. That happens in four places: praise, points, timed practice, and free discovery.

## One-page summary: fifteen design rules, each with its strongest evidence

1. **Make the mechanic the skill.** The thing the child's finger does to win the moment must be the school skill, expressed in the game's own objects. In the one direct experiment, children who divided skeletons in combat scored 58% on a delayed test against 41% for children who played the same game with the division moved into quizzes between levels, and with free choice they played the integrated version for 75.7 minutes against 10.3 [V, Habgood and Ainsworth 2011]. A later study found the gain comes from attention: players attend only to what the task needs [S, Cutting and Iacovides 2022]. The evidence is thin beyond these studies, and one small replication went the other way [V, Nidd and Muldner].

2. **Choose the representation before the game.** A representation teaches when its physical structure matches the idea. One hour on a straight number board improved preschoolers' sense of number size; the same game on a circular board did not [V, Siegler and Ramani 2009]. Counting on from the current square taught more than counting from one [V, Laski and Siegler 2014].

3. **Keep the working objects plain. Put the charm around them, not on them.** Plain manipulatives beat perceptually rich ones for transfer [S, Carbonneau et al. 2013]. Schematic games taught more than realistic ones (g 0.48 against −0.01), and more visual and story dressing went with less learning [V, Clark et al. 2016]. In story apps, animation that matches the story helps; hotspots and side games hurt [V, Takacs et al. 2015].

4. **Show an error as something that happens in the world, then let the child fix it.** Feedback that shows why works; feedback that only cheers does not. Preschoolers given explanatory feedback made fewer errors than those given cheering sounds, and "Great job!" added nothing [V via Outhwaite et al. 2023, reporting Callaghan and Reich 2021]. Practice with no feedback at all taught nothing in a fraction game where practice with feedback taught a lot [V, Fazio et al. 2016].

5. **No rewards for playing.** Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice interest (d −0.28 to −0.40), more so in children [V, Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999]. In an algebra tutor, students rewarded with stars for redoing problems learned less than students who could redo them without rewards [V, Long and Aleven 2014].

6. **Fade from object to picture to symbol inside the same game.** Start with things, turn them into drawings of things, then lay the symbol on top and finally let it stand alone. Support is mostly positive for early mathematics and mixed for science [S, Fyfe et al. 2014]. DragonBox is the worked example. For this studio the symbol stage starts at about six.

7. **Order the challenges; keep success high; hide the machinery.** In a study of 23 evaluated maths apps, a scaffolded sequence of difficulty plus feedback was a necessary condition for the most effective ones [V, Outhwaite et al. 2023]. In a 70,000-player experiment children played longer when the game was easier, not at "moderate" difficulty [S, Lomas et al. 2013].

8. **Guide the discovery. Do not leave a young child to find the idea alone.** Unassisted discovery loses to explicit teaching (d −0.38); discovery with feedback, examples and scaffolds wins (d 0.30) [S, Alfieri et al. 2011]. Problem-solving before instruction helps older students (g 0.36) but for grades 2 to 5 the effect favoured instruction first [V, Sinha and Kapur 2021]. In a wordless game, "instruction" means a character shows it once.

9. **Build for many short visits over weeks.** Games played in one session taught no more than ordinary lessons (g 0.08); games played over several sessions did (g 0.44) [V, Clark et al. 2016]. Total minutes did not matter; spacing did.

10. **Never let a voice carry the instruction alone.** Young children do not attend to audio prompts without a visual model [S, Hiniker et al. 2015]. Tutorials made no difference to engagement in simple games across 45,000 players [V, Andersen et al. 2012]. Reduced language demand is one of the features shared by effective early maths apps [S, Outhwaite et al. 2023 summary].

11. **Early reading: sound play first, then letters as soon as the child is hearing sounds, and build words before reading them.** Phoneme training improved reading more when letters were used (d 0.67 against 0.38) [S, Ehri et al. 2001]. Eight months of sound-only games in preschool still improved reading through grade 2 [S, Lundberg et al. 1988]. Children who built their own spellings learned to read more words than children trained on sounds alone [S, Ouellette and Sénéchal 2008].

12. **Short scenes belong after the action and must be about the content.** Story that competes with the task costs learning [V, Wouters et al. 2013; Clark et al. 2016]. A scene that acts out what the child just made (the word, the sum, the bridge) is both the feedback and the fun.

13. **Let the child choose the direction and the difficulty.** Choice, a meaningful context and small personal touches raised both motivation and learning in fourth and fifth graders [S, Cordova and Lepper 1996]. Felt autonomy and competence predict enjoyment and wanting to play again [S, Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski 2006].

14. **A game alone gives intuition. Build a bridge to words and symbols.** Games taught about twice as much when paired with other teaching (d 0.41 against 0.20) [V, Wouters et al. 2013]. DragonBox players solved 140 in-game equations and did not improve on paper equations in one study [V, Long and Aleven 2014], though a larger trial later found gains [V, Decker-Woodrow et al. 2023]. The bridge here is the symbol stage (rule 6) and a grown-up corner that says what to talk about.

15. **Expect small effects and test with children.** Educational apps average about 0.3 to 0.4 SD, but a large trial using an independent test of a broad skill should expect about 0.07, and neither minutes of use nor an expert-rated quality index predicted the effect [V, Gilbert's summary of Kim et al. 2021 and its 2026 replication]. A well-loved wordless maths program showed a negligible effect in a 52-school trial [V, Rutherford et al. 2014].

## 1. Intrinsic integration: learning built into the core mechanic

### What "chocolate-covered broccoli" is

The phrase describes a game used as a sweet coating for a lesson: answer a sum, get to shoot a spaceship. The game is the reward and the learning is the price. Habgood and Ainsworth call this using games "as a sugar-coating for learning content" [V]. Their test for it is simple: if the maths could be swapped for spelling without changing the game, the content is attached, not integrated [V].

### Habgood and Ainsworth (2011), Zombie Division

The game is a 3D sword-fighting adventure. Skeletons carry a number on their chest. Each attack is a divisor: one sword swipe divides by 2, a barge with a three-cornered shield divides by 3, a punch with a five-fingered gauntlet divides by 5, and a swipe plus a punch divides by 10. The attack defeats the skeleton only if it divides the number into whole parts. The weapons combine the way the divisors do [V].

They define intrinsic integration in two parts [V]:

1. The learning material is delivered through the parts of the game that are the most fun to play, without interrupting the flow of play.
2. The learning material is embodied in the structure of the game world and the player's interactions with it, so the core mechanic is an external representation of the content.

They also argue the fantasy does not matter: the skeletons could be robots with screens on their chests and nothing would change. The link that matters is between content and core mechanic, not content and story [V]. This revises Malone's older idea of "intrinsic fantasy".

Three versions were built: intrinsic (division in combat), extrinsic (the same combat with no maths, and the same division questions as multiple-choice quizzes between levels), and a control with no maths.

**Study 1** [V]. 58 children aged 7 years 1 month to 8 years 10 months, one school in a low-income area in the north of England. Two hours of play plus one teacher-led reflection session that was the same in all conditions. All three groups improved. Groups did not differ at the immediate post-test. They differed at the delayed test (F(2,48) = 7.49, partial eta squared 0.24): intrinsic 58%, extrinsic 41%, control 38%. The intrinsic group kept improving between post-test and delayed test; the extrinsic group did not. During play the extrinsic children were more accurate on the maths (67% against 57%), and still learned less.

**Study 2** [V]. 16 children aged 9 years 10 months to 11 years 2 months in an after-school club, free to choose between versions. They played the intrinsic version for a mean of 75.7 minutes and the extrinsic for 10.28 minutes (t = 7.38, df = 15, p < .001, r = .89).

What to take from it, and what not to:

- The learning advantage appeared only after a delay and with a teacher-led reflection session in the mix. The paper does not show that an integrated game alone, with no adult, beats a quiz game [V for the design; I for the caution].
- The samples are small and from one school [V].
- The motivation result is the cleanest: given a choice, children chose the integrated game seven to one [V].

### Later tests of the idea

- Cutting and Iacovides (2022) found higher learning in an intrinsically integrated game with no difference in motivation or cognitive load, and concluded the mechanism is attention: players attend to what the game task needs and ignore the rest [S, abstract; ACM blocked the full text].
- Nidd and Muldner (Cognitive Science Society proceedings; year not confirmed) tested two versions of Battleship teaching complex numbers to 58 adults. Motivation was the same and the extrinsic version produced more learning [V, abstract and discussion]. So the principle is not a law. In their intrinsic version the game's demands competed with the content.
- Clark et al. (2016) coded intrinsic versus extrinsic design across 57 studies and found no advantage for games coded "intrinsic" (g 0.19) over the others (g 0.33 and 0.49) [V]. This is a correlation across different games with different controls, and they found only one experiment that tested the factor directly, which was Zombie Division [V, via Nidd and Muldner's reading]. It does mean the meta-analytic record does not confirm the principle.
- McLaren et al. (2017): Decimal Point, a theme park of mini-games in which decimal misconceptions are the tasks, beat a conventional computer tutor with the same content on immediate (d 0.43) and delayed (d 0.37) tests and was rated far more enjoyable (d 0.95), with 153 middle-school students [V].

My reading [I]: the attention account is the useful one. Whatever the child must look at and think about to succeed is what the child learns. Integration works when that thing is the school idea, and it fails when the integrated game adds other demands (aiming, timing, navigation) that take attention from it. That is also why the Zombie Division children were less accurate in play: they were fighting as well as dividing.

### Malone and Lepper's taxonomy (1987)

Four individual sources of intrinsic motivation: **challenge** (clear goals, uncertain outcome, feedback, a stake in self-esteem), **curiosity** (sensory and cognitive), **control** (what I do changes what happens; choice; a sense of power), and **fantasy**. Three interpersonal ones: cooperation, competition, recognition. Their key distinction is **endogenous** against **exogenous**: learning is best when the motivating feature and the content depend on each other [S; the chapter is a book chapter and the copy I found was a scan with no text layer]. This is a taxonomy built from observation and small studies of 1980s games, not a tested model. Habgood's work keeps "endogenous" and moves it from fantasy to mechanic.

### Plass, Homer and Kinzer (2015)

A theory review, not a study [V]. Points that matter for design:

- **Game-based learning is not gamification.** Gamification uses incentive systems "to motivate players to engage in a task they otherwise would not find attractive", such as points and stars on homework. Game-based learning redesigns the task itself [V].
- Four arguments for games: motivation, engagement, **adaptivity**, and **graceful failure**, where failure is "by design an expected and sometimes even necessary step in the learning process" and its low cost encourages risk and exploration [V].
- Engagement has four kinds: cognitive, affective, behavioural and sociocultural. The aim is cognitive engagement with the content; the other three serve it [V].
- The content should determine the mechanic, the look, the story, the incentives and the music, in that order of dependence [V].
- Incentives can be intrinsic to play (a power-up that changes what you can do) or extrinsic (stars that do nothing) [V].
- They note that scaffolding which fades works well in entertainment games and has had "much more limited" success in learning games, because judging what the learner knows during play is hard [V].

### James Paul Gee's learning principles

Gee (2003, 2005) argues that good commercial games are already good teaching and lists principles under three headings: empowered learners (co-design, customisation, identity, manipulation), problem solving (well-ordered problems, "pleasantly frustrating" challenge, cycles of expertise, information on demand and just in time, fish tanks, sandboxes, skills as strategies) and understanding (system thinking, meaning as action) [S; I did not open the 2005 paper in this pass]. These are observations from analysing games, not experimental findings. The ones with independent support are well-ordered problems (rule 7), just-in-time information (rule 10) and sandboxes (rule 13). "Pleasantly frustrating" is contradicted for young children by Lomas's data (section 6).

### Kurt Squire

Squire studied commercial and purpose-built games in classrooms, most famously Civilization III for world history, and frames games as "designed experiences" in which players learn by taking on roles inside a system [S; not opened in this pass]. The work is qualitative. Its lasting finding is that the learning depended on the talk and teaching around the game, and that many students at first found the game hard and unappealing [S]. That agrees with rule 14.

### What "the mechanic is the skill" means in practice

A working test with five questions [I, built from the sources above]:

1. **Swap test.** Could the content be replaced with another subject without changing how the game plays? If yes, it is bolted on (Habgood's own test).
2. **Attention test.** At the moment of decision, what must the child look at and think about? That is what is learned. If the answer is "where to aim", the game teaches aiming.
3. **Representation test.** Is the idea visible in the objects, so the child can see why something worked? A number as a length that fits or does not fit passes. A number as a label on a balloon fails.
4. **Fun test.** Is the skill used in the most enjoyable moment of play, or does play stop for it?
5. **Guess test.** Can the child succeed by tapping at random or by trying every option? If so the mechanic is not carrying the skill.

## 2. What the meta-analyses say

### Wouters, van Nimwegen, van Oostendorp and van der Spek (2013)

Serious games against conventional teaching; 77 comparisons for learning (N = 5,547), 31 for motivation [V].

- Learning d = 0.29; retention d = 0.36; **motivation d = 0.26, not significant**.
- Game plus other teaching d = 0.41; game alone d = 0.20.
- One session shows little (for example d = 0.18 against active teaching); more than one session shows more (d = 0.43; against mixed teaching 0.15 and 0.58).
- Playing in pairs or groups beat playing alone.
- Schematic and cartoon-like games were as effective as, or more effective than, realistic ones. The authors' advice: focus on content, less on visual design.
- Games with a story were not significantly worse, but the authors say the pattern suggests "including a narrative is counterproductive", unless the story is tied to the learning goal.
- Randomised studies report smaller effects.
- Why were games not more motivating? Their three reasons: players in studies do not choose the game or when to play it (no autonomy); teaching elements such as reflection prompts break the flow; and motivation was measured by questionnaire afterwards. They cite Habgood and Ainsworth as the way forward.

### Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth (2016)

Digital games, K–16, 69 studies from 2000 to 2012 [V].

- Games against non-game teaching: g = 0.33 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.48).
- Better-designed game against standard version of the same game ("value-added"): g = 0.34 (0.17 to 0.51). Their main conclusion is that design matters as much as medium.
- Enhanced scaffolding (adaptive help, restructuring the game to bring out the learning mechanic): g = 0.41 (0.18 to 0.64).
- Single session g = 0.08; multiple sessions g = 0.44. Total duration was unrelated to effect. Games were played for 347 minutes on average. The authors read this as a spacing effect.
- Adding non-game teaching made no difference here (0.36 against 0.32), unlike Wouters.
- Single player with no competition g = 0.45; single player competing g = −0.06; team competition g = 0.22.
- **Points and badges added to school-like tasks: g = 0.53. More elaborate games: g = 0.25.** The difference was not significant (p = .07).
- Feedback level: success, failure or points only g = 0.26; enhanced scaffolding g = 0.48; scaffolding from a teacher g = 0.58.
- Schematic g = 0.48; cartoon g = 0.32; realistic g = −0.01.
- A summed "contextualisation" score (camera view, realism, human-like characters, story relevance, story depth) had a negative relation with learning (b = −0.07, p = .01).

The points-and-badges line needs a comment because it seems to cut against this studio's rules. It compares simple drill with points against a lecture or worksheet on short tests of the drilled skill. It says simple drill games work for drilled skills. It does not measure what happens to interest afterwards, which is where rewards do harm (section 5) [I].

### Sitzmann (2011)

Simulation games for adult trainees, 65 samples, N = 6,476 [V]. Self-efficacy 20% higher, declarative knowledge 11%, procedural knowledge 14%, retention 9% higher than comparison groups. Strong evidence of publication bias. Trainees learned more when the game taught actively and not through embedded text or lectures, when they could return to it as often as they wanted, and when it supplemented other teaching. **Entertainment value did not change how much was learned** (d = .26 for high, .38 for low). This is adult workplace training; the transferable points are active content and free return.

### More recent work

- **Barz, Benick, Dörrenbächer-Ulrich and Perels (2024)**, school studies from 2015 to 2020: overall g = 0.54, cognitive g = 0.67, affective-motivational g = 0.32, metacognitive not significant [S, abstract].
- **Sailer and Homner (2020)**, gamification (points, badges, leaderboards on non-game tasks): cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36, behavioural g = 0.25; only the cognitive effect held up in the rigorous studies [S, abstract].
- **Tokac, Novak and Thompson (2019)**, mathematics video games PreK–12, 24 studies: a small positive average with effects varying in size and direction [S; I recall the average as about 0.13 but could not confirm it].
- **Kusumawardani et al. (2026)**, 30 studies of elementary mathematics games, 2015 to 2025: a positive effect, largest for simulation-type games; game type, year group and duration moderated it [S, search summary only; the paper was blocked].
- **Mayer (2019)**, a review of value-added experiments: five features with repeated support are spoken rather than printed words (modality), conversational wording (personalisation), pretraining in key ideas, coaching, and prompts to self-explain. Subjects where games beat other media: science, mathematics and second-language learning [S, abstract].
- **D'Angelo et al. (2014)**, computer simulations in K–12 STEM: simulation against no simulation g = 0.62 (46 effects); simulation with added scaffolding or modified representations against the plain simulation g = 0.49 (50 effects) [V, results table].
- **Kim, Gilbert, Yu and Gale (2021)** and its 2026 replication, apps for ages 3 to 9 [V, from the author's own summary; the papers were blocked]. Original: 36 studies, 285 effects, +0.31 SD. Replication: 141 studies, 841 effects, d = 0.40 (0.32 to 0.49), with a 95% prediction interval from −0.33 to 1.13. Tests of narrow skills (letter names, single-digit sums) gave effects 0.18 SD larger than tests of broad skills (vocabulary, problem solving) in the same children. Tests written by the app's own researchers gave effects 0.22 SD larger than independent tests. Randomised trials gave smaller effects (b = −0.27). Length of intervention, number of sessions, session length and total time did not predict the effect. Neither did a quality index built on Hirsh-Pasek's pillars. Only 8 of 141 studies had any follow-up, and effects faded about 14% over five months. Their worked example: a 1,000-child randomised trial with a standard test of a broad skill predicts about 0.07 SD; a 100-child quasi-experiment with a home-made test of a narrow skill predicts 0.94.
- **Outhwaite, Early, Herodotou and Van Herwegen (2023)**, 23 evaluated maths apps for the first three years of school [V]. Fifteen were practice apps and 21 targeted basic number. No app set the maths in a real-life context or applied it to new problems. The combination of explanatory feedback, motivational feedback and a programmed sequence of difficulty (fixed or adaptive, as opposed to free choice of level) was a necessary condition for the most effective apps. Motivational feedback alone was not associated with effectiveness but "was not a hindrance". The two kinds of feedback could not be separated because no app in the sample had explanatory feedback only.

### What improves learning, what does not, and how strong the evidence is

| Feature | Direction | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Several spaced sessions | Helps | Strong: three meta-analyses agree [V] |
| Feedback that explains or shows why | Helps | Moderate to strong [V] |
| Ordered, scaffolded difficulty | Helps | Moderate [V] |
| Teaching, talk or reflection around the game | Helps | Mixed: Wouters and Sitzmann yes, Clark no difference [V] |
| Plain, schematic visuals | Helps or neutral | Moderate; consistent across Wouters, Clark, Carbonneau [V, S] |
| Free return, as often as wanted | Helps | One meta-analysis, adults [V] |
| Thick or unrelated story | Neutral to harmful | Moderate [V] |
| Visual realism | Neutral to harmful | Moderate [V] |
| Individual competition | No benefit | One meta-analysis, few studies [V] |
| Entertainment value as such | No effect on learning | One meta-analysis, adults [V] |
| Minutes of use | No effect | Two meta-analyses [V] |
| Intrinsic integration | Helps in the direct experiment; not confirmed across studies | Weak to moderate [V] |
| Games being more motivating than lessons | Not shown | Wouters: not significant [V] |

The overall average is real but modest (roughly 0.3 SD), shrinks with rigour, and says little about any one game.

## 3. Exemplars

For each: the one mechanic, where the school skill lives, the evidence, what brings a child back, and the idea to take. "No study found" means I searched and found no controlled evaluation; it does not mean the game does not teach.

### Mathematics

**DragonBox Algebra (5+ and 12+).** *Mechanic:* a board with two sides; get the box alone on one side by dragging cards, where a card and its night-coloured twin cancel, and anything added to one side must be added to the other. *Skill:* these are the legal moves of equation solving. Over each chapter the box becomes x, the creature cards become numbers and letters, and operation signs appear [S, developer and reviews; V for the gradual shift from pictures to notation, Decker-Woodrow et al.]. *Evidence:* in the Washington State Algebra Challenge, 92.9% of students who played at least 1.5 hours reached in-game mastery, in 41 minutes 44 seconds on average, with adaptive extra levels [V, University of Washington summary]. That is mastery of the game. In a classroom study of 267 middle-school students, DragonBox players did not improve from pre-test to post-test on solving linear equations, and tutor users learned significantly more [V, Long and Aleven 2014]; they had solved far more problems and enjoyed it more [S]. In a later randomised trial (analytic N = 1,850 seventh graders, nine 30-minute sessions), DragonBox 12+ and From Here to There both beat an active control on algebraic knowledge; plain problem sets with immediate feedback did not [V, Decker-Woodrow et al. 2023]. *Return:* each level is a small clean puzzle and the rules grow by one each chapter. *Idea:* teach the legal moves of a symbol system as the rules of a puzzle, then swap the pictures for the symbols, and do not assume the swap has transferred until the child has done it in the school form.

**DragonBox Numbers.** *Mechanic:* Nooms, creatures whose height is their number from 1 to 10, are stacked, sliced, fed to each other and sorted. *Skill:* composing and decomposing: slicing a 7 gives a 3 and a 4 because the lengths say so. The developer calls them "digital manipulatives inspired by Cuisenaire and Montessori rods" and says the app has no quizzes [V, developer page]. *Evidence:* no study found. *Return:* building pictures and filling puzzles with Nooms; a free-play area. *Idea:* give each number a body whose size is the number, so that every sum is something you can see fit.

**DragonBox Elements.** *Mechanic:* trace shapes in a figure to "find" triangles and quadrilaterals, then use powers (equal sides, equal angles, parallel lines) that unlock when their conditions are met. *Skill:* each power is a theorem from Euclid and each level is a proof [S, press coverage; not opened]. *Evidence:* no study found. *Idea:* a proof step can be a tap that only works when its premises are on the board.

**Slice Fractions (Ululab).** *Mechanic:* slice blocks of ice so the right amount falls and clears lava from a mammoth's path. *Skill:* the child partitions a whole into equal parts and chooses how many; later, fraction notation labels the pieces. Area model, no words. *Evidence:* a study by the game's academic partners at UQAM reported that three hours of play brought third graders to a level on TIMSS-type fraction items comparable to fourth graders [V, UQAM press release], and a conference paper reported the game group learned as much as a group taught conventionally [S]. I found no peer-reviewed journal report. *Return:* physics puzzles with several solutions; hats for the mammoth. *Idea:* let the fraction be the cut the child makes, and let gravity say whether it was the right cut.

**Motion Math (Fractions).** *Mechanic:* tilt the tablet to bounce a ball onto the right place on a number line. *Skill:* fraction as a position between 0 and 1, across fractions, decimals, percentages and pies. *Evidence:* 122 fifth graders, 20 minutes a day for five days: fraction test scores rose about 15% and liking and self-efficacy about 10%, both significant against a control [S, abstract of Riconscente 2013]. *Idea:* one-dimensional estimation with a body movement. Note it relied on speed; the falling ball is a timer and would need replacing here.

**Catch the Monster with Fractions (research game).** *Mechanic:* a monster hides on a 0-to-1 line; place the fraction to catch it; the game then shows where the fraction really is. *Evidence:* under 15 minutes of play produced large gains in number line estimation, magnitude comparison and recall in fourth and fifth graders; a control group that made the same estimates without feedback did not improve [V, Fazio, Kennedy and Siegler 2016]. *Idea:* estimate, then see the true position beside your guess. The seeing is the lesson.

**Linear number board game (Siegler and Ramani).** *Mechanic:* spin a 1 or 2, move along ten squares in a row, say the numbers as you move. *Skill:* the further along, the bigger the number, by distance, steps and time all at once. *Evidence:* about an hour of play across sessions improved low-income preschoolers' magnitude comparison and number line estimation and helped them learn arithmetic afterwards; a circular board did not [V, Siegler and Ramani 2009]. Counting on from the current square (for example "six, seven" and not "one, two") improved encoding of the board and number line estimates [V, Laski and Siegler 2014]. *Idea:* a straight path with equal steps, counted on. Cheap, well evidenced, and fits ages 4 to 6 exactly.

**ST Math (JiJi).** *Mechanic:* arrange a visual puzzle (blocks, fractions of shapes, a number line) so a penguin's path is clear; press go; an animation plays out the answer the child gave. *Skill:* the maths is the spatial arrangement itself; words and symbols are held back. *Evidence:* the developer describes the feedback as "informative": the animation shows what went wrong and why, for right and wrong answers alike [V, developer page; S, press]. A randomised trial in 52 low-performing California schools found "a negligible effect of ST Math on mathematics scores" and a non-significant two-year effect [V, Rutherford et al. 2014]. The developer's own re-analysis of the same trial reports significant gains in grades 4 and 5 [V, MIND Research Institute 2021; not independent]. *Return:* puzzle sequences, the penguin crossing. *Idea:* the cleanest model of feedback without judgement: run the child's answer and let them watch what it does. Also a warning that a good design did not guarantee a measurable effect at scale.

**The Number Race and its descendants.** An adaptive game comparing quantities, built by Wilson and Dehaene for children with dyscalculia. The first test had nine children and no control group; subitizing and comparison got faster, subtraction accuracy rose 23%, addition and base-ten did not improve [V, Wilson et al. 2006]. Later trials were mixed [S]. *Idea:* adapt on three dimensions (distance between numbers, speed, format). The speed dimension is not usable here.

**onebillion (onecourse maths).** A wordless-on-screen, spoken, structured sequence of small maths activities. A UK randomised trial found gains for 4- to 5-year-olds, and an Education Endowment Foundation trial found about three months' extra progress for lower-attaining Year 1 pupils [S]. Outhwaite's framework names what its apps share: autonomous use, fit for small hands, a settled task structure, reduced language demand and personal levelling [S]. *Idea:* a fixed, predictable activity shape lets the child spend attention on the maths, not on working out the game.

**Math Garden (Rekentuin), Netherlands.** An adaptive practice system used in Dutch schools. It sets item difficulty for a chosen success rate. An experiment gave children 60%, 75% or 90% success [S, Jansen et al. 2013]. Maths anxiety improved equally in all three [V, as reported in a later review]; my recollection is that the 90% group practised more and so improved more, but I could not open the paper to confirm [S]. It uses coins and time pressure, which this studio excludes. *Idea:* aim high on success rate; children vote with their feet.

**Numberblocks (television, with NCETM).** Each number is a character made of that many unit cubes; numbers add by jumping together and split by coming apart; arrangements show structure (a 6 that is two 3s or three 2s). The makers say they work closely with the National Centre for Excellence in the Teaching of Mathematics on every episode [V, makers' site]. *Evidence:* no controlled study found; wide classroom use in England. *Idea:* the number's body is its structure, and the story is an operation. One being can be rearranged without changing who it is (conservation).

**Cuisenaire rods.** Ten coloured rods, 1 to 10 units long, unmarked. Length is the number; putting rods end to end is addition; a train of equal rods is multiplication; one rod measured by another is a fraction or ratio. Evidence from modern controlled studies is thin [S]; one recent study found an intervention stressing relations between rods improved addition and subtraction fluency, most for lower-attaining children [S]. *Idea:* unmarked length forces relational thinking: nothing to count, so you compare.

**Numicon.** Flat plates with holes in a two-wide pattern, 1 to 10. Odd numbers have a bump; two plates fit together into the plate for their sum. A study with children with Down syndrome found better progress that was not statistically significant [S]. *Idea:* a fixed pattern for each number makes it recognisable at a glance and shows odd and even without words.

**Montessori materials and Montessori Preschool (Edoki).** Edoki's app reproduces golden beads, number rods, sandpaper letters and practical-life trays on screen [S, from the store description; not opened]. No study of the app found. A separate Montessori pink tower app in Outhwaite's sample "constrained incorrect moves to facilitate accuracy" [V], which is control of error in software. For the method itself see section 4. *Idea:* one difficulty isolated per material, and a material that will not go together wrongly.

**Prodigy (cautionary).** A role-playing game in which battles pause for multiple-choice maths questions. It fails the swap test. A complaint to the US Federal Trade Commission by Fairplay and 21 partners in 2021 says a play session showed many advertisements for paid membership and only four maths problems, and that the university study the company cites noted a "lack of remediation and actual teaching" [V, the complaint; an advocacy document, and the regulator has not ruled as far as I found]. *Idea:* what to avoid: content as a toll, loot as the reason to play, and pressure on children to ask parents to pay.

### Reading and language

**Endless Alphabet and Endless Reader (Originator).** *Mechanic:* letters are scattered; drag each into its outline in the word. While held, the letter comes alive and says its sound over and over. When the word is complete, monsters act out its meaning in a short scene and a voice gives a definition [S, reviews and store page]. *Skill:* letter-sound correspondence in the hand, vocabulary in the scene. No fail state and no score. *Evidence:* no study found. *Return:* the sounds are funny, the scenes are funny, and a child picks any word. *Idea:* the object says what it is while it is touched; the scene after the action is the meaning. Limit: letters go into outlines, so the child matches shapes and does not have to think about sounds. It would fail the guess test as a phonics game.

**Teach Your Monster to Read (Usborne Foundation).** *Mechanic:* a made-to-order monster travels through islands of mini-games that ask for the grapheme matching a spoken sound, then blending and reading. It follows the order of English synthetic phonics programmes and repeats graphemes the child got wrong [S]. *Evidence:* I found no controlled trial. *Return:* owning and dressing the monster, travel, prizes. *Idea:* the fixed, phonics-ordered sequence is the strong part. The mini-games are mostly "find the right one among several", which is close to a quiz with a skin, and prizes are used.

**GraphoGame (Finland).** Hear a sound, pick the matching letter from falling balls; adaptive. A meta-analysis of 19 studies found no overall effect on word reading (g = −0.02); effects appeared where adults were closely involved [S, McTigue et al. 2020; the adult-involvement number not verified]. *Idea:* the most studied phonics game shows that drill on letter-sound matching alone does not move reading.

**Khan Academy Kids.** A guided path of short activities across literacy, maths and social skills, with books and videos. In a randomised trial with 49 four- and five-year-olds from low-income families, ten weeks at about 13 minutes a day moved overall emergent literacy from the 34th to the 47th percentile and phonological awareness from the 23rd to the 47th, while a comparison group using other apps barely changed [V, publisher's report of Arnold et al. 2021; small sample]. *Idea:* a curriculum-ordered path with adult-quality voice modelling can move pre-reading skills at home. Its activities are conventional and praise-heavy; take the sequencing, not the feedback.

**Duolingo ABC.** Short letter, sound and word activities. The published evaluation is a single-group before-and-after study of 105 children over nine weeks with no control group [V, EDC report 2022]. It cannot show the app caused the gain.

**Metamorphabet (Vectorpark).** *Mechanic:* poke, pull and spin a letter and it turns into things that begin with it: A grows antlers, becomes an arch, goes for an amble, each word spoken. *Skill:* letter shape, initial sound and vocabulary; mainly it is a toy. *Evidence:* none. *Idea:* the standard for "alive at idle" and for one object that keeps surprising: each touch reveals the next state, with no goal and no failure.

### Logic and programming

**Zoombinis.** *Mechanic:* get a band of creatures, each with four attributes (hair, eyes, nose, feet), past gatekeepers whose rule is hidden; a rejected creature is bounced back, and that is the clue. *Skill:* sorting by attribute, forming and testing a hypothesis, controlling one variable at a time. *Evidence:* TERC built detectors of problem-solving strategies from play logs; in-game measures were related to independent tests of computational thinking, and more play related to more such thinking afterwards [S, TERC summary and abstracts]. This is correlational. *Return:* the journey, rising difficulty chosen by the player, caring for the band. *Idea:* the wrong guess is information. The pizza troll spits out the pizza and says nothing about the child.

**Lightbot.** *Mechanic:* lay out a row of command tiles (forward, turn, jump, light), press run, watch the robot carry them out. *Skill:* sequencing, then procedures and loops, because the slots run out. *Evidence:* an analysis mapping it to computational thinking and small classroom studies [S]; no controlled trial found. *Idea:* the program is a visible object and the bug is a robot walking off the wrong tile. The limit on slots creates the need for the next idea.

**Human Resource Machine.** A small office worker carries boxes between an inbox, the floor and an outbox under a list of commands; in effect assembly language [S]. For ages 10 and up. *Idea:* older children will accept a hard formal system when the stepping is visible and the tone is dry and funny.

**Thinkrolls (Avokiddo).** Roll a ball character down a maze; clear the way by moving a crate, popping a balloon, melting ice with fire. *Skill:* cause and effect, properties of materials, planning a sequence. *Evidence:* none found. *Idea:* each chapter adds one new object with one property, and later levels combine them.

### Science and engineering

**Toca Lab and the Toca Boca approach.** Heat, cool, spin and shock a cartoon element and it becomes another. The studio describes its products as digital toys: no rules, no levels, no winning or losing [S]. Toca Lab's transformations do not follow real chemistry [I], so it teaches the stance of trying things, not facts. *Idea:* a tool that responds richly to every action needs no goal for a young child. Be honest about whether the science in it is true.

**The Incredible Machine; Bridge Constructor; Physics Playground.** Build a contraption or structure from parts, press go, watch physics run. The bridge holds or falls. *Evidence:* for Physics Playground, 154 eighth and ninth graders improved on a qualitative physics test from play alone, and in-game measures tracked outside measures [S, Shute and colleagues]. *Idea:* the engineering design loop (build, test, see where it breaks, change one thing) is the whole game, and the failure is usually the funniest moment.

**Kerbal Space Program.** Build a rocket from parts and fly it under realistic orbital mechanics. Used in schools and endorsed by space agencies [S]. Formal evidence of learning is thin and mostly at university level [S]. *Idea:* a real model, comic failure, and goals the player sets. Nine- to twelve-year-olds respect that it is real.

**Universe Sandbox.** Change a mass, add a moon, collide planets, and watch gravity respond. No study found. *Idea:* "what if" on a true model.

**Minecraft Education.** A 2025 systematic review of 29 studies found mostly positive results for mathematics and science and rated every study at medium or high risk of bias [S, Slattery et al.]. *Idea:* wide walls. The evidence does not support claims that it teaches curriculum content by itself.

### Feelings and practical life

**Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood (television and app).** Each story names a feeling, shows it, and gives a short sung strategy. In a study of 127 children aged 2 to 6, those who watched ten episodes over two weeks showed higher empathy, self-efficacy and emotion recognition than those who watched a nature programme, but only where parents regularly talked with them about what they watched [S, Rasmussen et al. 2016]. *Idea:* name, show, one small strategy, repeat; and it works through the grown-up.

### What the exemplars have in common [I]

The ones that hold up share six things: one mechanic; a representation in which the idea is visible; a consequence the child can read; an ordered run of challenges that adds one thing at a time; no words needed to start; and a short pay-off scene. The ones with weak or no evidence of learning are mostly "pick the right one" games with a theme.

## 4. Representations that work, subject by subject and age by age

### The reference frameworks and what research says about them

**Bruner's enactive, iconic, symbolic.** Bruner (1966) proposed that an idea is first known through action, then through images, then through symbols [S]. It is a theory of representation, not a tested teaching sequence.

**Concrete, representational, abstract (CRA) and concreteness fading.** The teaching sequence built on Bruner. A systematic review found mostly positive support in early mathematics and less convincing support in science [S, Fyfe et al. 2014]. Fading means the concrete form is gradually stripped away within one sequence, not that three separate lessons happen.

**Manipulatives in general.** A meta-analysis of 55 studies (N = 7,237) found small to moderate advantages for teaching with manipulatives over symbols only [V, abstract of Carbonneau et al. 2013], with larger effects on retention, small effects on problem solving and transfer, and better transfer from plain objects than from perceptually rich ones [S]. Four conditions for manipulatives to work, from cognitive science and shown in Montessori practice [S, Laski et al. 2015]: use the same material consistently over a long time; begin concrete and move to abstract; avoid materials that look like everyday objects or have distracting features; and make the link between material and idea explicit.

**Montessori.** A Campbell systematic review of 32 studies found Montessori education ahead of conventional schooling: composite academic outcomes g = 0.24 (mathematics 0.22, language 0.17, general academic ability 0.26) and non-academic outcomes g = 0.33 (executive function 0.36, creativity 0.26), with larger effects in randomised studies and in preschool and elementary settings [V, Randolph et al. 2023]. A lottery-based study of 141 children in public Montessori preschools found they pulled ahead over three years in academic achievement, social understanding and mastery orientation, and liked school tasks more [V, Lillard et al. 2017]. These are results for the whole method, with trained adults and three-year age groups. They do not show that any single material works on a screen.

What the materials teach about design [S for the descriptions, I for the reading]:

- **Pink tower, brown stair, red rods.** One dimension varies and nothing else does. The error shows: the tower looks wrong or falls.
- **Number rods and golden beads.** Quantity first, as length or as unit, ten-bar, hundred-square, thousand-cube; numeral cards are added after and laid on the quantity.
- **Sandpaper letters.** Trace the letter while saying its sound: touch, sight and sound together. Small experiments with haptic letter exploration support this [S; not opened].
- **Moveable alphabet.** The child builds words from sounds with loose letters before being asked to read. Research on invented spelling supports encoding first (below).
- **Practical life trays.** A real task with a fixed order, real tools, a visible end state and a natural error (the water spills).
- **Control of error.** The material, not the adult, shows the mistake.

**Dutch realistic mathematics education (RME).** The Dutch tradition gives models that start as pictures of a situation and become tools for thinking: the rekenrek (bead frame in fives and tens), the empty number line, the bar or strip for fractions and percentages, the double number line and the ratio table [S, Van den Heuvel-Panhuizen 2003 and general knowledge of the tradition]. These are what Dutch children meet at school, and a bar model in particular carries from fractions through percentages to ratio.

### Mathematics

A real balance cannot show negatives or subtraction well. DragonBox avoids the scale and uses cancelling pairs |

By age [I]:

- **2 to 4.** Quantities to about 5. More and less by size. One-to-one matching as a kind act (a hat for each). Sorting by one attribute. No numerals. Spoken number words on each object.
- **4 to 6.** Quantities to 10, then 20. Patterns for subitizing. A straight path. Rods and frames. Making ten. Numerals arrive late in the band, on top of the quantity.
- **9 to 12.** Bars, number lines, ratio tables, area rectangles, the balance or the cancelling board, coordinate grids, with full notation.

### Early reading and language

What the reading research says [S unless marked]:

- Teaching children to hear and handle the sounds in words improves reading (d = 0.53 overall) and works better when letters are used (d = 0.67 against 0.38) [Ehri et al. 2001].
- Sound-only work is still worth doing for children who have no letters yet. Danish preschoolers given eight months of daily sound games, with no letters and no reading teaching, were ahead in reading and spelling through grade 2 [Lundberg, Frost and Petersen 1988].
- Building spellings teaches reading. Kindergartners who spelled words as best they could, with feedback, learned to read more words than children given sound training or drawing [Ouellette and Sénéchal 2008].
- Story apps: animation, music and sound that match the story improve comprehension (g 0.17) and vocabulary (g 0.20); hotspots, games and dictionaries inside the story distract, most of all for disadvantaged children [V, Takacs, Swart and Bus 2015].
- For ages 9 to 12, teaching word parts (roots, prefixes, suffixes) improves vocabulary (d 0.34), decoding (0.59) and spelling (0.30) [Goodwin and Ahn 2013].

| Skill | Sound-only play (no letters) | When and how letters appear |
|---|---|---|
| Listening and sound discrimination | Which animal made that sound; find the same sound; loud and soft, high and low | Not needed |
| Rhyme | A creature eats only things that rhyme with its name; things that rhyme stack or click together | Not needed |
| Syllables | One hop, clap or drum beat per syllable; a word train with one carriage per beat | Not needed |
| First sound | Feed the snake things that start with "sss"; group objects by first sound | The letter can ride on the creature as its badge once the child sorts reliably |
| Blending | A slow robot says "c … a … t"; the child slides the sounds together and the cat appears | Letters on the tiles being slid: this is the point where letters should come in |
| Segmenting | Stretch a word like dough; one bead per sound | Beads become letter tiles (moveable alphabet) |
| Letter-sound correspondence | None: this is the step that needs letters | Trace the letter while it says its sound (sandpaper letter); letters that speak when touched (Endless Alphabet) |
| Vocabulary | The word acted out in a short scene after the child makes or finds it | The written word appears with the scene |
| Narrative | Arrange picture cards and the story plays in that order; wordless books; the child's own arrangement is retold aloud | Captions later |

When letters must appear [I, from the evidence above]. For 2 to 4: none; all sound play. For 4 to 6: sound play first, and letters from the moment the child is isolating first sounds and blending, which is the same moment the Ehri result says letters start to help. A band-wide ban on letters for 4 to 6 would leave the strongest effect unused. Letters are not "words on screen" in the sense the youngest rule intends; a single letter that makes its sound is an object with a voice. Build words before reading them.

Two-language note [I]: letter-sound mapping is far more regular in Dutch than in English, and the sound sets differ. Sound-only games can share a design across languages with different word lists. Letter games need separate content packs, and English needs more of them.

### Science and nature

What the research says:

- Free discovery loses to explicit teaching; guided discovery wins [S, Alfieri et al. 2011]. In inquiry learning, guidance improves what learners do (d 0.66), their success (0.71) and what they learn (0.50) [V, abstract of Lazonder and Harmsen 2016].
- The control-of-variables strategy (change one thing, keep the rest the same) can be taught: g = 0.61 across 72 studies, with demonstrations and cognitive conflict as the features that mattered [S, Schwichow et al. 2016]. In the classic study, 77% of third and fourth graders mastered it after direct instruction against 23% after discovery, and those who learned it by instruction judged science-fair posters as well as those who discovered it [V, Klahr and Nigam 2004].
- Simulations help (g 0.62), and simulations with added scaffolding help more than plain ones (g 0.49) [V, D'Angelo et al. 2014].

| Idea | 2 to 4 | 4 to 6 | 9 to 12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause and effect | Touch it and something reliable happens; the same touch gives the same result | Chains of two or three steps; predict, then see | Systems with feedback and delay |
| Sorting and classifying | By one visible attribute | By two attributes; odd one out; a hidden rule to find (Zoombinis) | Branching keys; build your own key |
| Life cycles | Seed to flower in one sitting, the child giving water and light | Order the stages; a cycle that returns to the start; what happens if a stage is missing | Populations over generations; variation |
| Forces and motion | Push, roll, drop, splash | Ramps, heavier and lighter, sink and float with a guess first | Levers, gears, trajectories; measured and graphed |
| States of matter | Ice melts, water pours | Heat and cool back and forth; it is the same stuff | A particle picture: zoom in and see the same change as motion |
| Ecosystems | Who eats what, one link | A small pond or garden with three or four kinds | Food webs as a live model: remove one kind and watch what follows |
| Engineering design loop | Stack until it falls | Build a bridge or boat, test, change it | Goals and limits (materials, cost); compare designs |
| Controlled experiment | Not yet | "Which made the difference?" with two things changed, as a puzzle | The core skill: two test set-ups, one variable allowed to differ, and a fair-test question that only a clean comparison can answer |

Design notes [I]. For 9 to 12, a fair-test game is the obvious science mechanic and has the strongest evidence behind the skill. Show a confounded comparison giving a misleading answer (cognitive conflict), then have a character run one clean comparison (demonstration), then hand over. For all ages the model must be true where it claims to be science; a pretty transformation that is not real chemistry teaches curiosity and nothing else.

### Practical life and feelings

The evidence here is thinner and mostly about programmes with adults.

- Training children to reason about what others think and feel works in controlled studies: g = 0.75 across 45 studies with children averaging about 5 years old [S, Hofmann et al. 2016].
- Media about feelings helps when a parent talks with the child about it [S, Rasmussen et al. 2016].
- Montessori schooling, with practical life at its base, shows gains in executive function (g 0.36) [V, Randolph et al. 2023]. This is the whole method, not practical-life activities alone.
- I did not find a controlled study showing that an app by itself teaches a young child to regulate feelings or to carry out a self-care routine in real life. Treat games in this strand as rehearsal and vocabulary, with the real learning happening off screen [I].

| Skill | Representation | Mechanic |
|---|---|---|
| Sequencing a real task | The real objects in a tray, a visible end state | Do the steps in an order; the world shows what a skipped step does (the tea is cold, the sock is over the shoe); any step can be redone |
| Self-care | The body and its tools: brush, soap, coat | Care for a character with real motions: scrub until the dirt is gone, dry, dress for the weather outside the window |
| Naming feelings | Faces and whole-body posture, with weather or colour as a second cue | Notice what the creature feels from how it moves, then match it to a face; the spoken feeling word comes with it |
| Regulating feelings | Breath as something visible: a balloon, a sail, a slow wave | The child's slow drag or held touch is the calming; the creature's body settles in step with it |
| Another's perspective | Two views of one scene; what each character can and cannot see | Swap seats and see through the other's eyes; give each character what that character would want, not what the child would want |
| Caring for others | A creature or plant with visible needs | Notice the need without a prompt icon and answer it; nothing dies or is lost if the child leaves |
| Safety | A street, a kitchen, water | Stop, look and go as a rhythm with a real consequence that is not frightening (the ball rolls back, the pot hisses and the creature steps away) |
| Health habits | Plate, bed, tap | Build a plate, run a bedtime, as routines the child arranges |

Design notes [I]. A feelings game that asks "which face is sad?" is a quiz. The intrinsic version has the child read the feeling from behaviour in order to do something kind that works. Regulation can be honestly intrinsic on a touch screen because slow, steady touch is itself the skill. No game in this strand should imply the child has feelings wrong.

## 5. Feedback without judgement

### Natural consequences

The pattern in the best exemplars is the same: the game runs the child's answer and shows what it does.

- ST Math plays the animation for whatever the child built; the penguin crosses or meets the gap [V, developer].
- In Bridge Constructor the truck drives on and the bridge holds or sags at the weak joint.
- In Zoombinis the wrong creature is bounced back, which is the clue.
- In a Montessori pink tower app, wrong moves were constrained [V via Outhwaite].
- In Catch the Monster the true position appears next to the guess [V].

Rules for a readable consequence [I]:

1. It follows from the child's action by the world's own logic. A cake that is too salty makes the creature pull a face and reach for water; it does not make a buzzer sound.
2. It shows where and why. The bridge breaks at the weak span. The rod is too short by a visible gap.
3. The state stays. Nothing resets to punish. The child changes one thing and tries again.
4. It is as interesting as success. Failure animations are often the funniest part; children then explore failure on purpose, which is good.
5. It is about the thing, not the child. No face turned to the camera in disappointment.
6. Success is also a consequence, not a fanfare: the monster eats, the boat floats, the door opens. The pay-off scene is the world carrying on.

### Why explaining beats cheering

- Preschoolers with explanatory feedback made fewer errors than those with cheering sounds; verbal praise did not improve accuracy; the advantage of explanation faded once children could do the task [V via Outhwaite et al. 2023, reporting Callaghan and Reich 2021]. So feedback should be richest early and can thin out.
- Across 607 effects, feedback improved performance on average (d = 0.41) but made it worse in over a third of cases, and it tends to fail when it turns attention from the task to the self [S, Kluger and DeNisi 1996]. Praise and verdicts are feedback about the self.
- In games, feedback that only says right, wrong or points gave g = 0.26; richer scaffolding gave 0.48 [V, Clark et al. 2016].

The evidence against the no-praise rule, stated fairly: in Outhwaite's sample every highly effective maths app had both explanatory and motivational feedback, and the authors say praise "was not a hindrance" [V]. No app had explanation without praise, so the sample cannot say whether praise is needed. Positive verbal feedback raised free-choice interest overall in Deci's meta-analysis (d = 0.33), but not in children (d = 0.11, not significant) [V]. So the fair summary is that praise for young children is close to neutral for learning and interest, the explanation does the work, and removing praise costs little as long as the consequence is clear and satisfying [I].

### Productive failure (Manu Kapur)

The idea: let learners attempt a problem they cannot yet solve, generate their own partial methods, then teach the standard method. The failed attempts activate what they know and show them what the standard method is for [V, Kapur and Roll]. Kapur is explicit that "not every failure is productive": the task must allow several approaches and partial progress, be challenging without being frustrating, and let learners judge their own attempts from the situation without external feedback [V].

The meta-analysis: 53 studies, 166 comparisons, g = 0.36 in favour of problem solving first, and 0.37 to 0.58 when done as Kapur specifies [V, Sinha and Kapur 2021]. Two limits matter here:

- "Contrasting trends were, however, observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) ... for which effect sizes favored" instruction first [V]. Young children may not have enough prior strategies to generate useful attempts.
- The benefit depends on the consolidation phase. Failure with no follow-up is just failure.

For this studio [I]: use productive failure for 9 to 12, in the form "try to build it your own way, then a character shows a neat way and you compare". For under 7, show first (a character does it once, wordlessly) and keep failure small, local and quickly fixed.

### Desirable difficulties

Bjork's term for conditions that slow learning during practice and improve it later: spacing, mixing problem types, varying conditions and retrieving from memory [S; not opened in this pass]. Spacing is the one the game meta-analyses support directly (multiple sessions; section 2). Mixing has a design consequence: once a mechanic is learned, mix the kinds of problem so the child has to decide what kind it is, not just do the same thing again [I]. Lomas's most engaging conditions gave the slowest learning [S], which is the same trade seen from the other side.

### Why this matters for motivation

**Self-determination theory.** People keep doing what gives them a sense of autonomy, competence and relatedness. In games, felt autonomy and competence predicted enjoyment and wanting to play again [S, Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski 2006]. A verdict from the game undermines both: it takes the judgement away from the child and it marks them as not competent. A consequence the child can read leaves the judgement with the child.

**Rewards.** Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards reduced later free-choice interest (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28). Tangible rewards were more harmful for children than for students [V, Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999]. This meta-analysis has been disputed by behaviourist researchers [S], but the finding for expected tangible rewards on an already interesting task is the most stable part. In classrooms, a gamified course with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower motivation, satisfaction and exam scores than the same course without them (80 university students over 16 weeks) [S, Hanus and Fox 2015]. Stars for redoing problems lowered learning [V, Long and Aleven 2014].

**Dweck and growth mindset: contested.** The original claim is that praising ability makes children avoid challenge after failure and praising effort does not (Mueller and Dweck 1998), and that teaching children that ability grows improves achievement. The current state [S throughout]:

- Meta-analyses of mindset interventions find very small effects on achievement: d = 0.08 (Sisk et al. 2018); d = 0.04 or less after accounting for bias (Macnamara and Burgoyne 2023).
- The large US national study found 0.10 to 0.11 SD on grades for lower-achieving ninth graders (Yeager et al. 2019), and effects depended on the classroom.
- A replication of the praise study (Li and Bates 2019) was reported as a failure; a re-analysis argued the pooled data did replicate it.

So do not justify design decisions by mindset theory. The case against verdicts and praise stands on firmer ground: the feedback literature, self-determination theory and the reward studies. A game-specific finding is worth noting: Refraction's "brain points", which rewarded strategy and effort, increased persistence [S; I could not open the paper], but that is still a reward and is outside this studio's rules.

## 6. Difficulty and progression without levels as rewards

### What the evidence says about difficulty

- **Easier keeps children playing.** In experiments with 10,000 and 70,000 players of Battleship Numberline, children played longer the easier the game was. There was no peak at moderate difficulty. The most engaging settings gave the slowest learning [S, Lomas et al. 2013]. A follow-up found novelty and choice mattered more to motivation than difficulty [S, title and abstract of Lomas et al. 2017].
- **A high success rate does no harm to feelings and may raise practice.** Math Garden's 60%, 75% and 90% experiment [S; see section 3].
- **The "85% rule"** is a mathematical result about a class of learning algorithms doing two-way classification: learning is fastest at about 15.87% error [V, Wilson et al. 2019]. It is not a study of children and should not be quoted as one. It agrees loosely with the other two findings: most attempts should succeed.
- **Structured sequence beats free order.** A gradual increase gave faster, more accurate work than a non-strategic order [V via Outhwaite]. Programmed levelling was necessary for the best maths apps [V].
- **Adaptive against fixed.** No overall difference in a reading game for young children; in a number game the adaptive version was more efficient; lower-attaining children did better with a fixed path and higher-attaining children with an adaptive one [S, Vanbecelaere et al.; V for the last point via Outhwaite]. For a tiny team this is good news: a well-ordered fixed sequence with a few branches gets most of the benefit.
- **Adaptation in practice.** In the Algebra Challenge almost every student needed extra levels beyond the basic 60; some needed five times as many; mastery took 102 levels on average [V]. The spread between children is very large, so the amount of practice must flex even if the order does not.

### Zone of proximal development

Vygotsky's term for the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help [S]. In a game the "help" is the scaffold: a partly built example, a ghost outline, a reduced set of choices, a character who shows the move. The design task is to fade the scaffold, and to bring it back quietly when the child is stuck. Plass notes this has been hard to do well in learning games [V].

### Invisible adaptation

How to adapt without the child seeing a level number or a difficulty verdict [I]:

- Adjust the next problem, never the current one. The child should not see the game give up on them.
- Vary quantity size, number of distractors, how much is pre-built, and whether the scaffold is shown. Do not vary speed.
- After two consequences in a row that did not work, the next problem is one the child has done before with a small change; after several clean ones, add a step.
- Offer help as something in the world: the character scratches its head and tries one piece; a firefly lands on the useful thing. This is the guidance ladder as fiction.
- Keep the model simple: a short per-skill history is enough. Dutch and Californian children of the same age will be at different points in different skills, and a fixed age gate would be wrong for both.

### Child-chosen challenge

Choice raises motivation and learning [S, Cordova and Lepper 1996]. Children do seek harder things when the choice is theirs and visible [I]. Ways to offer it without a menu: three doors of visibly different size; a small, a middling and a tall mountain; a recipe with more parts. Lomas suggests showing in advance how hard something will be so that choosing it is a decision [S]. Montessori classrooms run entirely on chosen work within a prepared range.

### Low floor, high ceiling, wide walls

Papert described Logo as having a low floor and a high ceiling [V, as reported by Resnick and Silverman 2005]. Resnick adds wide walls: "we have put less emphasis on high ceilings and more emphasis on what might be called 'wide walls' ... technologies that support and suggest a wide range of different explorations" [V]. Their other principles that apply here: "make powerful ideas salient, not forced"; "support many paths, many styles"; "make it as simple as possible, and maybe even simpler" [V].

For a learning game with a target skill, wide walls means the same mechanic supports making things, not only solving things [I]: the rods that fit a gap can also build a picture; the commands that solve a maze can also draw; the fractions that cut ice can also split a pizza for friends.

### How week three comes from the same mechanic

Seven ways to deepen without adding a second game or a reward track [I, drawing on the exemplars]:

1. **One new object, then combinations.** Thinkrolls and DragonBox add one element per chapter and then mix them. The number of situations grows much faster than the number of rules.
2. **Reverse the question.** First "what fits here?", later "what was here?" or "make the gap that this fits". Composition becomes decomposition; multiplication becomes division.
3. **Remove a scaffold.** The same task with the outline gone, the count hidden, the rod unmarked.
4. **Constrain.** Lightbot's limited slots force a loop. "Do it with three pieces." Constraints create the need for the next idea.
5. **Fade to symbol.** The same puzzle with numerals on, then with the objects ghosted, then gone (from about 6).
6. **Sandbox.** A place where the same pieces are free to build with. What children make there shows what they understood.
7. **The world accumulates.** Progress is a place that fills up: a garden, a village, a shelf of things made. It is a record, not a currency. It must not count, rank or be lost by staying away.

Unlocking new places can be progression without being a reward if the new place is new work, not a prize for work [I].

## 7. Ages 9 to 12: games this age respects

### What they respect [I, from the exemplars and Gee]

- A real system that behaves truly: orbits, circuits, populations, markets, a programming language.
- Real difficulty and room for expertise; a clever solution should look clever.
- Tools and making; their own design is the output.
- Dry humour, not cuteness. Failure that is funny and not embarrassing.
- Honest notation. They know what a fraction looks like and are insulted by its absence.
- No talking down, no mascot telling them they are amazing.

### What school covers, in general terms

**California, grades 4 to 6 (Common Core mathematics; Next Generation Science Standards)** [S, from the standards as generally known; not re-read in this pass]:

- Grade 4: multi-digit multiplication and division with remainders; fraction equivalence and ordering; adding and subtracting fractions with like denominators; multiplying a fraction by a whole number; decimal notation for tenths and hundredths; angles; area and perimeter; factors and multiples.
- Grade 5: adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators; multiplying fractions; dividing with unit fractions; decimals to thousandths and operations on them; volume; the coordinate plane (first quadrant); numerical expressions and patterns.
- Grade 6: ratios, rates and percent; dividing fractions; negative numbers and the full number line; four-quadrant coordinates; expressions and one-variable equations; area of triangles and polygons, surface area, volume; statistical variability and distributions.
- Science, grades 4 to 5 and into middle school: energy and its transfer; waves; structure and function in organisms; matter as particles too small to see; matter and energy in ecosystems; Earth's systems; Sun, Earth and stars and their patterns; weather and climate; cells; and engineering design throughout (define a problem with criteria and limits, compare solutions, run fair tests).

**Netherlands, groep 6 to 8** [V for the science core goals, which I read; S for the rest]:

- Mathematics (rekenen-wiskunde) is organised in four domains: numbers, proportions (which holds fractions, percentages and ratio together), measurement and geometry, and relationships (tables and graphs) [S, SLO]. Core goal 26: pupils learn to see the structure and connections of whole numbers, decimals, fractions, percentages and ratios and to calculate with them in practical situations [S, SLO]. By the end of groep 8 pupils are expected at reference level 1F, with many reaching 1S [S].
- In practice groep 6 to 8 covers: written multiplication and division; fractions (compare, simplify, add, subtract, multiply); decimals; percentages, including percentage as a fraction, as an operator and as a decimal; ratio and scale; the metric system, area, volume, time and money; maps and scale; tables, bar and line graphs [S].
- Nature and technology (core goals 40 to 46): common plants and animals and how they function in their surroundings; the build of plants, animals and people; investigating materials and physical phenomena "such as light, sound, electricity, force, magnetism and temperature"; describing weather and climate by temperature, rainfall and wind; relating how products work to their form and materials; designing, carrying out and evaluating solutions to technical problems; and how the Earth's position relative to the Sun gives seasons and day and night [V, SLO core goals 2006].
- The core goals are being revised; draft new goals for Dutch and mathematics were published in 2023 [S]. Check the current text before fixing content.

The overlap is large: fractions, decimals, percentages and ratio as one connected family; area and volume; coordinates and graphs; early equations; fair tests; energy, forces, light, sound and electricity; ecosystems; Earth and Sun; and design.

## 8. What fails

**Quiz with a skin.** The content could be swapped for any other; play stops for the question. Children choose the integrated game seven to one when free to choose [V, Habgood and Ainsworth]. Most evaluated early maths apps are practice of isolated skills, and none set the maths in a context or applied it [V, Outhwaite et al. 2023]. Few preschool apps give guidance or scaffolded feedback, and fewer than a fifth have adjustable content [S, Callaghan and Reich 2018]. GraphoGame, a pick-the-match phonics game, shows no overall effect on reading [S]. Be precise about the failure: simple drill does produce gains on tests of the drilled skill [V, Clark: g 0.53]. What it does not produce is interest, understanding or transfer.

**Speed drills.** The honest picture is mixed. Timed practice does build fact fluency in the short term, and differences from untimed practice fade [S]. The widely repeated claim that timed tests cause maths anxiety rests on weak evidence [S]. Under time pressure working memory is loaded and anxious children do worse [S]. So speed drills are not shown to be harmful in general. They are ruled out here by the studio's constraints, and the cost is fluency. The substitute is many short, varied, untimed encounters spread over weeks, which the spacing evidence supports, and games in which knowing the fact makes play smoother without a clock [I].

**Reward loops on top of worksheets.** Rewards reduce later interest, most in children [V, Deci et al.]. A gamified course ended with lower motivation and exam scores [S, Hanus and Fox]. Stars for redoing problems reduced learning [V, Long and Aleven]. Gamification does show short-term cognitive gains (g 0.49), with unstable motivational effects [S, Sailer and Homner], so the pattern is a quick gain and a long cost. Prodigy shows where the model leads commercially [V, Fairplay complaint]. Manipulative design (pressure to keep playing, to buy, to watch) is common in apps used by 3- to 5-year-olds; about a third of 160 children had manipulative advertising in at least one of their most-used apps [V, Radesky et al. 2022, for the advertising table; overall prevalence S].

**Decoration that competes.** Realistic and heavily dressed games teach less [V, Clark; Wouters]. Hotspots and games inside stories distract [V, Takacs]. Free apps scored lower for engagement in the learning process because of "distracting enhancements" [V, Meyer et al. 2021]. Perceptually rich manipulatives transfer worse [S, Carbonneau].

**Too little guidance.** Unassisted discovery loses [S, Alfieri]. Only 23% of children found the control-of-variables strategy alone [V, Klahr and Nigam]. Sandbox games show weak, biased evidence for curriculum learning [S, Minecraft review]. Problem solving first does not help the youngest [V, Sinha and Kapur].

**Too much guidance.** The benefit of explanatory feedback disappears once children can do the task [V via Outhwaite]. Help that can be requested without limit is often used to skip the thinking; this is known as gaming the system in the tutoring literature [S; not opened]. In a game the usual form is "tap everything until it works": if wrong tries cost nothing and tell nothing, children stop reasoning [I]. The consequence must carry information, which is the opposite of both a buzzer and a free retry.

**Voice-over instruction.** Children do not attend to audio prompts without visual support [S, Hiniker et al. 2015]. Children under about 5 often cannot follow in-app prompts of any kind without an adult, and 4- to 6-year-olds can follow non-text prompts [V, a study of 2- to 8-year-olds summarising Hiniker]. Tutorials did not improve engagement in simple games [V, Andersen et al. 2012]. Among the evaluated maths apps, seven gave instructions that could not be repeated and five gave none [V, Outhwaite]. Spoken words are still valuable as content (number words, feeling words, letter sounds), attached to the object at the moment it is touched. They should not be the instructions.

**No bridge.** Game-only learning is about half as large as game-plus-teaching in one meta-analysis [V, Wouters]. DragonBox mastery did not show on paper in one study [V]. Television about feelings needed a talking parent [S].

**Believing your own numbers.** App effects shrink from 0.94 to 0.07 as the study gets larger and the test more independent [V, Gilbert]. A quality rubric did not predict effects [V, Gilbert]. A well-designed, wordless, spatial maths program showed a negligible effect at scale [V, Rutherford]. None of these games should be described to parents as proven to raise attainment. What can be said is that they are built on representations and mechanics with evidence behind them.
