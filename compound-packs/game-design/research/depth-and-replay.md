# Depth and replay without hooks

A research dossier for about 20 touch-screen cartridges in three age bands (2 to 4, 4 to 6, 9 to 12).

Question: with no scores, coins, streaks, timers, rare drops, counters or punishment for leaving, what makes a child want to come back to a game for weeks, and how do the best games do it?

## How to read the evidence marks

- **[V]** I opened and read the source in this research pass (a paper, a designer's own article, an interview with the designer, an official guideline).
- **[S]** I have it only from a secondary summary (a search-result abstract, a review, an encyclopedia entry, someone's notes on a talk).
- **[I]** My own inference from the above. Treat these as proposals to test, not findings.

Things I could not open, so nothing below rests on them directly: the video of "Juice it or lose it" (Jonasson and Purho) and of "The art of screenshake" (Nijman), both known here only through written notes; Koster's book and slides (file too large, known through a review I read); Fullerton's book; Schell's book (one lens read from a reproduction); the Ofcom "Children's Media Lives 2025" PDF (refused the request); Roger Ebert's Miyazaki interview (refused); the PC Gamer pieces on Townscaper and Kerbal Space Program (truncated); Zagal, Björk and Lewis's paper (refused); the GDC videos for Florence, Monument Valley, A Short Hike, LocoRoco and Rosen's procedural animation talk (written summaries only).

One honest gap up front: I found no published study that tracks how long children keep returning to any of the hook-free children's apps named here. The claim that Toca Boca, Sago Mini or Pok Pok are "played for weeks" rests on the designers' testimony, on reviews, and on commercial longevity (Toca Boca had sold "more than 40 million apps" since March 2011 when PocketGamer.biz wrote about it, and over 50 million downloads by the later Gulf News piece [V]). The one piece of survey data is for Minecraft. So the rules below are built from mechanism (why a thing should keep working) more than from measured retention.

## One-page summary: fifteen rules, each with its strongest evidence

1. **Build the toy first. The core touch must be fun with no goal at all.** Schell's Lens of the Toy asks "If my game had no goal, would it be fun at all?" and whether people "want to start interacting with it, even before they know what to do" [S, reproduction of the lens]. Gabler and Gray's first prototyping rule is "Build the Toy First" [V]. Miyamoto on Mario 64: "this didn't really have to be a game"; Iwata: "if it's fun at the very core, the rest somehow works itself out" [V].

2. **Depth comes from parts that combine, not from more content.** Zoombinis gets 625 individuals from four attributes with five values each, and the designers watched children still making and naming paper Zoombinis "days later" [V]. Cook: loops (act, see, update your model, act again) keep giving value when repeated; arcs (a story or a level consumed once) lose value "substantially" on repeat [V]. Resnick and Silverman judge a kit by "diversity of outcomes" and call it a failure if a child "feels that s/he is 'done'" after one project [V].

3. **Wanting to play again is predicted by felt autonomy and felt competence, and controls that need no thought are the entry price.** Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006): in-game autonomy and competence each predicted enjoyment and preference for future play; intuitive controls worked through those two; when Zelda was preferred to A Bug's Life the difference was explained by autonomy and competence [V].

4. **Do not reward play. A promised reward makes the activity less wanted later.** Lepper, Greene and Nisbett: preschoolers who drew "in order to obtain" a certificate later spent "significantly less time" drawing in free play than children who got no award or a surprise one [V, Lepper's 1973 report]. Stålberg on Townscaper: "When you keep score, you play to the score... You stop watching the actual world you're building" [S].

5. **Characters with opinions are the cheapest depth and the fastest liveliness.** In Toca Kitchen the cat is delighted by raw fish and the girl is disgusted; in Toca Hair Salon clients frown or grin at what you did [S, reviews]. The MDA paper's own worked example for 3 to 7 year olds tunes a game "not for 'winning' or 'competition' but for having the baby express emotions like surprise, fear, and anticipation" [V].

6. **Low floor, wide walls; for 9 to 12 add a high ceiling.** Resnick and Silverman [V]. Sakurai built Kirby so "you could clear the game just by inhaling and exhaling" while copy abilities gave skilled players more [V]. Kouno on LocoRoco: "as few buttons as possible", and "something that draws you in initially, and then gets more interesting as you invest more time" [V].

7. **Shape every play cycle in four beats: introduce, develop, twist, conclude.** Hayashida's kishōtenketsu structure for Mario levels: learn the mechanic, a slightly harder case, "something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting", then show mastery [V]. The twist is where the comedy and the short "movie" belong.

8. **Every touch answers on touch-down, with sound and motion, and the answer is bigger than the touch.** Sesame Workshop: children "expect immediate feedback", tap harder and repeatedly until they see it, so register "upon touch rather than on lift", and give each action a sound [V]. The juice definition: "A juicy game feels alive and responds to everything you do; tons of cascading action and response for minimal user input" [S].

9. **Liveliness comes from the child causing things and from comedy, never from pressure.** Two-year-olds' humour is misusing objects, strange sayings, invented words and taboo topics; three-year-olds add mislabelling and tricks (Hoicka et al., 671 children) [V]. House House: "everything from pulling up a carrot to honking at someone feels like 'success' when your goal is just to bother people" [V].

10. **Hide things. Never count them.** Gingold on Miyamoto's worlds: "hide-and-reveal arouses an exploratory impulse" [V]. Hidden Folks has hundreds of pokeable things (over 500 by the makers' count [S]) and cut its timed, counted areas because they threatened the relaxed feel [V]. Radesky et al. classify "stickers, trophies, and leaderboards" shown at decision points as lures, a manipulative design found in 45% of preschoolers' apps [V].

11. **Make it the child's place: it keeps what they made and it waits.** Gingold: a miniature garden is "complete", bounded, has "an inner life of its own" and feels "emotionally safe" [V]. Animal Crossing shows both halves: a town shared across family members who play at different times, and a design that "pretty much requires that you come" to keep it clean [V, Eguchi]. Take the first half only.

12. **Give every cycle an ending the child causes, and never start the next one for them.** Radesky: auto-advance with no pause or exit is a navigation constraint (46% of apps) [V]. Hiniker's Coco's Videos: with autoplay, preschoolers were less likely to make their planned transition away [S]. The UK Children's Code asks for a pause "at any time without losing their progress" and for continuing to be an "active choice" [V].

13. **For 9 to 12: real difficulty, real systems, more than one solution.** Papert: children say "hard fun" and "they mean it's fun because it's hard"; they "prefer things that are hard, as long as they are also interesting" [V]. Olson: when asked what makes a game worth replaying, "challenge was a key factor"; fifth-graders were moved more by challenge than by competition (Greenberg, cited by Olson) [V].

14. **Movies are short, caused by the child, star what the child made, and are staged like silent film.** Florence used touch mechanics "to evoke a feeling or an idea" [V]. Shaun the Sheep's makers: "the great films do it with expressions", and children "sit very still... concentrating" [V]. Sesame: children "become impatient with uninterruptible audio prompts" [V]. Rosen's procedural character runs on 13 keyframes [S].

15. **Test every design against week three.** Koster's claim, as reviewed by Cook: a pattern that is too simple is "immediately" understood and filed "as a solved problem" [V, review]. So ask of each cartridge: on day 15, what can the child do, find or make that they could not on day 1? If the only answer is "the same thing again", it needs more combinations, a skill with a ceiling, or secrets.

## 1. Where depth and replay come from when extrinsic hooks are removed

### What the design sources actually claim

**MDA (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek).** Games are "more like artifacts than media": "the content of a game is its behavior, not the media that streams out of it." The designer sets mechanics (rules and data), which produce dynamics (run-time behaviour), which produce aesthetics (the feelings). The player meets them in the reverse order. The paper lists eight kinds of fun: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission (pastime). It notes each game mixes several: "The Sims: Discovery, Fantasy, Expression, Narrative" [V]. For this studio the useful move is to drop "challenge by scoring" and deliberately pick from the other seven. The paper says expression "comes from dynamics that encourage individual users to leave their mark" [V].

**Self-determination theory (Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski 2006).** Four studies. Study 1 (89 undergraduates, 20 minutes of Super Mario 64): autonomy and competence each predicted enjoyment and preference for future play; only competence predicted the behavioural choice to keep playing; intuitive controls related to outcomes only through autonomy and competence. Study 2 (50 students, Zelda: Ocarina of Time versus A Bug's Life): the preference for Zelda was accounted for by higher autonomy and competence. Study 4 adds relatedness for multiplayer games [V]. The paper expects autonomy to be raised by "flexibility over movement and strategies, choice over tasks and goals" and by rewards "structured so as to provide feedback rather than to control" [V]. Limits: adult samples, short sessions, self-report. The later model paper (Przybylski, Rigby, Ryan 2010) argues that appeal comes from need satisfaction rather than from content [S].

**Koster, A Theory of Fun.** Fun is the feeling of the brain learning a pattern; "Fun is just another word for learning" and "Boredom is the opposite of learning" [S]. A pattern that is too hard reads as noise; one that is too simple is solved and discarded [V, Cook's review]. Consequence for replay: a game stays fun only while there is something left to learn in it. That is an argument for systems (which hold many patterns) over single activities (which hold one).

**Cook, Loops and Arcs.** A loop: "The player starts with a mental model that prompts them to apply an action to the game system and in return receives feedback that updates their mental model." An arc "is a broken loop you exit immediately". Loops "deliver value through the act of being exercised"; for arcs "the value of repeated exposure... drops substantially" [V]. A cartridge meant to last weeks is mostly loop. A "movie" is an arc, and should therefore be cheap to make and vary with what the loop produced.

**Schell.** Lens 15, the Lens of the Toy (quoted in rule 1) [S]. Schell separates "fun to play" from "fun to play with".

**Fullerton, Game Design Workshop.** A playcentric process: set player-experience goals, prototype, playtest, revise; analyse a game as formal, dramatic and dynamic (system) elements [S; book not opened]. The transferable part is the process claim, which Toca Boca and Sago Mini repeat from practice: paper prototypes in kindergartens, "three or four" child tests per title [V, PocketGamer.biz and Gulf News].

**Swink, Game Feel.** Game feel is "the sensation of controlling a digital object". Six parts: input, response, context, polish, metaphor, rules. "Constraints define sensation"; polish includes "sprays or dustlings of particles where things hit or interact, screen shake, view angle shifts" [V]. Swink's point for small teams: the moment-to-moment steering is most of what the player experiences, so it is where the first weeks of work go.

**Jonasson and Purho, Juice it or lose it.** They take a grey Breakout clone and, without touching the rules, add in order: tweening, colour, stretch and squash on the paddle and ball, sound, particles, a ball trail, screen shake, and eyes on the paddle [S, Townsend's written breakdown]. The quoted definition is in rule 8 [S, Cornell lecture notes]. A critic's caveat from the same breakdown: effects can drown each other and can misinform the player about what an object is [S].

**Nijman, The art of screenshake.** About 30 small changes turn a dull shooter into a forceful one: bigger effects, camera lerp, screen shake, a pause of a frame or two on impact [S; talk not opened]. His stated principle, as summarised: if one button is most of the player's contact with the world, make pressing it matter [S].

**Eiserloh, Juicing your cameras with math.** "Camera shake is like salt." Keep a "trauma" value from 0 to 1, add to it on impacts, let it decay, and shake by trauma squared or cubed, so small events barely move the camera and large ones do [V, slide text].

**Gabler, Gray and others, How to prototype a game in under 7 days.** "Build the Toy First"; "Complexity Is Not Necessary for Fun"; "Make it juicy": "A juicy game element will bounce and wiggle and squirt"; and against over-reliance on polish, "Heavy Theming Will Not Salvage Bad Design" [V].

**Gingold, Miniature Gardens and Magic Crayons.** Reads Miyamoto's "miniature garden" as a design principle: a small, bounded, complete world with "an inner life of its own" that "surprise[s], delight[s], and invite[s] participation"; overview plus ground-level detail; and safety: "It must feel safe to experiment with a miniature garden." He lists "manipulation" as a basic pleasure: "being a cause; putting your hand in the water and splashing about; something responds to me" [V]. He quotes Will Wright on consistent abstraction: children "fill in the blanks really well" when everything is abstracted to the same level [V].

**Resnick and Silverman, construction kits for kids.** Ten principles. The ones that matter here: "Low Floor and Wide Walls" ("a space to explore, not a collection of specific activities"); "Support Many Paths, Many Styles" (the group that built a refreshment stand before its Ferris wheel; "patterners" and "dramatists" both need a way in); "Make it as Simple as Possible, and Maybe Even Simpler" (the cut-down Cricket beat the bigger brick) [V].

**Papert, Does Easy Do It?** Quoted in rule 13. He also calls it "downright immoral to trick children into learning" by hiding it inside a game [V]. Relevant to the 6+ maths cartridges: show the maths as the material, not as a toll gate.

### The ten sources of depth, each with what it needs to work

1. **Systems with emergent combinations.** A small set of objects, each with a few properties, where any two do something together. Zoombinis (attributes), Scribblenauts (a dictionary of objects with properties, built on a data-driven object engine [S]), Townscaper (one click, many rule-driven results [V]), Toca Kitchen (ingredient x tool x eater [S]). The cost is in the interaction table, not the art. [I] For a cartridge, plan a grid of N objects by M verbs and require every cell to produce a visible, audible, distinct result; 6 by 5 is already 30 discoveries and the pairs and sequences multiply it.

2. **Expressive creation.** MDA's "expression" [V]. Works when the result is visibly the child's, differs from other children's (Resnick's diversity test [V]), and persists. Toca Hair Salon, Townscaper, Minecraft creative, Scratch.

3. **Mastery of a physical skill with a high ceiling.** SDT competence [V]; Swink's "constraint is the mother of skill" [V]. LocoRoco (tilt and bump with one input) [V], Kirby (float anywhere, then abilities) [V], Katamari, Alto. With no score, the ceiling must be visible in the world: the tower is taller, the pour is cleaner, the bird threads the gap. [I]

4. **Collection by discovery, without counters.** The finds live in the world as things (the shell is on the shelf because the child put it there), not as filled slots in an album. An album with empty silhouettes is a lure by Radesky's definition [V for the definition; I for the application].

5. **World persistence and change: "my place".** Gingold's garden [V]; Animal Crossing's shared town [V]. The world remembers the child's actions and may mirror the season in how it looks. It does not decay, tidy itself, or comment on absence.

6. **Characters with personality and relationships.** Sesame Workshop builds on a "feeling of friendship" with familiar characters [V]. Zoombinis' designers found an "understated quality which makes it possible to imagine many different personalities for them" [V]. Radesky notes the same attachment is what parasocial pressure exploits [V]; the line is in section 5.

7. **Secrets and hidden interactions.** Hidden Folks (hundreds of interactions) [V]; Townscaper's "recipes" that reward curious placement with arches, stairs and gardens [V]; Pok Pok's Busy Board "hidden functions and surprises" [S]; Sago Mini's "delightful hidden surprises" [S].

8. **Variation and procedural generation.** Zoombinis: "Each time, the puzzle is reconfigured to require a different solution" [V]. Useful for puzzles and for scenery; not a substitute for depth. Random variety of outcomes for the same action is fine as texture; a desirable outcome gated behind chance is a variable reward (section 5).

9. **Self-set goals.** Eguchi: collecting everything is "just one play style" [V]. Stevens et al., cited by Olson: two girls in Zoo Tycoon, one maximising function, one "absorbed by design principles and beautification" [V]. The design job is to make many goals possible and legible without stating any.

10. **Role play and story.** Toca's "toys, not games": "no points, there is no score, it's just play" [V]; Sago Mini's team watches "kids in playtesting creating their own narratives" [V]. The app supplies place, props and reacting characters; the child supplies plot.

## 2. By age band

### Ages 2 to 4

**What they can do with a touch screen.**
- Tap is "the most intuitive and foundational" gesture. Dragging works but children lift the finger, so "support partial completion". Swipe works if a visual cue shows where. Avoid making pinch essential; avoid tilt and shake on a tablet; expect accidental multi-touch; double tap makes them think the app is broken [V, Sesame Workshop].
- They rest wrists on the bottom edge and trigger whatever is there. Hot spots must be "large and adequately isolated". Children hold tablets in landscape. Touchable things should look touchable and move a little; things that look touchable must be touchable [V, Sesame].
- "There are vast differences between 2-, 3-, and 4-year-olds' dexterity" [V, Sesame]. In YouTube videos of real use, over 90% of two-year-olds showed at least moderate ability with a tablet [S, Hourcade et al.]. Two to three year olds managed tap, drag, one-finger rotate and two-finger scale in a lab study [S, Nacher et al.].
- They do not read. Audio instructions alone are ignored; pair any cue with a visual one. Offer an idle hint after about 6 to 8 seconds without input in a game [V, Sesame].

**Attention and repetition.**
- Focused attention during free play lengthens steadily from age 1 to 3.5 [S, Ruff and Lawson]. US 2 to 4 year olds average about 21 minutes of gaming a day across everything [S, Common Sense census via Kidscreen]. [I] A cartridge will get a few minutes at a time; a whole cycle should fit in one to three.
- They want the same thing again and get more from it each time. Three-year-olds learned words from the same three storybooks read repeatedly and not from nine different ones [V, Horst et al.; n=16]. Preschoolers shown one Blue's Clues episode five days running kept watching and joined in more each day [S, Crawley et al.]. [I] At this age "week three" should look almost like day one, with the child faster, surer, and trying one new thing.

**What is funny.** From Hoicka and colleagues' paper, which reviews earlier studies and reports a parent survey of 671 children: in the first year, peekaboo, tickling, funny faces, funny voices, chasing; from one, misusing objects ("put cup on head") and bodily silliness; at two, "misusing objects, saying strange things, inventing words, and addressing taboo topics (e.g., poo)"; at three, mislabelling, tricks, playing with social rules, "scaring others" ("hides behind couch and jumps up saying 'boo'") [V]. McGhee's stage model says the same in outline: toddlers first make jokes with objects in the wrong place, then with wrong names [S]. Sago Mini ships "farting unicorns or pickle milkshakes" on exactly this basis [V].

**Schemas of play.** Athey's schema theory describes repeated action patterns: transporting, positioning (lining up), rotation, trajectory (dropping, throwing), enveloping (wrapping, covering), enclosure (fencing in), connecting, transforming (mixing), orientation [S, practitioner summary quoting Athey]. No ages are attached in the source I read. [I] These are a ready list of verbs for a wordless toy: each maps to one touch gesture (carry, line up, spin, fling, cover, surround, join, mix, tip over) and each is something a toddler will do fifty times unprompted.

**Games that hold up.**

- **Sago Mini (several titles).** *Minute to minute:* tap or drag a character through a scene; each object does one funny thing. *Week three versus day one:* the child goes straight to favourite gags and narrates. *Mechanism:* dense one-touch surprises aimed at toddler humour, usable with "no help required". [V interview; no retention data]
- **Pok Pok Playroom.** *Minute to minute:* flip switches, ring bells, join blobs, drive around a town; no goals. The designers say "kids never go play with a toy the same way" and that parents ask "what's the point of this?" until a child shows them. *Week three:* the Busy Board is meant to give a two-year-old plain cause and effect and an older child magnetism and gravity; "as kids dive deeper... they'll be rewarded with more play." Musical toys follow fixed rules ("the color blue is always a C"). *Mechanism:* one object that answers at several levels of understanding, with strict consistency so discoveries stay true. [V] Caveat: Pok Pok also ships new toys every 4 to 6 weeks [S], which a fixed cartridge cannot do, and it was designed to be quiet ("parents shouldn't need to mute the app in a restaurant" [S]), which is the quality the owner found too quiet.
- **Metamorphabet.** *Minute to minute:* poke a letter; it becomes a thing that starts with that letter, then another (A grows antlers, becomes an arch, ambles) [S]. Smith built "a kind of rhythm: event, silence, event" [V]. *Week three:* limited; it is an arc with 26 parts and is finished when seen. *Mechanism:* transformation as the reward for a poke, paced with rests. Holds up as a model for single interactions, not for weeks of play.
- **Toca Kitchen.** See 4 to 6; three-year-olds can already feed and watch reactions.
- **LEGO DUPLO World.** Build, explore and role-play scenes with multi-touch for two players [S, store listing and LEGO announcement]. I found no designer account of its mechanics, so I draw nothing from it beyond "two hands, two people, one screen".

### Ages 4 to 6

**What they can do.** Tap and drag-and-drop improve significantly in speed and accuracy between 3 and 6 [S, Vatavu et al., 89 children]. Tracing and drawing are loved but fingers still lift [V, Sesame]. Still no reading. Avoid double tap at 5 and under [V, Sesame].

**How they think and play.** Role play and pretend are the main mode; the "dramatist" style Resnick describes [V]. Sorting, ordering and simple deduction are in reach: Zoombinis' designers place "the most basic principles of logical thinking" at "around age 4" [V]. [I] Tricking and surprising others become funny because children now model what another mind expects, which is why reacting characters work so well here.

**What is funny.** Everything from 2 to 4, plus wrong names, nonsense words, tricks, mild naughtiness, slapstick with a victim who overreacts [V Hoicka for the first four; V House House for the last]. Memorised riddles and wordplay come later, around 6 to 8 [S, McGhee].

**Games that hold up.**

- **Toca Hair Salon.** *Minute to minute:* wash, cut, shave, grow back, colour and dry a client's hair; the client pulls faces, groans or beams [S]. "No points, there is no score, it's just play" [V]. *Week three:* the child is doing characters and intentions (a terrible haircut on purpose, a rainbow for the bear), not learning tools. *Mechanism:* a reversible material (hair grows back) plus a client with visible opinions makes every action safe and every result a small scene.
- **Toca Kitchen.** *Minute to minute:* pick an ingredient, prepare it some way or none, feed it to one of a few characters; they gulp, sneeze, cough, faint or spit [S]. *Week three:* testing combinations on purpose to find what each eater hates. *Mechanism:* ingredient x preparation x eater, with the eater's reaction as the only feedback; disgust is as good an outcome as delight.
- **Toca Life / Toca Boca World.** *Minute to minute:* drag characters and props around dollhouse scenes; things open, wear, stack, eat [S]. *Week three:* long-running stories with a fixed cast; some children record them. *Mechanism:* persistent places and props with no script. Caveat: the current product is funded by in-app purchases of places, so take the dollhouse, not the model. [S]
- **Hidden Folks.** *Minute to minute:* scan a dense hand-drawn scene and poke it: doors open, blinds roll up, bushes get cut, crops grow, and every poke makes a mouth-made sound. *Week three:* returning to areas to poke what was skipped. The makers kept things "added for the sake of pure fun" and cut the timed, counted areas. They also found that making some things interactive raised the expectation that everything is [V and S]. *Mechanism:* looking closely is rewarded by hand-made comic responses. Caveat: the targets strip uses text clues; a younger version needs picture targets or none.
- **Untitled Goose Game** (upper end of the band, or with a parent). *Minute to minute:* waddle, honk, grab, drag; people notice, chase, tidy up, give up. The team found "it was funnier when the goose tried to steal things when people weren't looking", so they gave villagers limited vision and discrete states. The music jumps "to a higher intensity... or to just pause" with the action. "The goose is never really that bad" [V]. *Week three:* inventing pranks outside the list. *Mechanism:* a few verbs against characters who have routines, attention and overreactions. Caveat: it has a written to-do list; the mechanism works without it.
- **LocoRoco.** The shoulder buttons tilt the world [S]; blobs roll, merge, split and sing in an invented language [V], and stack up because Kouno "wanted to use AI to make people laugh" [S]. He "definitely wanted the game to be accessible to children" [V]. *Mechanism:* a soft, singing crowd as the thing you steer; depth through exploration.
- **Kirby.** Clearable with two basic actions; depth by choice [V]. *Mechanism:* a floor so low nobody is stuck, with optional powers that change how you play.
- **Katamari Damacy.** Roll a ball; smaller things stick; the ball grows until the things that blocked you become things you collect [I from play knowledge; the Takahashi interview I read does not describe mechanics]. *Mechanism:* growth you can see in the world, which is escalation without a number. Caveat: the original has timers and size targets.
- **Townscaper.** Tap to add a block, tap to remove; the system turns blocks into houses, arches, stairs, gardens. "More of a toy than a game" [V]. *Week three:* learning the hidden recipes and building toward a picture in the head. *Mechanism:* one input, rule-driven surprise, consistent rules that can be learned by watching.
- **Tearaway.** The player's fingers poke into a paper world and the player draws and cuts decorations that the world then uses [S]. *Mechanism:* the child's own drawing appears inside the story.

### Ages 9 to 12

See section 3 for what holds this age. The games:

- **Minecraft creative mode.** In a survey of 753 Melbourne parents it was the dominant game for ages 3 to 12, played by over two thirds of 9 to 12 year olds, about half usually with someone else [S, Mavoa et al.]. *Minute to minute:* place and break blocks. *Week three:* a project with a plan. *Mechanism:* a uniform, countable material with which anything can be approximated, in a world that keeps it.
- **Scratch.** Core users are 8 to 16, peaking at 12 [S]. Built on "low floor, wide walls" and on making things "that are meaningful to them" [V]. *Mechanism:* the child makes the game. Papert argued for exactly this: "encourage children to become game designers themselves" [V].
- **Zoombinis.** Twelve puzzles, each regenerated on every pass, four difficulty levels, no instructions: "just a challenging obstacle... behaving in its own peculiar way". Wrong guesses "provide the very evidence needed to solve the puzzle" [V]. *Week three:* systematic strategies replace guessing. *Mechanism:* deduction puzzles where the material answers (the troll rejects the pizza and says which way it was wrong), regenerated so the skill, not the answer, is what is learned. Caveat: the original leaves Zoombinis behind after too many errors, which is a loss mechanic.
- **The Incredible Machine.** Fix a broken contraption with a given set of parts; the free-form mode, a late addition made by exposing the level editor, was praised as the best feature [S]. *Mechanism:* a physics parts kit with puzzles as a tutorial for the sandbox.
- **World of Goo.** Grew from a seven-day toy, Tower of Goo [V for the method, S for the lineage]. *Mechanism:* a building material that wobbles and can fail.
- **Scribblenauts.** Summon any noun; objects carry properties and interact [S]. *Mechanism:* a very wide object table. Caveat: Radesky et al. flag Scribblenauts Remix for offering "a gold crown for a repetitive gameplay" [V]; the reward layer was not needed.
- **Baba Is You.** The rules are blocks in the level; push them to change the rules. Teikari designs by "thinking of cool or amusing combinations... and then working backwards", aiming for "that one neat trick" where "the seemingly impossible turns out to be fairly simple" [V]. *Mechanism:* a tiny rule language with surprising consequences. Caveat: uses written words, which is fine from about 8 but ties the game to a language.
- **Zachtronics games (SpaceChem and its successors).** Any machine that produces the output counts as solved; puzzles are designed without a single intended solution [S]. Barth noted the game gives few rewards beyond story and the next level, and built GIF export after seeing a player make one [V]. *Mechanism:* open-ended construction where the reward is watching your own machine run, and showing it. Caveat: the histograms comparing your solution with other players' are a soft leaderboard and need the network; leave them out.
- **Human Resource Machine.** Program a little worker with a few instructions; optional size and speed challenges per level [S]. *Mechanism:* optional, self-chosen optimisation of something that already works.
- **Kerbal Space Program.** Build a rocket, launch, fail spectacularly, rebuild [S]. *Mechanism:* failure that is funny and informative. Too large for a cartridge; the loop is the lesson.
- **A Short Hike.** A tiny open world designed so that "your curiosity [is] rewarded" and so that pacing survives the player "going the exact opposite of the obvious route" [V]. *Mechanism:* small, dense, climbable in any order. It is an arc: a few hours, then done.
- **Monument Valley, Florence, Journey.** Short authored arcs with no fail states [V for Florence and Journey, V-thin for Monument Valley]. Loved, finished in an evening, rarely replayed. Models for the "movies", not for weeks of play.
- **Mini Metro, Dorfromantik.** The main modes score and end on failure; both have creative or endless modes with no score and no loss [S]. [I] These modes were added to games whose interest was built on the pressure, and some players find them empty without it. That is a warning: removing the score from a score-driven design does not leave a toy behind. Design the toy first.
- **Alto's Odyssey Zen Mode.** Removes "goals, points, tricks, and fail states" [S]. Pleasant for minutes; I found no evidence it is played for weeks.
- **Animal Crossing.** Holds up as the model for "my place" and for sharing a world between family members who play at different times [V]. Does not hold up under the constraints: real-time appointments, shops, collections with catalogues, and upkeep that "requires that you come" [V].

## 3. Ages 9 to 12: what holds them without scores or competition

**What the research says they want.**
- Olson (survey of 1,254 pupils aged 12 to 14, plus focus groups and a review): fun, competition and challenge lead for boys; "roughly equal proportions of boys and girls were strongly motivated by creativity ('I like to create my own world') and curiosity ('I like to learn new things')"; more than half liked learning new things from games; 36% of boys and 30% of girls liked teaching others how to play; "an easy game that does not require much time or focus to beat is not as much fun" [V]. Note the sample is at the top edge of the band.
- Greenberg et al., as cited by Olson: competition mattered more to eighth and eleventh graders than to fifth graders, "who were more motivated by challenge" [V as cited]. That is good news for a no-competition design aimed at 9 to 12.
- Funk et al., cited by Olson (17 children in grades 4 to 6): pride in accomplishment, "I feel like I actually did something right", and also frustration that made them stop when a game was too hard [V as cited]. So: real difficulty, with a way back in.
- Olson on "possibility spaces": open-ended games with multiple solutions support self-expression; players enjoy finding and exploiting a system's quirks (the 12-year-old who found a faster way to get taxi fares) [V].
- Ofcom's qualitative work reports that for this age Roblox and Minecraft are largely about being with friends [S; report not opened]. A single-device, no-network cartridge cannot offer that. [I] What it can offer is something worth showing to the person in the room: a machine that runs, a town, a short film, a solved thing.

**What follows for design [I unless marked].**
1. **Systems to optimise for their own satisfaction.** Let any working solution stand, then make better ones visibly better: fewer parts, smoother run, taller, faster. Show the property as an instrument on the thing itself (a part count on the workbench, a height against a ruler). An instrument describes the current object. It becomes a score when the game stores a best, sets a target, or celebrates a lower number. Keep it an instrument. This is a judgement call for the owner; the constraint says no scores and allows numerals from about 6.
2. **Puzzles with several solutions and an "aha".** Teikari's method: find an amusing interaction, then build the level that needs it [V].
3. **Simulation and tinkering with funny failure.** The bridge sags and the truck slides off; the rocket pirouettes. Nothing is lost, the build is still there, one tap to try again.
4. **Building that persists and can be shown.** A replay or a looping view of the child's own machine is the hook-free version of sharing (Barth's GIF insight [V]).
5. **Strategy with visible consequences.** Mini Metro's network clogging is legible at a glance; keep that legibility and drop the game-over.
6. **Story and humour pitched up, not down.** Dry, a bit absurd, characters with flaws. Tweens are "age aspirational" and prefer characters somewhat older than themselves [S].
7. **Real difficulty.** Papert [V]. Do not auto-solve, do not hint unasked. A hint should be something the child goes and gets.

**What they find babyish and reject.** I found the claim in marketing-research summaries (tweens "reject all things babyish") [S], not in a study that lists features. The list below is therefore inference from the sources above and should be checked with real 9 to 12 year olds: praise for trivial actions; a mascot that explains; no possibility of being wrong; one right answer; rounded pastel baby styling; slow unskippable animations; forced tutorials; anything that looks like schoolwork wearing a costume (Papert's "Shavian reversal": school's content with a game's wrapper keeps the worst of both [V]). What reads as respect: tools that look like tools, numbers and units as part of the material, consequences that are allowed to be large, and humour that is not explained.

## 4. Short animated scenes, story and emotion

### What works in wordless storytelling

- **Expression carries it.** Aardman on Shaun the Sheep: "the great films do it [just] with expressions". The writers had to "think about how we tell that story in a visual way"; the "emotional things" were hardest without words. Children "sit very still... concentrating on what's happening" [V]. Wordlessness began as a budget limit on the TV series [V], which is this studio's situation too.
- **Invented speech.** Pingu's "Penguinese" lets toddlers follow feeling without language; episodes are 5 minutes [S]. LocoRoco sings in a made-up language so it works everywhere [V]. Hidden Folks' sounds are all made by mouth [V]. A synthesised babble with clear intonation (question, delight, grumble) is within reach of a tone.js voice. [I]
- **Let the audience do the sum.** Stanton: the audience "wants to work for their meal"; characters have a "spine", one thing they are always after [S]. For a cartridge character: one want, visible at all times (the cat wants the fish; the baker wants a full shelf). [I]
- **Mechanics as feeling.** Florence: "The mechanics... were used to evoke a feeling or an idea"; repeating a mechanic later in a changed form shows that a character has changed; the touch screen "can be very powerful" for this [V].
- **An intensity curve.** Journey was planned as a curve of emotional intensity over three acts: "This huge change of emotional intensity, this creates catharsis" [V]. Chen prototypes around music first because it is "the most effective and powerful medium that can create emotion" [S]. The team also removed everything that distracted from feeling: no HUD, no names, no text chat [V].
- **Rhythm with rests.** Metamorphabet's "event, silence, event" [V]. Miyazaki's often-quoted idea of "ma", the quiet beat between actions, points the same way [S; interview not opened].
- **Four beats.** Kishōtenketsu (introduce, develop, twist, conclude) fits a 10-second gag as well as a level [V for the structure; I for the application].

### How long

Reference points: Pingu 5 minutes, Shaun the Sheep 7 minutes [S]; these are for watching. Inside play, Sesame Workshop's testing found children "become impatient with the uninterruptible audio prompts" and recommends making non-essential ones interruptible, "especially on replay" [V]. From that [I]:

| Kind | Length | Interruptible | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reaction | 0.5 to 3 s | no need | the eater's face after a bite |
| Consequence scene | 4 to 10 s | any touch joins in or moves on | the fire truck arrives, hose sprays, cat climbs down |
| Ending tableau | 10 to 25 s, then a living still | yes | everyone eats the pizza the child made; the lights go low |
| Longer film for 9 to 12 | up to 30 to 45 s | yes | a replay of the child's machine run, cut like a short |

On the second and later viewings the scene should differ because what the child made differs. A fixed cutscene is an arc and wears out (Cook [V]).

### When to play them

1. **As a consequence of what the child did.** This is the main case. It is feedback, it is caused, and it stars their work. The Goose model: prank, then overreaction [V].
2. **As a discovered secret.** A particular combination always produces a particular scene (hat on the cat: the cat struts). Deterministic, findable again, never counted.
3. **As an ending.** The cycle's natural completion, followed by rest and a child-initiated restart.

Not as a random treat. A scene that plays "sometimes" for the same action is a variable reward and belongs to section 5.

### How a tiny team makes them procedurally

- **Pose library plus springs.** Rosen's Overgrowth character uses 13 keyframes in total: four each for walk and run, one crouch, one idle, with interpolation doing the rest [S]. For a cartridge character: 6 to 10 poses (idle, anticipate, reach, hold, delighted, disgusted, startled, sleepy, proud), blended by springs. [I]
- **The classic principles as code.** Squash and stretch as volume-preserving scale on a spring; anticipation as a short move the opposite way before any action; follow-through as secondary parts (ears, tail, hat) on looser springs; arcs by easing position on curves; exaggeration by overshoot. These are the first items Jonasson and Purho add [S] and Disney's long-standing principles [S, not opened].
- **A face from parts.** Two eyes, brows, a mouth shape, each with a few states and a gaze target. "Add eyes" is the cheapest character there is [S]. The eyes should look at what the child touches. [I]
- **A cue list, not a video.** A scene is a short list of timed beats (look, anticipate, act, react, hold) with parameters filled from play state: which food, which colour, how tall. The same list plays differently each time. [I]
- **Staging.** One idea per shot, strong silhouette, action in the middle band of the screen away from the bottom edge [V Sesame for the edge; I for the rest].
- **Camera as a character.** Push in on the reaction, a small kick on impacts scaled by trauma squared [V Eiserloh], a slow pull back for the ending.
- **Music that follows.** The Goose's piano jumps intensity or stops with the action [V]; A Short Hike fades instruments in and out by place and activity [V]. A few synthesised layers switched by state is enough.

## 5. Where "fun for weeks" turns into "addictive"

### What the literature names

**Radesky, Hiniker and colleagues (JAMA Network Open, 2022).** 133 apps that were the longest-used apps of 160 children aged 3 to 5. They defined features "as manipulative if they used age-inappropriate interaction or reduced user autonomy in service of the monetization goals". Four kinds [V]:

- **Parasocial relationship pressure** (25% of apps for prolonging play): a character "applies pressure to play longer... through shame, taunting, or manipulation". Examples: Talking Tom saying "Do you want to give up?" and, when the player is idle, "You're making me want to go to sleep"; Blockcraft 3D notifying "people are protesting your absence!"; "Come back tomorrow to get THIS dragon!"
- **Time pressure** (17%): countdown clocks at decision points; "Save me!" with 4 seconds left.
- **Navigation constraints** (46%): "auto-advancing without providing a clear pause or disengagement options between levels", no exit button, pop-ups.
- **Lures** (45%): "tokens, rewards, candy, virtual toys", "daily rewards", "achievements users could earn for repeated play", "stickers, trophies, and leaderboards" shown "just as the player is trying to make a decision".

Almost every child (98.8%) had at least one such feature in their top apps; only 20% of apps had none; free apps had more; PBS KIDS apps had none apart from autoplay. The authors tie the designs to the industry habit of judging apps by "duration and frequency of use", and to children's attachment to favourite characters and "immature impulse inhibition" [V].

**Meyer, Radesky and colleagues (2019).** 95% of 135 apps for under-fives contained advertising, including characters used commercially and videos interrupting play [S]. Not relevant to an ad-free cartridge beyond confirming the baseline.

**5Rights Foundation, Disrupted Childhood.** Names the persuasive strategies: autoplay ("minimising or eliminating breaks during which a user might decide to disengage"), feeds "without any end point", streaks, notifications, and variable or randomised rewards including loot boxes; ties the last to anticipation and to gambling-like mechanics [V].

**UK Children's Code (ICO), detrimental use.** Lists "reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play" as sticky features; asks services to present continuing "neutrally without suggesting that children will lose out if they don't", to avoid features that "automatically extend use instead of requiring children to make an active choice", and to provide "pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress" [V].

**Hiniker et al., Coco's Videos (2018).** Three versions of a preschool video player: one that stops, one that suggests another activity, one that autoplays. With autoplay, children were less likely to state their own plan and less likely to make the planned transition [S for the finding; V for the design and the recommendation that apps let children "make decisions on their own about whether to continue"].

**Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013).** Dark game design patterns include grinding (time over skill) and "playing by appointment" (the game, not the player, sets when to play) [S].

**Lepper et al. (1973).** Covered in rule 4 [V].

**Common Sense Media (2025 census).** Gaming time among children 8 and under rose 65% in four years; 40% have a tablet by age 2 [V, report page]. Context for why the question matters; not a design finding.

### The line, drawn as pairs

Each row: a technique that stays off the table, the legitimate thing it resembles, and a test that tells them apart. The tests are my synthesis [I] of the sources above.

| Off the table (source) | Legitimate lookalike | The test |
|---|---|---|
| Daily rewards, streaks, "come back tomorrow" (Radesky lures; 5Rights streaks; playing by appointment) | The world mirrors the season, weather or time of day in how it looks | Does anything become available, or get lost, because of the date or the gap since last play? |
| A world that decays or scolds when left: weeds, sad pets, "protesting your absence" (Radesky; Eguchi's "requires that you come") | A world that persists and waits exactly as left; a creature that wandered and can be called back with one tap | Is the first thing the child sees on return a chore, a loss, or a remark about being away? |
| A character who pleads, guilts, cries or comments when the child stops or idles (Radesky parasocial pressure) | A character with feelings about what the child does inside the fiction: disgust at a lemon, pride in a haircut | Is the feeling about an action in the scene, or about the child's staying, leaving or returning? Idle behaviour must be self-directed (the cat grooms itself), never addressed to the child. |
| Countdowns and timers at decisions (Radesky time pressure) | Physical processes the child started that take their natural time and then stay done (dough rises, kettle boils, ball rolls) | Can anything be lost, burnt or missed by waiting or by putting the tablet down? |
| Auto-advance to the next round, no exit, autoplay (Radesky navigation; Hiniker; ICO) | A cycle that ends in a finished scene; starting again is an obvious act the child performs | After the ending, if the child does nothing, does anything new start? |
| Trophies, stickers, coins, stars, progress bars, albums with empty slots (Radesky lures) | The made or found thing itself stays in the world where the child put it | Is there a number, meter, or empty outline on screen that says "more to get"? |
| Variable-ratio rewards, rare drops, surprise boxes (5Rights; loot-box literature) | Deterministic secrets that work every time; random variation in ordinary detail (which of three yawns) | Would a child repeat an action they do not enjoy, hoping this time it pays out? |
| Promised rewards for doing the activity; praise as payment (Lepper) | The consequence in the world: the customer eats the pizza and reacts | Is the feedback information about what happened, or a prize for having done it? |
| Forced escalation: speed-ups, harder rounds that arrive on their own | Challenge the child chooses: a taller tower, a trickier customer they tapped on | Who raised the difficulty? |
| Unlock trees and gated content that create sunk cost | Everything present from the start; depth found by combining | Is anything locked behind amount of play rather than behind understanding? |
| Notifications and re-engagement prompts (5Rights; ICO) | Nothing. A cartridge has no channel to the child when closed, and should not want one | Not applicable. |

Three questions that cover most cases [I]:

1. Does anything get better or worse because of **when or how long** the child plays, rather than **what they do**?
2. Does anything on screen refer to the child's **leaving, staying or coming back**?
3. Is there anything the child wants that they can get only by **repeating something they do not enjoy, or by luck**?

A yes to any of them means the feature is on the wrong side.

One more consequence of Radesky's observation about metrics [V]: if the studio measures a cartridge by session length or daily return, it will drift toward these techniques. Measure instead whether a child asks for it again the next week, how varied the things they make are (Resnick's test [V]), and whether they stop on their own without distress.

### Two places where the evidence is less tidy than the rule

- **Praise.** Sesame Workshop's guidelines recommend payoffs and encouragement ("Nice job choosing the letter A!") [V]. The Montessori position, and this studio's constraint, is no praise. Lepper's result is about *expected* rewards for doing the task; the unexpected-award group did not lose interest [V]. So the research supports "never promise a reward for playing" more firmly than it supports "never show warmth". A character being delighted by the pizza is a consequence inside the fiction and is on the safe side of both.
- **Persistence.** "My place" and "appointment play" are built from the same part, a world that remembers. The difference is only whether the remembered state can go bad.

## 6. Liveliness: why calm toys felt "too slow or too quiet", and what fixes it without pressure

The lab's gentle rules already ask for an answer to every touch, and also ban screen shake, hit-stop, confetti, flashes and fanfares, with low volumes and gaps of quiet. The owner then found some results too slow or too quiet. My reading [I]: calm was achieved by turning down the *size of the response*, when the thing to remove was only the *pressure*. Pok Pok made the same trade on purpose (a "quieter experience" [V]); it suits a restaurant and is not what this owner wants. Response size and pressure are separate dials.

What makes an open-ended toy feel lively inside the first seconds:

1. **Something is already going on.** A character is mid-want when the scene opens: the customer is tapping the counter, the kitten is stuck up the tree. The MDA example tunes for "surprise, fear, and anticipation" in a character before any goal exists [V]. Sesame's timings suggest a prompt after 6 to 8 idle seconds [V]; better that the scene needs no prompt because its want is visible at second zero. [I]
2. **Touch-down response in the same frame, and bigger than the touch.** Sesame [V]; "cascading action and response for minimal user input" [S]. One tap on the hydrant: the cap pops, water arcs, the dog bolts, a puddle spreads, the puddle reflects. Chains are the difference between "it answered" and "it's alive".
3. **Characters who react, with opinions, including bad ones.** Toca Kitchen and Hair Salon [S]; the Goose's villagers [V]. The reaction should start within a few hundred milliseconds and read from across the room.
4. **Comedy from misuse.** The wrong thing must always work and be funnier than the right thing: boot on the head, soap in the soup, hose on the baker. This is exactly two-to-four humour [V Hoicka], and "your goal is just to bother people" is why the Goose feels like constant success [V].
5. **Sound on everything, with character.** Sesame: "user actions should be recognized with a corresponding sound" [V]. Hidden Folks did every sound by mouth [V], with about 2,000 of them [S]. The fix for "too quiet" is density, variety and range more than volume: several variants per sound, pitch that follows size or speed, a clear comic punctuation sound, voices in babble, and music layers that rise and drop with the action (Goose, Short Hike [V]).
6. **Safe physical chaos.** Things topple, spill, bounce, roll off the table and can be picked up again. Gabler: a juicy element "will bounce and wiggle and squirt" [V]. Kerbal's lesson is that a failure which is spectacular and costs nothing is a reason to try again [S]. Gingold: the miniature world must "feel safe to experiment" with [V].
7. **Escalation inside a cycle.** The kishōtenketsu twist [V]: the third customer is a giant; the second bubble is enormous. The child caused it and can stop; it is not a speed-up imposed by a clock.
8. **The child sets the tempo; the world never makes them wait.** Reactions run in parallel with input. No animation locks the next touch. Sesame on impatience with anything uninterruptible [V].
9. **Impact feedback is not reward feedback.** [I] Squash and stretch, anticipation, a camera kick proportional to mass (trauma squared, so only big things move it [V]), a frame or two of pause on a heavy landing [S], dust where things hit [V Swink] are all statements about weight. They can come back into the gentle set. Confetti, fanfares, floating words, flashes and stars are statements about approval and stay out. This asks for a small change to the lab's rule 7: split the ban list along that line.
10. **Contrast.** Metamorphabet's "event, silence, event" [V]. A scene that is never quiet cannot be loud. Keep the rests; make the events bigger.

A caution on the other side. Nine minutes of a fast-paced fantastical cartoon lowered four-year-olds' executive function immediately afterwards, compared with a slower show or drawing (Lillard and Peterson, n=60) [S]. That is about pace imposed on a passive viewer. It argues for liveliness that is contingent on the child's own action and returns to rest when they stop, and against scenes that keep cutting and flashing on their own. In one study the youngest toddlers (around two) learned a word from video only when the screen required a touch on the relevant spot, not when any touch or no touch would do; for slightly older children the same requirement got in the way [S, Kirkorian et al.]. So contingency helps most when it points at the thing that matters.
