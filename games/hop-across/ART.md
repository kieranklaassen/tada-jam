# Design sheet

Hop Across is a look-ahead game with no learning goal. A rabbit leads three small ones across a folding game board, one hop for each tap, and every other thing on the board moves one step of its own each time she lands.

## The band and its age rule

- **Band.** The manifest band is 5 to 9, so age 5 governs the design.
- **Cue-table row.** The row for a youngest age of 5 to 6 in the age-band cue table of wordless clarity. Cues the game uses from it: a breathing glow on what can be touched now, a ghost hand showing one move, a character who shows a new thing once inside the fiction, characters who gaze at what they want, and a world that answers physically. Its "Avoid" column binds the game: no word or letter, no numeral or symbol (the band starts at 5), no voice instruction, no hint that gives the answer. More than one way to hop is open at once, and the game still has one function: hop.
- **Pack rule for the age range.** (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md) for the youngest: tap only, no double tap, no reading, slapstick in which the victim overreacts and is never hurt, and characters who notice only what is in front of them. For the oldest the board is a real system that behaves truly, with more than one way across (pack: game-design, ages-9-to-12.md, as far as age 9 reaches into it): the rules never change, a failure is large, funny and free, and nothing is stored as a best.
- **Symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, optional or not, and the game has no `symbols.ts`. The board is printed without numerals or lettering, and no square is numbered.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only where a first visit starts in the designed order: 5 and younger starts at `cars`; 6 or 7 starts at `logs`; 8 and older starts at `bell`. A saved position wins over the age. Age locks nothing: every position is reached by play from any start.
- **What `null` gives.** The youngest default, `cars`.

## The toy

**The action.** A tap, and the rabbit hops one square towards it. It is the only thing the finger does to the board. A tap on the cat is answered by the cat alone and makes no hop and no step, and so is a tap on the duck at its post, on the hamper, on an old rabbit she does not stand next to, or on a toy that looks on from the rim: each answers as itself. A press in the top right corner is the grown-up's and is answered by nothing. A hop needs a tap in its direction and never a tap on one given square, so nothing the child must touch lies under the round home control at the top centre, and the cat sits to one side of it.

**In an empty scene.** The board lies open with its printed lanes, the rabbit stands on it with three small ones in a line behind her, and nothing on it wants anything.

- The finger lands and the rabbit is already in the air: a quick arc one square towards the finger, ears streaming behind, and a squash as she lands, with a sound that belongs to what she landed on. Grass gives a soft thump and a rustle, road a wooden clack, cloth a muffled flump, a log a hollow knock.
- A tap on the rabbit herself is a hop on the spot, straight up and down with a double thud.
- The small ones copy the hop one step late, one after the other, each in its own way and with its own squeak, so one tap sends a ripple down the line.
- When she lands, everything else on the board takes one step of its own, and then stands still: a car rolls one square and nods on its springs, a lorry trundles two, a log drifts one with the current. Nothing moves again until the next hop.
- The hop needs no waiting. A tap that lands while the last hop is still settling is answered at once, from where she is.
- The answer is bigger than the touch: one tap moves the rabbit, then the line behind her, then every car, lorry and log on the board, and the cat that watches from the table's edge follows her with its eyes.

**Why it is a pleasure with no goal.** The hop is quick, squashy and exact, and the child is the clock of the whole board: tap, and the world ticks once. Tapping fast makes the traffic run and tapping slowly makes it creep, so a child plays the board like a wind-up toy long before trying to cross anything. Random tapping always gives something: a hop onto a car slides her off its roof with a honk, a hop into the river brings up the fish, standing in front of a car makes a pancake that peels itself up. Nothing is lost by any of it. Someone watching sees within three seconds that the child is hopping a rabbit across a board game.

The demo this comes from asked whether a perfect hop with slapstick failure is enough to keep going. It ran its traffic on a clock and kept a distance score. The game keeps the hop and the slapstick, writes both new, and gives the clock to the child.

**New on day 15.** The child uses one mover against another: rides the lorry down a lane to get past a queue of cars, wakes the train on purpose to clear a lane of traffic, and times a hop so the turtle is up under a small one when it lands. The lanes combine and the rules never change, so what the child found out stays true.

## The four mechanic questions

The game has no learning goal, so the four questions about a school skill do not apply; the decision at each tap is where each thing will be after its next step.

## The error as a consequence

A wrong hop is run by the world's own rules and shows where and why.

- **In front of a vehicle.** The car, lorry or train that takes its step into her square rolls over her. She is flat, on that square, under that vehicle, so the child sees which one did it and from which side it came. A tap while she is flat is still answered when the finger lands: she wriggles under the wheels with a squeak, the world takes its step, and when that step clears her square she peels herself up there. Nothing is moved back and nothing is lost.
- **Into open water, or down with a turtle, or off the edge on a log.** The fish spits her back onto the last dry square she stood on. She is wet for a few hops.
- **The small ones.** Each follows the one ahead and looks no further, so it is flattened or dunked by its own mistake in its own square, and then scampers after the line again. A small one left behind is still on the board, where it fell behind, and comes when the line is near.
- **The state stays.** Every car, log and turtle is where its steps have brought it, and stands still. The child changes one thing, the next hop, and tries again.
- Nothing gives a verdict: no buzzer, no cross, no count of mishaps, no face turned to the child. A mishap costs the hop it took and the wriggle under the wheels, and nothing else, and is at least as good to watch as a clean hop.
- A clean crossing is a consequence too: the family arrives dry and round, and the old rabbit at the picnic reacts to exactly how each one arrives.

## The designed order, and what is stored

**The board.** A crossing is a home bank, seven lanes and a far bank with the picnic cloth, each lane six squares long. The lanes run up and down the board and the family crosses them from left to right. Beyond the cloth lies the first lane of the next crossing, already printed and with its movers standing on it. A mover that leaves one end of its lane goes into a tunnel arch or under the board's rim and comes back in at the other end after two more steps, so a lane is a loop of eight squares of which six show.

- **The cloth.** The top square of each bank is a seat and not a place to land: on the far bank the old rabbit sits there with the cake, and on the first home bank the burrow stands there. The five squares below it are free, and the family fits on them in a line.
- **A hop that cannot land where it was sent** still happens. Onto the seat of an old rabbit from a square next to it, she bounces off its tummy with a boing and it chuckles. Onto the burrow, as at the rim, she hops on the spot with a thunk. At the board's rim she hops on the spot with a thunk. Each of these is a hop on the spot, and the world takes its step. Onto a square where a small one stands or lies flat she leapfrogs: the small one ducks with a squeak and goes under her to the square she left, where a flat one is up again, so the family can never box her in. Only a small one flat under a lorry stays, and she rides the bed above it.
- **How the line follows.** Each small one hops to the square the one ahead of it has just left. When the one ahead did not leave its square, it hops on the spot. While the rabbit is on the cloth and one of the family is not, a small one on the cloth that would hop on the spot hops one square along the cloth away from her instead, so that the one behind it has a square to land on. When it has fallen behind, it hops one square towards the one ahead, along the longer way first, but never into open water and never onto a square where another of the family stands: then it hops on the spot. It looks no further than that, so it can be flattened on the way.

**The order.** Nine positions, one new thing each and then combinations. The ids are those of `LADDER` in `config.ts`; each names a place in the game's own order.

| Position id | The one new thing | What the seven lanes hold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `cars` | A car steps one square. | Roads with one or two cars and grass lanes among them; two roads may lie side by side. |
| `lorry` | A lorry steps two squares, and its bed can be ridden. | Roads with cars, one road with a lorry, grass. |
| `logs` | Open water cannot be stood on, and a log carries whatever stands on it. | Roads as before and one river with logs, later two. |
| `turtle` | A turtle stays where it is and goes under on every third step. | Roads, a river of logs and a river of turtles. |
| `bell` | A train comes only on the step after its bell is stepped on, and takes its whole lane. | Roads, a river, and rails with a bell plate on the grass before them. |
| `shared-road` | Nothing new: cars and a lorry in one lane, where the lorry shunts the car. | As before, with one shared road. |
| `shared-river` | Nothing new: logs and turtles in one river, where a log rides over a turtle. | As before, with one shared river. |
| `rail-road` | Nothing new: rails laid in a road, where the train clears the lane. | As before, with one road that has rails and a bell. |
| `all` | Nothing new: any of the above side by side, two rivers together, the bell plate between the rails of its own lane instead of on the grass before it. | Any lanes of the kinds above. |

- A crossing is laid from its position by a seeded pick among that position's lane kinds, directions and starting places. Chance decides only that ordinary detail. Every crossing that can be laid has a way across with no mishap for the rabbit from the square she starts on, by plain look-and-hop, and a test plays a solver over every layout to hold that.
- The same order deepens without new content: the same movers meet in one lane, and the bell moves from beside the path onto it.

**A harder option looks harder, and the child picks it.** No crossing needs a ride, a rung bell or a turtle timed for a small one. They are there on the board to be seen: a lorry is plainly faster and plainly bigger than a car, a turtle plainly less steady than a log. The child who wants the quicker or the cleverer way takes it, and the child who waits for a plain gap gets across as well.

**Which crossing a new position lays out.** The first lane of the next crossing is on screen while the child plays this one, so the next crossing is laid out whole when the board slides onto the current one. The position moves when the current crossing is judged. So a new position first shows in the crossing after next.

**How a cycle is judged.** A cycle is one crossing, from the board sliding onto it to the family on the cloth. Only the rabbit's own mishaps are counted, and only inside the saved state: each time she is flattened or spat back is one. The small ones' mishaps are not counted.

- Well: the family reached the cloth by hopping there and she had at most one mishap. The position moves up one.
- Mixed: two to four mishaps, or she hopped on to the next crossing before every small one was on the cloth. The position stays.
- Badly: five or more mishaps. The position moves down one.
- A crossing that was laid from another position than the current one is not judged. Nothing shows the position, the count or that either moved.

**Every field of the saved state.**

| Field | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | The version of the shape. |
| `position` | The id of the place in the order from which the crossing after next is laid. |
| `finished` | The family is on the cloth and the crossing has been judged. It stays so until the child hops the rabbit onto the lane beyond the cloth. |
| `crossing` | The crossing on the board: the position id it was laid from, whether its home bank is the first one with the burrow or the cloth of the crossing before, and its seven lanes. A lane holds its kind (grass, road, river, rails, or road with rails), the square of its bell plate if it has one, and its movers. |
| a mover | Its kind (car, lorry, log, turtle), where it stands on its lane's loop of eight, and which way it goes. A turtle also holds its count (0, 1 or 2), and a lorry the train has shoved into the tunnel holds how many more steps it stays there (1 to 3). A lane with rails holds which arch its train sleeps in and whether it is awake. |
| `next` | The next crossing, in the same shape. Its first lane is the one on screen beyond the cloth. |
| `family` | The four pawns in the order of the line, the rabbit first. Each holds its column and row, whether it is flat, what it rides (nothing, or which mover and which square of it), and the last dry square it stood on. |
| `mishaps` | The rabbit's mishaps in this crossing, 0 to 9, where 9 means nine or more. Read only when the crossing is judged. |
| `shown` | The ids of the new things the duck has already shown, so none is shown twice. |
| `seed` | The state of the seeded stream that lays the crossings. |

- Every hop is one whole step of the saved state: where each pawn lands and where each mover stands after its step are known the moment the finger lands, and that is what is saved. The arc of a hop, a car's roll, the peel of a pancake and the fish's spit are views of that step and are not saved. Nothing is saved in the air: on load every pawn and mover stands where its last step put it.
- Short-lived and gone on load: the tyre track on a peeled pawn, the drips on a wet one, the bleary eyes of a train that was woken often, and where the fish's shadow is (it is drawn beside the rabbit's row). None of them changes a step.
- Nothing moves while the game is away, since nothing moves without a hop. No clock is read.
- The largest legal state is far under half of 64 KB, and a test says so.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

| Character | Its want, always visible | What never changes | Its best reaction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The rabbit | The picnic: when she stands still her ears lean towards the cloth and she looks across at it. | She hops wherever she is sent, at once, and never looks first. She cannot bear being wet. | Wet, she shakes until her ears whirl. Flat, she blinks up at whatever is parked on her. |
| The first small one | To be next to her: it leans forward on its toes. | Eager. It hops early and high and squeaks highest. It loves the lorry's bed and bounces on it. | Flattened, it peels up before anyone and looks round to see who saw. |
| The middle small one | A nap: its eyes are half shut and its ears trail. | Dozy. It hops late and low, with a yawn. It loves a log and lies down on it. | Flattened, it goes on dozing as a pancake, and peels up with a stretch. |
| The last small one | To keep its balance: one ear is bent and it teeters where it stands. | Wobbly. It lands, tips, and windmills upright. It is afraid of the fish and hides behind its ears when the shadow is near. | Spat out, it lands on its head and rolls three times. |
| The old rabbit at the picnic | The family: it stands on tiptoe on the cloth and holds the cake out towards them. | It likes its guests dry and round. | The duck | To be across first: it stands at the board's edge with its chest out. | It walks straight into every new thing and learns nothing. | Peeling itself up, it quacks at the car as if the car had been in its way. |
| A car driver | The road ahead: a peg head with wide eyes. | Jumpy. It honks at anything on its roof and looks back only when something hits it from behind: a lorry that shunts it, or the train. | Its head pops up like a cork when she lands on the roof. |
| The lorry driver | The road ahead: a peg head that stares straight down it and never turns. | It notices nothing, ever. | None, which is the joke. |
| The fish | Something to spit: its shadow keeps beside the rabbit along the river. | It spits out everything it catches, always back where it came from. | It puffs its cheeks twice before a spit, and looks pleased after. |
| The turtle | Peace: its eyes are on whoever stands on it. | Shy, and it keeps its count of three whatever happens. | It looks up, blinks slowly, and goes down anyway. |
| The train | Sleep: it snores in its arch. | It hates its bell and comes whenever it rings. | Rung four times running, it runs the lane with its eyes shut. |
| The cat at the table's edge | The rabbit: its eyes follow her everywhere. | It only watches, and never touches the board. Touched, it purrs, blinks slowly and thumps its tail. | It flinches and flattens its ears when anyone is flattened, and licks its lips when the fish comes up. |

- A reaction is to the exact thing that happened, starts as it happens, and is as good to watch when it is a mishap as when it is a clean hop. No feeling is about the child: no one thanks, praises, pleads or hurries, and the old rabbit goes on holding the cake out however long the crossing takes.
- The drivers, the lorry and the train notice only what is in front of them, so what happens behind them is the child's to play with.

## The scenes

Three scenes. Each is a list of timed beats on game time, built on `scene.ts`, and each gives way to any touch at once.

**The duck goes first** (4 to 7 seconds, once for each of the five new things, `cars` to `bell`).

- Cause: the board has slid onto a crossing that holds a thing not yet in `shown`. On a first visit the game opens on such a crossing. The showing starts when the slide ends. Nothing plays by itself on a load, on a first visit too: a showing that is owed starts at the child's first touch, where its sound can begin, and that touch makes no hop.
- Beats: the duck leaves its post at the near edge beside the home bank and crosses in one long waddle to the lane of the new thing, and nothing in the lanes between touches it; it waddles three or four steps of its own, and at each of them that lane alone takes its step, as it does for a hop of the rabbit's; the new thing does to the duck what it does (the car flattens it, the lorry carries it into the arch, the log carries it off the edge to the fish, the turtle goes down under it, the bell brings the train); the duck peels up or shakes itself, quacks, and waddles off the edge of the board onto the table.
- Filled in from: which thing is new, and where that lane's movers stand.
- Saved when it starts: the thing's id in `shown`, and that lane's movers in `crossing` as they stand after the duck's steps, which are known beforehand. The family does not move.
- Any touch ends it: the lane's movers are where the save has them, the duck is off the board, and the touch is then an ordinary hop.

**The picnic** (6 to 9 seconds, and it holds as long as the child likes).

- Cause: the last of the four lands on the cloth by hopping there.
- Beats: the old rabbit's ears shoot up; it hands a slice to each of the four in the order of the line, and each takes it as itself and as it arrived (a wet one is wrapped in the napkin first, a tyre track is laughed at and dusted off with an ear); they eat with a run of crunches; the old rabbit gives away the last slice; they sit in a row on the cloth.
- Filled in from: the order of the line as `family` holds it, and who is wet or tyre-marked at that moment.
- Saved with the hop that causes it, in that hop's one save: the family's squares on the cloth, `finished`, `position` as the judging moved it, and `mishaps` back at 0. Put away before the first beat, the game opens on the row on the cloth and no picnic plays.
- Any touch skips to the row on the cloth, and is then an ordinary hop.

**The tip** (3 to 4 seconds).

- Cause: the rabbit hops onto the lane beyond the cloth while a small one is not yet on the cloth.
- Beats: the leaf of the board behind her lifts at its fold; the small ones still on it slide down to the cloth in a squeaking heap; the leaf drops flat with a slap; the heap untangles into a line behind her; the board slides on.
- Filled in from: which small ones were left, where, and whether flat.
- Saved when it starts, all at once, since the slide follows on the same touch: the crossing judged as mixed and `position` unmoved; `next` as the new `crossing` and a newly laid `next`; `family` with the rabbit on the first lane of the new crossing and the small ones in line on the cloth, none flat and none riding; `mishaps` at 0, or at 1 if that hop left her flat under a car on the new lane; `finished` false; `seed`. Put away in the middle, the game opens on the new crossing with the line already on the cloth, and nothing replays.
- Any touch skips to that state, and is then an ordinary hop.

Every other answer is a reaction and not a scene: a pancake, a peel, a spit, a ride, a rung bell. Each lasts under two seconds, runs beside the child's next touch and blocks nothing.

**How a cycle ends and the next starts.** The picnic leaves the family in a row on the cloth, eating, for as long as the child likes. Beyond the cloth the first lane of the next crossing lies in view with its movers standing still. Nothing starts by itself: nothing moves without a hop, the old rabbit says nothing about waiting, and a hop on the spot on the cloth only makes the row bounce and the world take one step. The next crossing begins when the child hops the rabbit off the cloth onto that lane: the board slides until the cloth is the home bank, the next crossing fills the frame with a new picnic at its far side, and that step saves `finished` false, `next` as `crossing`, a newly laid `next`, `family` in its squares on the new crossing, and `seed`. On load no scene replays: the game opens on the state the last step or scene left, every pawn and mover standing where it stood.

### What the picture is

A board game seen from straight above, unfolded on a wooden table and filling the frame from side to side. Everything is a thing from the box or from the playroom floor round it: a linen-covered board with a fold, squares printed on it, turned wooden pawns, toy cars, cardboard cards standing in plastic feet, and other toys that have come to watch. Nothing is drawn as a real rabbit, road or river; it is always the toy of one.

- **The table.** Warm brown wood (`#8a5a3c`, grain a shade darker and a shade lighter). It shows as a band along the far edge, where the cards and the onlookers stand, and a band along the near edge, where the loose toys lie.
- **The board.** Linen (`#eadfc6`) with a woven thread pattern. It is one long folding strip that runs on under both side edges of the frame: ten lanes show, each six squares long, and a square is 118 by 104 stage units. The fold is a crease with a dark side and a light side between the seventh lane and the picnic cloth. The leaf to the left of it is the one that lifts in the tip.
- **The squares.** Flat printed colour with rounded corners and a hair of linen between them, two close shades alternating so the grid can be counted by eye: grass `#7ec65a` and `#88cd63` with printed tufts and daisies, road `#586676` and `#616f80` with a cream centre dash, river `#36a3e4` and `#41acea` with pale wave marks, a rail bed in sand (`#c8b28c`, `#d1bc97`) with sleepers and two rails, the picnic cloth in red and cream gingham. Road, river and rails run off over the board's border at both ends, under the cards.
- **The prints.** Each lane kind has small things of its own printed in the corners of its squares, at most two to a square and in opposite corners, so the middle of every square stays plain under a pawn. Grass: molehills, mushrooms, flowers, stones, clover. Road: puddles, manhole covers, tar patches, one curved tyre smear, fallen leaves, and on some roads a zebra crossing across one row. River: lily pads, rushes, ripples drawn as broken arcs, pebbles. Rails: gravel, clover, stones. The cloth: plates, sandwiches, apples, cups, a jam jar, grapes. The home bank: carrots, paw prints, flowers. A bell plate is a red plate with its brass bell standing in the middle, never an empty ring, and a plain cord runs from it along its rails with one knot at the plate and nothing spaced along it.
- **The far edge.** A brick arch at the end of every road, reeds at the end of every river, a tunnel mouth at the end of rails with the train's face in it, the burrow at the home bank, the picnic hamper behind the old rabbit. At the end of a grass lane stands a fruit-tree card or the head of a large toy that rests its chin on the rim and watches: a teddy, a tin robot, a giraffe, a rag doll. No two neighbours are the same. A ginger cat looks over the cards left of the middle; only its ears, eyes and nose show.
- **The near edge.** Arches, reeds and tunnel mouths again, and between them what was left lying about: a spinning top, a ball and a little wooden car, marbles, a spare pawn on its side, crayons, building blocks, and the box lid under the cloth. None of them carries pips, dots in a row or any mark that could be counted. The duck stands at its post at the near edge beside the home bank.
- **The pawns.** Turned wood: a round painted body, a cream ball head, two long ears, dot eyes, one gloss stripe. The rabbit is red and 1.3 of a square tall; the small ones are yellow, teal and purple at 0.9. The old rabbit is the largest thing on the board at 1.66: grey-brown wood in a blue body with wire spectacles, on tiptoe on the cloth's seat, holding out a cake on a stand.
- **The movers.** Toy cars in one candy colour each with a peg driver whose eyes show through the roof; an orange lorry with a wooden slatted bed and an exhaust pipe that puffs; logs of plain turned wood with end rings and a knot, in a pale ring of water; turtles with a painted shell that sits high, awash, or under a loose ring of bubbles of all sizes, and a head that comes out to look up at a rider; a tin train that sleeps in its tunnel mouth, its face two shut lids, a brass nose and a small smile.

### Rules of the look

- **No outline anywhere.** Shapes are told apart by colour and by the shadow, never by a drawn contour. The one white edge is the die-cut edge of a card.
- **One light, one shadow.** Every standing thing throws the same hard shadow down and to the right (10 by 8 stage units, `rgba(46, 26, 12, 0.32)`), never blurred. A thing in the air throws it further away and smaller; a flat thing throws none.
- **Flat is printed, standing is a toy.** What cannot be hopped on or moved is printed on the linen. What moves or can be met stands on the board with a shadow. A child can tell what is alive from that alone.
- **The pieces the child reads stay plain.** The movers and the middles of the squares carry no decoration: a car is one colour, a log is plain wood, and the prints keep to the corners. The decoration is on the setting: the prints, the cards, the onlookers and the loose toys. Movers and squares stand still between steps; what breathes and blinks is the family, the cat, the old rabbit, the duck and the onlookers' eyes.
- **Colour means kind.** Grey is road, blue is river, green is safe, sand with rails is the train's, gingham is the picnic. No colour is used for two kinds, and the family's four body colours are used by no mover.
- **Faces are two dots.** Only the train and the fish have a mouth. Pawns and drivers show feeling with the eyes, the ears and the whole body: ears trail in a hop, flop when wet and lie flat on a pancake; eyes become lines when flat or asleep. No mouth shapes, no eyebrows.
- **Nothing is written and nothing is a count.** No word, letter, numeral or sign is printed on the board, a card, a car, a block or the box lid, and there are no dice.
- **The home control and the grown-up corner.** A hop needs a tap in its direction, never on one given square, so nothing a child must touch lies under the home control at the top centre or in the top right corner. The characters that answer a touch as themselves (the cat, the duck, the hamper, the old rabbits and the onlookers) are extras and none is needed to play; the cat lies left of both controls.

### Motion in this look

Wood does not bend, so the squash is small and quick and the weight is in the landing: a pawn tips forward as it leaves, lands with a short squat and a knock, and its ears arrive after it. Nothing shares a curve (`motion.ts`, with a test that says so).

- **The rabbit** hops fast and flat, squats deep and comes up by one ear when she peels. On a log her ears windmill, one each way, until she has her balance. Over a small one she vaults twice as high as a hop while it ducks to half its height and scurries under her.
- **The first small one** hops early and highest, rings quickly, and snaps up from a pancake before anyone, then looks round.
- **The middle one** hops late and low with trailing ears, goes on dozing as a pancake, and peels up slowly with a long stretch.
- **The last one** tips sideways on every landing and windmills upright. It peels most of the way up, slips back down, and makes it at the second go. Spat out, it lands on its head and rolls.
- **A car** darts one square and nods on its springs. **The lorry** heaves itself off, never overshoots, and its tailboard rattles. **A log** drifts evenly, bobs and rolls a quarter turn, and dips and rocks when somebody lands on it; its rider leans when it rides up over a turtle and wobbles when the fish noses it. A driver's eyes go round the rim of its head when it looks behind. A rider's ears stream while the lorry rolls. One that comes down on the cab of a lorry whose bed is taken slides back off it to the square it came from. A small one the fish spits out comes down on its head and rolls upright, the wobbly one three times round. **A turtle** only sinks and rises. A lorry the train shoves goes into the tunnel ahead of it, tail first if it met the train head on, stays there three steps and trundles out of the other arch, going the way the train went. **The train** takes the whole lane in under half a second, and its wind lays over the ears of everyone a lane or two away. In the far mouth it yawns, mouth wide, and shuts its eyes. Asleep, it breathes and blows a bubble at its funnel.
- **The duck** waddles with its chest out, quacks at whatever flattened it, and shakes itself at its post. **The fish** is a shadow first, then two puffs of the cheeks and the spit.
- **The cat** sways, blinks slowly, follows the rabbit with its eyes, flattens its ears when anyone is flattened, licks its nose when the fish comes up, and gets down behind the cards while the board is lifted or sliding. **The old rabbit** rocks on tiptoe and holds the cake out; its ears shoot up when the family lands. **The onlookers** move their eyes only, until one is touched: then it swells from its chin in its own way (the teddy bounces twice, the robot grows in hard steps with large eyes, the giraffe stretches up, the doll shuts her eyes and shivers) and is never smaller than it stands, so the head in the still picture stays covered. The hamper throws its flap up and an apple hops out and back; the old rabbit bobs on its toes with its ears up and lifts the cake; the one asleep on the home cloth starts up with a snort and sinks back. Each cloth has its own hamper and its own old rabbit, and the one touched is the one that answers, behind the home cloth as behind the far one. At the picnic a tyre-marked one bends one ear down from the side of its head and brushes it three times across its tummy while the track fades.
- **The board itself** moves twice: the leaf behind the fold lifts and drops in the tip, and the whole strip slides one crossing along. The cards and the onlookers stand on the table and do not lift with the leaf. The printed squares never move otherwise: the water marks and the tufts are print.

### How it is drawn

- Everything that stands still is painted once to an offscreen picture: the table, the linen, the squares, the prints, the cards, the onlookers' heads and the loose toys. It is painted again on a load, a resize, a tier change and a slide, never in a frame of play.
- A frame stamps that picture once and then draws the standing things over it, clipped at the cards so that a mover goes out of sight under them. While the board slides it is one stamp with an offset. While the leaf is lifted it is four bands (above the lanes, the leaf squeezed, the rest of the lanes, below the lanes) that cover the surface once.
- What the frame-budget test counts: stamps, and one for every figure drawn over them. At rest and in a slide one stamp; in the tip four; over a walk of every position and every scene at most 39 figures and 451 paths, with no blur, filter or read-back among them. A touched onlooker or the opened hamper is one figure more and is not in that walk. That is the game's draw-call count: at most about 44.

### How each tier keeps the look

Tiers step down the pixel ratio (2, 1.5, 1.25, 1), then drop the gloss stripes and lathe rings, then the linen weave and the table's grain. Shadows, colours, silhouettes, prints and motion are the same at every tier; nothing a child reads is removed.
