# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

Dig for the Duck has no learning goal. It is rebuilt from the demo `lab/arcade/protos/dig-for-the-duck/`, which lends its idea and its feel in the hand and no code. The demo's counted buried finds, its stars for water saved and its locked run of levels are gone.

## The band and its age rule

The manifest band is 6 to 10. Its youngest age, 6, governs the design.

- **Cue table.** The row for a youngest age of 5 to 6 in the wordless-clarity convention. Its "Avoid" column binds the game: no word or letter; no numeral or symbol the child must read in order to go on; no voice instruction; no hint that gives the answer.
- **Age rule.** The band lies between two of the pack's age rules, and the game takes from each what holds for a six-year-old. From (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md): tap and drag are the only gestures, a rubbed line survives a lifted finger, nothing is read, there is no double tap, and whoever a joke is played on is bewildered and never hurt. From (pack: game-design, ages-9-to-12.md): the hill is a real system that behaves truly and always the same, more than one way works and any way that floats the duck stands, and a way that goes wrong is large, funny and free. From (pack: game-design, guided-discovery.md), for children under ten: a new kind of ground is shown once by one of the hill's residents, without words, before the child meets it; and a try that fails is local and is mended with one more rub.
- **Symbol rule.** The band starts at 6, so numerals and mathematics signs would be allowed where each lies on its quantity. The game draws none: no numeral, no sign, no letter and no written word anywhere, so it has no `symbols.ts`.
- **`ctx.childAge`.** It sets nothing yet. A first visit starts at the first hill at every age and at `null`: the position never moves down, so a child who started further on would never meet the first hill. The first-visit table in `config.ts` holds that one row, read through the age with both ends open. A saved position wins. Every hill, every ground and every resident is reached by play at any age.
- **`ctx.language`.** Nothing depends on it: the game has no spoken or written content.

## The toy

**Rubbing a round tunnel into coloured sand, and letting water find it.** The hill is seen in section behind a pane of glass: bands of dyed sand, one above the other, with a pond lying in a hollow at the top. The finger lands on the sand and rubs.

- **On touch-down, in the same frame:** the sand under the fingertip is gone, a round bite as wide as the finger, and a puff of grains in that band's colour jumps out of it with a gritty hiss.
- **While rubbing:** the tunnel follows the finger as one round-ended groove. The sand hisses lower the deeper it lies, so a rub down through the bands plays a falling run of gritty notes and a level rub holds one note; a fast rub is brighter and louder than a slow one. The rim the finger loosens trickles down the tunnel as loose grains that keep their colours and pile at their slope, so the floor of a tunnel is striped with what was dug above it.
- **When the tunnel meets water:** the water falls in at once, runs along the floor, pools in every dip, spills over the lowest lip and goes on. It trickles in small bright plinks, more of them the more water is moving, and the sand it touches darkens.
- **On lifting the finger:** the last loose grains go on sliding until they lie at rest, and the water goes on until it is level. Nothing waits for the finger.
- **A rub that is lifted and set down again** carries on the same tunnel; nothing is lost by lifting.
- **A tap** leaves a dimple, a pinch of grains hops, and they patter down.

It is a pleasure with no goal because it is three things at once: scratching a picture into striped sand, playing the bands like a gritty xylophone, and pouring water down a slide the child has just made (pack: game-design, toy-first.md). One rub sets off a chain that is bigger than the touch: grains slump, water finds the gap, a pool fills and spills, and whoever lives below looks up (pack: game-design, touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md). Random rubbing always cuts a tunnel and sooner or later lets water somewhere, and no rub does harm. Someone watching sees within three seconds that the child is digging the water a way down.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

Six kinds of ground by five actions. Every kind answers water in its own way and always the same way. "Wet" is the ground after water has reached it, which shows on each: sand goes dark, clay shines, rock glistens, moss is swollen, the hot stone steams, seed earth has put out a root. There is no wrong tool, since the finger and the water are the only two; the wrong uses are leading water where nobody wanted it and rubbing a ground that cannot be dug, and each of them works and is funny.

| | Rub it dry | Rub it wet | Tap it | Water runs onto it | Dig out from under it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sand** | It crumbles away under the finger into a round tunnel and hisses at the pitch of its depth; the loosened rim trickles down as coloured grains. | It comes away as mud in fat blobs that slide down the tunnel wall and plop one after another. | A dimple, and a pinch of grains hops and patters down. | It darkens and drinks the first of the water with a soft fizz, then lets the rest run by. | Its loose grains pour down in a hushing curtain and settle as a striped cone; packed sand keeps its arch. |
| **Clay** | It is stiff: only a narrow groove peels out behind the finger, with a low creak, and curls of clay roll away. | The finger skids with a rubbery squeak and leaves a polished chute that water shoots down. | A thumbprint is pressed into it with a dull thud: a shallow dent of open cells, which stays. | It holds every drop as a pool that glugs as it fills and wobbles when the next drop lands. | It stays where it is as a shelf over the gap and groans once; water on top of it stays on top. |
| **Rock** | It is not dug: the finger scrapes it with a clack and leaves pale scratches that fade, and the mole puts his fingers in his ears. | It squeaks like a washed plate and shines. | It knocks like a door, and something inside the hill knocks back twice. | The stream splits in two around it and patters like rain on a roof. | It drops whole with a thud that makes every resident hop, and lies where it lands as a plug or a stepping stone. |
| **Moss** | It tears off in tufts with a rip, and the tufts float down slowly. | Swollen moss is squeezed like a sponge: it squirts its water out sideways with a squelch and shrinks back. | It springs back with a soft boing and lets go a puff of spores. | It drinks with slurps, swells fat and closes the tunnel it grows beside. | It hangs down as a beard over the gap; moss that has drunk drips, plip, plip, and dry moss hangs still. |
| **Hot stone** | It is not dug: it glows brighter under the finger with a sizzle, and a fingertip of bright light fades from it. | A steaming stone whistles like a kettle, higher the faster the rub, and puffs three small clouds of steam, each of its own size. | It tings like a stove lid and one spark hops off. | The water hisses into steam, which rises by any open way, gathers as a cloud over the hill, drifts with the wind and rains down further on. | It drops with a clonk and keeps its heat: in the bath it sets up a rolling boil that goes blub-blub, and in any other pool it boils the pool away as steam, faster than the spring can fill it. |
| **Seed earth** | It crumbles dark and soft with a muffled puff, and each seed in it pops out with a tock and rolls down to lie on the first ground below. | A root is plucked: it twangs like a bass string and shakes off its drops; a hard rub snaps it with a crack. | The seed rattles in its husk like a maraca. | Each wet seed creaks and puts out a root that grows level, both ways, through open space until it meets ground: a floor over a shaft, or a lid on one. | The earth falls away and its seeds drop and bounce like beads, tick, tick, tick, to lodge lower down. |

**Other things that answer.** The duck, tapped, squeaks: flat and dusty in a dry bath, bright and wet when afloat. The bath's tin rings where it is touched and gleams there. The oak frame knocks as wood and lets go a few chips that fall. The plug, pulled before the duck floats, lets out whatever is in the bath with a gurgle for as long as it is held up and drops back on its chain. Each resident answers a tap as itself (see "The characters").

**Day 15.** On day 1 the child rubs one tunnel through sand from the pond to the bath and watches the duck float. On day 15 the child leads one stream through three grounds at once and by more than one way, on purpose: lets a pool stand on a clay shelf, drops a rock to stop a leak, lets moss close the short way so the water takes the long one past the mole, runs a stream onto a hot stone so that its steam rains on the badger's washing, drops the stone into the bath for a duck that sighs, and rolls a seed to where its root will carry the water over a shaft. It is six grounds by five actions that combine by rules which never change, with three residents whose answers the child knows well enough to stage (pack: game-design, depth-from-combinations.md; pack: game-design, liveliness-from-causing-and-comedy.md).

## The error as a consequence

Nothing gives a verdict: no cross, no buzzer, no sad face turned to the child, no reset, no lost water (pack: game-design, errors-show-as-consequences.md). The water does exactly what the tunnels and the grounds make it do, and that is the whole answer.

- **Water led the wrong way.** It arrives where the tunnel leads and that place fills. *Where:* the pool stands in the mole's sitting room or the worm's boot room, in plain sight; on the badger's drying ground the sand drinks the rain and lies dark where it fell. *Why:* the wet tunnel floor is dark all the way back to where the stream left the way the child meant. *In the world:* the resident there does what its fixed taste makes it do, which is the best part of the game.
- **Water that stops.** A pool stands in a dip, on clay, behind a rock, or the tunnel is shut by swollen moss or a root. *Where and why:* the pool is level at the lip that holds it, the moss is fat, the root lies across. The child rubs one more groove below the lip and the pool runs on.
- **Water that goes up.** On a hot stone it turns to steam and leaves by the top. *Where and why:* the stone steams, the cloud gathers over the hill and drifts one fixed way, and the rain falls where the cloud has drifted to.
- **A ground that is not dug.** The finger scrapes rock or a hot stone and nothing opens; the answer is the clack or the sizzle and the mark that fades. The way round is visible sand.
- **Too little water below.** The duck sits in a bath that is part full and keeps looking up. The pond's spring bubbles and keeps the pond up to its brim for as long as the duck does not float, and after that for as long as the hill holds less water than a fixed plenty, so water sent the wrong way is never water lost.

The state stays. Every tunnel, pool, fallen rock, swollen moss and root is where the child left it, and the child changes one thing and watches again. Success is the same kind of consequence: the bath fills and the duck floats. On every hill a way through sand alone leads from the pond to the bath, and the bath is the lowest place in the hill, so the simplest use always works.

## The designed order, and what is stored

**A cycle is one hill.** The child digs for as long as they like. When the bath is full enough to lift the duck, the duck floats and the hill is finished; it stays as it is until the child pulls the plug, which drains the hill and brings up the next one.

**What a first visit starts with, by age.** A child of any age, or of no known age, starts at `dune`. A saved position wins. The position never moves down and nobody starts above the first hill, so every hill, every ground and every resident is reached by play at any age.

**The order.** One new ground at a time, then combinations (pack: game-design, ordered-challenges-high-success.md). The ids name kinds of hill and nothing else. They stand in `LADDER` in `config.ts` in this order.

| Id | The one new thing | What the hill holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `dune` | Sand, and water that finds its way. | Sand in bands from the pond down to the bath, with the three residents' places beside the short way. |
| `claypit` | Clay, which holds a pool. | A clay shelf lies across the short way, so water stands on it until a groove is peeled through it or a way is dug round its end. |
| `scree` | Rock, which is not dug. | Boulders stand in the short way; one of them rests on sand and can be dropped. |
| `fen` | Moss, which drinks and swells shut. | Moss lines one stretch of the short way and closes it behind the first water, so the stream must go by sand beside it or the moss must be squeezed. |
| `hot-spring` | A hot stone, which turns water to steam. | A hot stone lies under the short way; water that meets it leaves as a cloud that rains over the badger's line. |
| `orchard` | Seed earth, whose wet seeds put out roots. | A shaft lies between the pond and the bath with seed earth at its mouth, so a root can carry the water over it or shut it. |
| `foothills` | Nothing new: three grounds at once. | Three of the five later grounds together, a different three in each layout. |
| `massif` | Nothing new: everything together. | All six grounds, with more than one way down. |

- **Layouts.** Each id has three hand-laid hills, which differ in where the pond, the bath, the grounds and the residents' places lie. A new hill takes the next layout of its id in turn, so a return visit is never the same hill twice running (pack: game-design, many-short-visits.md). Every layout keeps the two rules under "The error as a consequence": a way through sand alone from the pond to the bath, and the bath in the lowest place. A test walks every layout for both. In every layout the top centre of the frame, where the jam's home control lies, is open sky with no ground, pond, resident or washing under it, and the plug's chain hangs to one side of the duck, seated or afloat, so a pull on the chain is never taken for a tap on the duck; the same test holds both at 1180 by 820.
- **A harder way, chosen by the child.** Nothing makes a hill harder unasked. On every hill the short way through sand is in plain sight, and the longer ways through the other grounds are visibly longer and stranger; which one the water takes is the child's choice, made by where the finger rubs.
- **What play changes.** Any way that floats the duck stands, so every finished hill counts as one that went well, and the position moves one step up when the duck floats. No hill is judged as going badly or as mixed, and the position never moves down. A hill that is put away unfinished moves nothing. At `massif` the position stays and its layouts keep turning. Nothing on screen shows the position or that it moved.
- **Which hill a new position lays out.** The next hill is laid out when the plug is pulled, after the position has moved, so a new position lays out the very next hill. Before that pull nothing of the hill to come is on screen: what waits is the plug on its chain.
- **Sizes.** These are the game's own choice. The hill is a grid of 184 by 122 cells behind the pane. A bath holds between 300 and 400 cells of water and lifts the duck at three fifths full. Whatever lies in the bath takes up room as it would in any bath. How full the bath is, is how high its water stands: the water and everything under the water's surface (sand, a fallen rock or hot stone, a seed) are counted together, and the duck lifts when they fill three fifths of the bath and at least a fifth of the bath is water. Sand can always be rubbed out of the bath again, and the layout test also holds that the rock and hot stones which can drop into a bath leave room for that fifth of water. The spring keeps the pond at its brim while the hill holds less than two and a half baths of water.

**What is stored.** Plain JSON, versioned, each field repaired by itself on reading.

| Field | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | The version of the saved shape. |
| `position` | The id of the child's place in the order: the hill that the next pull of the plug lays out, or, while a hill is unfinished, the hill on screen. |
| `finished` | True from the moment the duck floats until the plug is pulled. |
| `hill` | The id of the hill on screen. It differs from `position` only while `finished` is true. |
| `variant` | Which of the three layouts of `hill` is on screen: 0, 1 or 2. |
| `cells` | The whole hill as one string, one character for each of its 22,448 cells in rows from the top: what the cell holds (open space, each ground in its dry and its wet form, sand packed or loose and in which colour, a seed, a root, water, steam). Every tunnel, pile, pool, fallen rock, swollen moss, root and cloud is in it, so the hill is rebuilt from it exactly. |
| `mole` | `home` or `afloat`. |
| `worm` | `dry` or `squelching`. |
| `badger` | `dry` or `rained`. |
| `shown` | The ids of the grounds whose first showing has started, each at most once. |

What is not stored, and why: the duck's height, its mud and its sigh are views of what the bath holds in `cells` (the water's surface, loose sand in the water, a hot stone in it); a boot that steam has shrunk, the mole's fogged glasses, a scratch on rock and the drift of the floating chair are short-lived and gone on load; grains, drops and puffs in the air are short-lived and gone on load, and the cells they came from or land in are already in `cells`; the spring is a rule over `cells`; the dark of wet sand is the wet form of the cell. The largest legal state is the fixed-length `cells` string and the short fields beside it, about 23 KB, under half of the 64 KB cap, and a test says so.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Four characters live in the hill. Each has one want that is always visible, and likes and dislikes that never change, so a child can learn them and test them on purpose. Their feelings are about what happens in the hill and never about the child, and none of them hurries anyone or notices a put-away (pack: game-design, characters-with-opinions.md). Each moves like itself and answers a tap as itself.

- **The duck** wants a bath. It sits in the dry bath in a shower cap with a back brush in its wing and looks up toward the pond. *Likes:* water of any kind, muddy included; warm water most of all, so in a bath that a hot stone has boiled it sinks to the beak and sighs. *Dislikes:* loose sand in the bath, which it shakes off its cap with a sneeze. *Moves:* quick and jerky, head first, with a tail that waggles after every move. *Tapped:* it squashes flat and squeaks, dusty when dry and bright when afloat.
- **The mole** wants to finish his book. He sits in his armchair under a lamp, reading, and does not look up. *Likes:* quiet; and floating, though he would not say so: flooded, he lifts his feet without looking up and goes on reading as the chair drifts. *Dislikes:* noise, so a scraped rock puts his fingers in his ears and a dropped one makes his book jump; and steam, which fogs his glasses so that he wipes them on a slipper. *Moves:* slow and heavy, belly first, with a nose that twitches by itself. *Tapped:* he lifts the book in front of his face with a low harrumph.
- **The worm** wants every boot polished and in its row. It stands among its boots with a cloth, polishing. *Likes:* wet boots and mud: when water fills them it pulls on as many as it can and squelches up and down. *Dislikes:* steam, which shrinks a boot until it will not go on; and loose sand in a boot, which it tips out one boot at a time. *Moves:* in a wave that runs from tail to head, never all at once. *Tapped:* it shoots down into the nearest boot with a pop and looks out of another.
- **The badger** wants the washing dry. She stands at her line on the hillside with a peg in her mouth and a basket, pegging. *Likes:* wind, and steam, in which she holds a shirt to press it. *Dislikes:* rain on the line: she wrings each piece in turn and holds her umbrella over the smallest sock only. *Moves:* brisk and square, shoulders first, with a tail that flicks when she is cross. *Tapped:* she snaps a peg at the air and flaps a shirt straight.

The residents' places lie beside the short way on every hill, so that a tunnel a little off line finds one of them.

## The scenes

A scene is a list of timed beats filled in from the state of play. It is the consequence of what the child just did, lasts 4 to 10 seconds, and gives way to any touch: the touch jumps it to its last pose and is then an ordinary touch (pack: game-design, endings-and-short-scenes.md). The water and the sand never stop for a scene. On load no scene plays: every character is in the pose its stored field names.

- **The mole afloat.** *Cause:* the water in the sitting room reaches the seat of the armchair. *Saves when it starts:* `mole: afloat`. *Beats, about 6 s:* his slippers float away; he lifts his feet without looking up; the chair comes off the floor and turns half round; the lampshade drifts by as a boat; he turns the page. *Filled in from:* which side the water came in, since the chair drifts away from it, and how deep it stands. *After it:* he floats for as long as the room holds water.
- **The mole set down.** *Cause:* the water in the room falls below the seat. *Saves when it starts:* `mole: home`. *Beats, about 4 s:* the chair grounds with a bump, he wrings one slipper over the floor, and reads on. *Filled in from:* how deep the water stood. The chair grounds on its own rug: its drift while afloat is short-lived and gone on load, when a floating chair rides over its rug.
- **The worm's boots.** *Cause:* water reaches the first boot. *Saves when it starts:* `worm: squelching`. *Beats, about 6 s:* the boots fill one after another in the order the water reaches them, each with a glug at its own pitch; the worm lies down along the row and pulls the boots on over itself two at a time, until it wears all six, and squelches along. *Filled in from:* the side the water came from, which is the end the boots start to fill at. *After it:* it squelches for as long as the hill stands; nothing dries.
- **The badger's washing.** *Cause:* rain from a steam cloud, or a spout of water, lands on the line. *Saves when it starts:* `badger: rained`. *Beats, about 6 s:* the four pieces sag one by one, the one nearest the rain first; she trots to each piece in turn, the nearest first, and wrings it; she opens the umbrella, holds it over the smallest sock and looks at the cloud. *Filled in from:* where the rain first fell. *After it:* the line stays wet for as long as the hill stands.
- **The first showing of a ground.** *Cause:* the first touch on a hill that holds a ground whose id is not in `shown`. *Saves when it starts:* that id added to `shown`, and in `cells` what the showing puts there (the tea as water on the clay, the sock's water in the moss and on the hot stone, the water on the seed), so a showing cut off by a put-away has still happened and the hill's own rules carry it on. *Beats, about 4 s:* the resident nearest that ground does the new thing once, inside the fiction (pack: game-design, guided-discovery.md). For clay the mole tips his teacup onto it and the tea stands as a pool. For rock the worm knocks a boot on it and nothing gives. For moss the worm wrings a sock over it and it swells. For the hot stone the badger drops a wet sock on it and a puff of steam rises and rains on her head. For seed earth the mole waters a seed and its root shoots across his doorway. *Gives way:* any touch ends it, and what it did to the cells stays done. A showing that a put-away cut off is not played again.
- **The duck floats: the ending.** *Cause:* the bath is three fifths full. *Saves when it starts:* `finished: true` and `position` moved one step up, at once. *Beats, about 8 s:* the duck's tail lifts, it bobs free, squeaks, turns a full circle, squirts a jet from its beak, and every resident turns to look as it is (the mole over his book, afloat or not). *Filled in from:* how the water came (a muddy bath gives a brown duck with a clean ring where the cap was; a boiled one gives the sigh) and each resident's stored state. *After it:* the finished hill stays as long as the child likes, and can still be dug. If the child does nothing, nothing new starts.
- **The plug: the next hill.** *Cause:* the child pulls the plug while `finished` is true. A pull that is let go before the chain is taut drops the plug back. *Saves when it starts:* `finished: false`, `hill` and `variant` of the new hill, its fresh `cells`, and `mole: home`, `worm: dry`, `badger: dry`, at once. *Beats, about 6 s:* the bath empties in a whirlpool, the duck spins down with it, the old hill slides up out of the frame and the new one rises under it with the duck already seated in its dry bath. *Filled in from:* how full the old bath was. *Gives way:* a touch jumps to the new hill at rest. A put-away in the middle finds the new hill at rest on load.

**How a cycle ends and how the next one starts.** A hill ends when the duck floats and stays finished and diggable. The plug on its chain is the one thing that waits, in plain sight in the bath, and it does not move. It calls only as the idle ladder does: once the duck floats and the hands are still, the glow and then the ghost hand rest on its knob. The next hill comes only on the child's pull, and comes with its duck already waiting in a dry bath.

## The look

Written after the style spike, not part of the sheet. First reserved look, **Coloured sand behind glass**; the spike passed on clarity, the lead kept the look after the toy, and the second reserved look was not spiked.

**What it is.** A glass case in a wooden frame, seen from the side, filled with dyed sand. Everything behind the pane is sand or stands in it: flat strong colours with visible grains and no outline anywhere. The hill is bands of sand lying one on another in waves; where sand has been rubbed away the dark back of the case shows, and above the hill the back board is painted as sky.

**Palette.**

- Sand, five dyes: sunflower `#f7c82c`, tangerine `#f07c1e`, raspberry `#e83e78`, violet `#784cc4`, cream `#f8ecd0`. No blue and no green among them, so water, moss and the residents' things never read as sand.
- Dug space: ink violet `#241e38`. Sky board: pale blue `#c4e4ee`.
- Water: `#2696ec` with a pale top line `#96dcff`. Wet sand is its own dye darkened and cooled. Water that stands in a room or in the bath is laid once more, thinly, over whoever stands in it, up to its surface.
- The five other grounds, each one colour family that no sand has: clay is blue-grey and shines pale when wet; rock is dark slate with pale flecks and glistens wet; moss is leaf green, brighter when full and pale green where it has swollen; a hot stone is brick red with orange cracks and glows orange all over when it steams; seed earth is brown, darker wet, with pale seeds and pale roots.
- Steam on its way up is soft pale puffs, no two of a size, each larger the higher it has come, drawn over whatever lies behind them. In the sky it gathers into a cloud of round heaps side by side, each as large as the steam it holds, pale on top and shaded underneath, clear of the top of the glass; the heaps go with the wind as the steam does, and the cloud darkens as it nears the washing. A hot stone that shows itself at the badger's feet stands out of the ground as a dome.
- The bath: white enamel `#f2f4ee` with a blue rim `#2c5caa`. The plug's knob is red on a grey chain.
- The residents' things: teal `#1f8f7f` (the armchair), boot colours, the washing. The frame: honey oak `#c98a3c` with brass screws.

**Materials.**

- *Sand.* The hill is one small picture scaled up to the pane: three grains to a cell's side at full quality, each a little paler or darker than its dye, with a bright grain here and there. Packed sand is close-grained. Loose sand shows every grain. Only the cells that changed, and their neighbours, are painted again.
- *Poured edges.* No band and no tunnel has a straight edge. At a corner of a cell the grain takes after what lies beyond it, so steps are rounded, and along an edge between two loose materials one grain in four is scattered across. The dry grounds (clay, rock, moss, hot stone, earth) take the rounded corners and not the scatter, so they keep a firm shape against the sand.
- *The other grounds.* Each has its own grain: clay in flat strata with a lit top; rock flecked, lit along its top; moss in dark and light tufts with a dark fringe where it hangs; a hot stone in glowing cracks; earth crumbly. A lump of rock or hot stone is a rounded square, never an ellipse with a single cell at its ends, and moss beside the way is a chain of cushions, the two sides out of step.
- *The residents and their things.* Each body part and each piece of furniture is painted once as a flat shape and sprinkled with paler and darker grains inside its own outline, so it reads as poured sand like the hill. The whites of the eyes are part of the head; the pupils are drawn live. The worm's body is one soft line of its own colour with a paler line along its back, through its spine as it stands and down into each boot it wears as it lies. The duck's, the worm's and the badger's follow the finger; the mole's stay on his book and leave it only for what he has just poured, or once for the duck.
- *The glass.* Two slanted bars of light across the pane, and a dark lip where the frame meets it. The frame is the only thing with wood grain and the only thing with no sand grain. It is drawn once and stays on the surface: the game is drawn inside its lip, and the frame is drawn again only after a resize, for a knock on it, and with the idle hint.

**Nothing that reads as a count or a sign.** No row of evenly spaced dots or beads, no crossed lines, no bare ring. The idle glow is two soft discs, one in the other. Steam puffs, ripples, bubbles and sparks are filled discs of unequal size. The lines on the mole's pages are plain bars of unequal length at unequal steps; the picture on his wall is a carrot; the spots on the washing are scattered, not in rows. The water from the mole's can is one wavering ribbon, the badger's apron pocket holds one peg, her basket has one band, and a new scratch on rock wipes the older ones near it, so two never cross. Every disc of a puff, a ripple or a bubble is begun apart from the last, so no sliver joins them. The worm is one long body, never a row of beads. Rain is streaks that lean with the wind, each of its own length and none in line with the next. The gleam on the tin is a sliver pointed at both ends with its shadow along one side, not an oval; the chips off the oak are four of unequal size that fall apart; the badger's peg is two stout halves side by side that open only a little, so it never stands as a fork. The heaps of a cloud differ in size and height, are wider than high and stand at uneven steps, so even a thin tail is a strand of lumps and not a row of discs; the drips under wet washing differ in size and in the height they fall from; and the pond's glints never lie one over the other.

**Lighting.** None is modelled. Daylight from the front; the one lamp is a soft yellow disc on the wall of the mole's room.

**Motion rules.**

- Sand, water, steam, moss and roots move only as the hill's own rules move them, a cell at a time. Nothing in the working material sways, breathes or glows; the idle glow is the one mark laid on it.
- Every scene and every short answer is one row of `timeline.ts`: the model plays it, the figure reads its pose from it and the voice is named in it, so a sound and the move that makes it start together. A test walks every row and every cell of the grid.
- Each resident has its own tempo and its own leading part (`residents.ts`): the duck snaps head first, the mole breathes belly first, the worm runs a wave from tail to head, the badger hitches her shoulders and reaches. No two share a curve, and a test holds that.
- Alive with no touch: two clouds drift, the pinwheel turns, the washing swings, the pond glints, the spring runs as a ribbon with foam when the pond is short and hangs as one trembling drop at the pipe when it is not, and all four residents keep at what they are doing.
- The duck faces the pond, with its brush behind it. Afloat, it rides on whatever lies under it in the bath.
- The wind of a hill blows from the pond's side toward the badger's line, and it is the same wind everywhere on that hill: the two clouds, the pinwheel, the spring's drop and a cloud of steam all go with it. The badger stands at the end of her line the wind comes from, so on the frame where the line lies left of the pond the whole drying ground is drawn the other way round.
- Rain is a streak that is seen to fall from the cloud to where it lands, never water standing in the sky. The piece it lands on sags, darkens and drips. A wisp of steam rains as soon as it is over her head; a cloud carries on along the line and lets go a drop here and a drop there. Sand drinks a drop and goes dark; on anything else it lands as water.
- A showing changes the hill on the beat where that is drawn: the tea is on the clay when the cup tips, the sock's water in the moss when it is wrung. A put-away before that beat stores the hill as if it had been poured.
- A touch on the bath's tin leaves a sliver of light with a dark edge that runs down the tin. A touch on the oak frame knocks a few dark chips off the wood, which fall; they are the only mark ever drawn on the frame. The frame does not shake.
- The badger stands in front of her line, beside the piece she has in her paws and never over it. A piece she wrings is twisted thin and its water runs out of it to the ground in one wavering stream. Her umbrella stands upright on a long handle with its canopy over the smallest sock and the sock in plain sight under it. When she snaps a peg, the peg is in her paw.
- Wet sand comes away under the finger as blobs of mud that slide straight down the tunnel's wall. A pool that stands on a hot stone outside the bath boils away as steam faster than the spring fills it; in the bath a hot stone only boils.

**What fills the frame.** The wooden frame on all four sides; the sky with the pinwheel, two clouds and the badger at her line of washing, the smallest sock nearest her and, once it has rained, the umbrella open in her paw over that sock; the pond with its pipe; the mole's sitting room with armchair, lamp, picture, table and teacup; the worm's boot room with six boots, a shelf of hat boxes and an umbrella; the bath in its chamber at the bottom with the duck, the brush and the plug on its chain; and bands of sand everywhere between. Three frames place these differently (pond left or right, the line left or right, the duck at either end of the bath). Under the home control at the top centre there is sky only, and a test holds that on every layout. A touch in the top right corner is the grown-up's and the game does not answer it.

**How each tier keeps the look.** Tier 0: pixel ratio 2, three grains to a cell's side, up to 140 grains in the air. Tier 1: ratio 1.5, two grains, 90 in the air. Tier 2: ratio 1.25, two grains, 50 in the air. Tier 3: ratio 1, one grain, 24 in the air, and no rounded or scattered edges. The hill, the water and every resident are the same on every tier.

**The frame's cost.** A frame is one stamp of the sand's picture (the only one that covers the surface), sprites, and pen fills for pupils, water, glints, marks, steam and grains in the air; every resident's room is one picture until something happens to it. The oak frame, the pinwheel's post and the whites behind the mole's lenses cost nothing in a frame: the first stays on the surface, the second is part of the sand's picture, the third is part of his head. The plug, its cord and its knob are one sprite, the worm's body is two strokes, and rain is filled with the water that leaves the washing. Counted as stamps plus pen strokes and fills: 45 at rest on any hill with the idle glow and the hand, 41 to 54 through the residents' scenes and short answers, 37 to 62 through the plug's scene, and 68 at the worst the tests reach: a rub on a soaked hill with grains of every kind in the air and steam, a cloud, rain and the badger's peg all in the frame. A model test draws every state a child can reach on a recording canvas and holds each frame to 72, and holds that no figure is drawn over ground.
