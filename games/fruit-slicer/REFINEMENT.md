### Template notes

- `symbols.ts` (written here as the pilot, and since taken into the template by the lead). It draws a whole number, a fraction with a horizontal bar, a mixed number, the eight signs by name, and a decimal number with a point or a comma; it takes numbers, a fraction as two integers or a sign by name, never a string; it imports nothing and knows nothing of fruit, and its test holds that. What writing it showed:
  - The check refuses a helper that takes the text as a parameter, so every `fillText` and `strokeText` formats its number in place (`String(Math.round(n))`) and carries its own `wordless-ok: numeral` comment. An outline under a fill is therefore two commented calls, not one helper.

  - The decimal number is drawn digit by digit on cells of one width, since a comma cannot be put into a formatted number without a call the check refuses.

### For the owner to decide

The game works under every default of the guide as written and asks for none to be changed. These are for the owner all the same:

- **The give of a tin**: a cut within one twenty-fourth of the fruit's length counts as a fit. No record sets it. It decides how often a first cut succeeds, so it is the first thing to judge with a child.
- **No fraction on a piece the child cut.** Under the default on readings, the written fraction is laid only on what was ordered (the ticket and the tin), never on a cut piece. A child therefore never sees a name for a piece of its own making. If the owner would like a piece that lies exactly on ruled parts to show its fraction, that is a change to the default.
- **A piece rings by its length** (half the length, an octave higher). It is true of a string and is used as a toy, not taught. Whether it stays is a matter of taste.
- **The look**: comic-book halftone, after the look pass: a full stall with a street behind it and large customers. He has not answered on this game, and it is not brought in without his yes.
- **The toy**: the slice. Whether cutting fruit again and again, with its thwack, its juice on the wall and the dog, is a pleasure with nothing to achieve is his to say.
- **Carrying**: a finger that lands on a piece takes hold of it, and one that lands anywhere else is the blade. Whether that reads to a child, or whether a piece should be cut wherever the finger lands, is his to try.
- **How full the counter is**: the stall's panel is full now and the two who wait are half as large again as they were, but the board and the shelf are still two plain pale slabs, empty at rest. Dressing them further would put things on what the child measures with.
- **Slivers**: a child who cuts a fruit into its thinnest slivers with no tin open and then lays them in a tin one by one until the lid shuts has, by the rule, cut every piece by eye, and such a cycle moves the place in the designed order as any other that goes well. It takes many strokes and the customer's body shows every sliver, and the sheet's own example of filling an order from pieces left over rests on the same rule. Whether a tin filled with crumbs should count as mixed is a change to how a cycle is judged, and so the owner's.
- **The voices**: nobody has heard them.

### How the game meets the quality bar

The game is built. No frame rate has been measured on any machine, no physical iPad has been used, and nobody has heard a sound.

- **Alive at idle.** Every character breathes at its own tempo and, after a rest, does one small thing of its own, never the same twice running: the pelican blinks, preens, gapes and shuffles; the twins sniff, startle, squabble and groom; the ants wave their feelers, drill and turn about; the cat blinks slowly, flicks its tail, yawns and looks away; the boa flicks its tongue, sways and shifts its coils; the dog blinks, flicks an ear, sniffs, tilts its head, yawns and pants. The awning's scallops sway out of step. Every eye follows a finger that is down. In the street behind the stall three passers-by go by at their own paces, with long gaps between. All of it runs on the attended clock and stops when the game rests. Tests hold that no two actions are alike, within a character or across them.
- **Motion and sound on every touch.** The blade and its ring, or a piece taken hold of with its own pop, are there when the finger lands. Every cell of the grid answers: six things by five acts, each drawn and each heard, and a test holds the thirty-two answers different in name and in sound. A seeded play of the whole game holds that every one of them is reached. The sheet was read against the code sentence by sentence, and what it promised that was only heard or only counted is now drawn; each customer has its own noise.
- **Weight, squash and follow-through.** Pieces hop and land with a squash that never changes their length; a fresh fruit drops in and settles; a carried piece is lifted with a hard shadow; the crate rocks, the awning flaps and the tin jolts, each on its own spring; a customer rolled flat springs back, and a snipped tuft pops back. The answer is bigger than the touch: a cut sprays a burst and up to eleven drops that splat on the wall and on the bare wood; a poke gets an impact star; the others stare.
- **Kid-clear.** Two panels, heavy outlines. The fruit, the board, the shelf, the tin and the tickets are flat; the dots are on the street, the counter, the crate and the characters. The street is in thin blue line behind a veil of paper, so the customers are the darkest things in their panel and the fruit, the tickets and the tin the plainest on the page. The customer at the window stands as tall as its panel. Every length on the counter starts from one left edge at one scale. Lanes and rows are 54 units tall.
- **Wordless clarity for the declared age.** No word or letter anywhere. Digits, the fraction bar and the three comparison signs are drawn only by `symbols.ts`: the fraction on a bracket over the share it names on a ticket and on the lid of the open tin, the sign between the cat's two tickets, with the two strips it compares on either side of it, and only once the tin has opened; none appears before the position named `written`, and the shut tin carries nothing. `npm run wordless:check` passes.

- **Found as left.** The world changes on the touch and what moves afterwards, a scene included, only shows what has already happened. Tests put the game away at every step of a stroke, a carry and each scene and open it exactly as it was, with nothing replaying; a seeded play holds the same after each of several thousand touches.
- **How a cycle restarts.** The served customer stays at the window with its tin for as long as the child likes, the two who wait are in view the whole time, and nothing new starts until the child touches one of them. That one steps up, and the served one leaves like itself as it does, in a second or less; it has left the game on the touch, so no touch waits for it.
- **Nothing passes through anything.** A canvas game: its own model tests. A seeded play of the whole game, at every position of the designed order, holds after every touch that no two pieces cross as drawn, that each lies on its slab, and that the model agrees with itself. It found one crossing, in the twins' tin, which is fixed.
- **No engagement mechanics.** No score, count, streak, timer or reward. The position in the designed order is never shown; a cycle is judged for one purpose only, and a customer's taste never enters it.

### The learning claim

### Defaults taken for the owner

From the guide, each kept as written:

- No symbol stands alone: every fraction and sign sits on or beside the length it names, and no order can be known only by reading one.
- No letters and no written words, on a ticket or anywhere else. The comic look is drawn without its sound words.
- No reading on the object: nothing is written on a piece the child cut, and no number says how close a cut was. How close it was is the gap or the overhang.
- No camera shake and no impact pause: the slice answers with its chain, its sound and squash.
- No speech: the characters' voices are invented and synthesized.
- The look is the first row the lead reserved; the second was not spiked.
- The demo's verb was changed (its flying fruit and clock are gone); its swing of the blade and its wet slice are kept.

From the game's own sheet:

- The give of a tin is one twenty-fourth of the fruit's length to either side.
- The parts in play are halves, quarters, eighths, thirds, sixths, fifths, tenths and twelfths; hundredths are left out.
- Written fractions start at the fifth position, after halves and quarters have been cut by eye.
- A first visit from age 11 opens where the notation first appears.
- A piece rings by its length, as a string does.

### What the next builder should know

- Decide what the tin's give is in the same unit as the shortest piece a cut can make. Here both are one twenty-fourth of the fruit, so anything thinner than the give is not a piece, and the largest state is bounded by one number.
- Keep every length a whole number of points, with a whole that divides by every part in play and by twice each. Two quarters then lie exactly on a half with no rounding, and "fits" never depends on floating point.
- A tolerance that is fair for an order is too loose for a taste. With twelfths, a give of one twenty-fourth lets every length count as "a whole number of parts". The ants' taste uses the give or a quarter of a part, whichever is smaller.
- A test that lays out hundreds of customers at every position and holds each to the limits found two faults the eye had missed: the twins ordering a share whose halves were not yet in play, and the cat ordering a whole.
- A position's id can be derived from what a customer carries, so "shown once" needs no extra field on the customer: `ideaOf` in `orders.ts`.
- On load, do not re-place saved pieces through the rule that sets a piece down: that rule snaps to a neighbour, and a legal state would open changed. Keep a saved place when it is free and fall back to the rule only when it is not.
- For a still on a machine with no graphics card, a canvas 2D scene is drawn as it will look; only the frame rate is unknown.
- Let the world change on the touch and let every movement afterwards be an effect that only shows how. Found as left then costs nothing: there is never anything in the air to put down before a save.
- Keep the toy's runtime out of the Mount, in a class that takes gestures and seconds and gives a frame and a list of sounds. A test can then play the whole toy, a put-away in the middle of a stroke included.
- A stroke is a run of short steps. Test the step that ends exactly on the line it is meant to cross: with a strict comparison on both sides it cuts never, and a child's straight slow stroke hits that case.
- A list of the things that left the world has to be read from the world before they left; and when a cut piece is itself what drops, it drops at its new length.
- Give the painters the context to draw on and have them return what they drew. A stand-in that counts calls then gives a frame budget that does not depend on the machine.
- Play the whole game with seeded touches in a test, and after every touch check what a child would see cross and that the save opens as it was. It is a few dozen lines once the runtime is out of the Mount, it found a crossing no hand-written case had, and its list of what happened shows which states the play never reached.
- A hurried drag is not a throw. Decide a throw by what the piece is let go over, not by speed alone: over a thing, it is given to the thing.
- Do not let the thing that shows the truth give it away early. The tin is exactly as long as the order, so the shut tin had to be folded small, or its length on the rail was the answer before the cut.
- A scene's sounds are cues that start with their beat; when a touch ends the scene, the template plays every unfinished beat once, cues included, so the game mutes them for that call.
- When the state moves on before the scene plays, keep what the scene needs to show (the ending, the customer who left) beside the scene, and drop it when the scene is over. The saved state never needs it.
- Before the gates, read the sheet against the game sentence by sentence, and for every number a pure module works out, find the figure that reads it. Here five were computed and tested and never drawn (a hiccup, a spin, a lift, a feather), and the tests were green all along: a test of the number says nothing about the picture.
- To make a bare frame full without touching what the child measures on: make the characters' panel taller and draw them as large as it allows; paint a place behind them in a second, fainter line (here thin blue, behind a veil of paper) so that nothing in it is ever as dark as a character; put everything still on the plate; and let the edge have a life of its own that answers no touch. Then look at every new shape as a reader of signs would: a pane with crossed bars, a hook that is an upright and a crossbar, a round attic pane, a ball on a post.
- A reaction that the others have to what happens to one of them is cheap and reads at once: one more reaction on the sheet each character already has, started for everyone but the one it happened to, and left alone when a character is busy.
