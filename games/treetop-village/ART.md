# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

Treetop Village has no learning goal. The idea the child feels is a piece clicking into a tree and someone small trying it at once: a great oak with doors in its trunk, a basket of woodland building pieces at its foot, and five tree folk who use whatever ways the child has made, each in its own way and always the same way.

## The band and its age rule

The manifest band is 4 to 8, and its youngest age, four, governs the design.

- **The cue-table row.** The row for a youngest age of 3 to 4 in the wordless-clarity convention. Its "Avoid" column binds the game: no text, numeral or pictorial icon that must be decoded, no spoken instruction, no verdict, no several activities live at once, no tool present before it means anything. One activity is live, building in the tree, and one next act is offered at a time by the idle ladder.
- **The pack's rule for the range.** Pretend play with characters who react: the game supplies a place, props and five characters, and the child supplies the plot. Tap and drag-and-drop only, no double tap, no reading. The victim of a joke is bewildered and never hurt (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md).
- **The symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, optional or not, and the game has no `symbols.ts`. The squirrel's letter is a blank folded leaf, and the doors carry no sign: each door is its owner's colour and shape.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only what a first visit finds in the tree (under "The designed order"). It never gates anything: every piece, every place and every walker is there at every age.
- **What no age gives.** `null` gives the youngest start.

## The toy

**The action.** Carry a piece from the basket to a place in the tree and let go. That is the touch the finger performs most.

**In an empty scene.** The oak stands in the middle of an open pop-up book with nine landings on its limbs and the meadow at its foot. Between the landings are twelve places where a piece can go, each marked by a pair of plain pale pegs: seven rises, from a landing up to the one above, and five spans, between two landings at one height. A flat basket lies along the front of the page with seven pieces in it: a plank, a ladder, a rope bridge, a bucket on a rope, a swing, a string of lanterns and a bark slide. The five tree folk stand on the landings of their doors, each on its own spot. Nothing asks for anything. Every piece is plain, with no face and no pattern, and none moves unless the child or a walker moves it.

- **When the finger lands on a piece in the basket,** in that frame, the piece jumps up under the finger with a papery flick and a knock of wood, stretched a little as it comes, and every walker's eyes turn to it. The basket shows another of the same kind in its place, so no kind ever runs out.
- **While it is carried** the piece hangs from the finger and swings behind the hand's motion. The walkers' eyes follow it. The pair of pegs nearest the piece opens outwards as it comes near, the pegs of a free place only.
- **Let go near a free place,** the piece snaps on: it unfolds between the two pegs like a pop-up, overshoots and settles, with a wooden knock whose pitch is the place's own (low in the tree is low). The leaf cards above shake and a leaf drops. Then the nearest walker who can get to it comes out and tries it in its own way (the grid below), and stays where it took them. One that is in the middle of a try takes the new piece up when that try ends, the newest if the child has hung several, and one that stood stopped on its way somewhere goes on with that afterwards.
- **Let go anywhere else,** the piece flutters back to the basket, turning over and over like a dropped card, and lands with a soft pat that makes the basket hop.
- **A finger that lands on a piece in the tree** gets a knock of that piece's wood and a jog of the piece in that frame. Drawn away, the piece comes off its pegs with a pop and is carried like any other: to another place, or back to the basket. A piece that a walker is standing on, hanging from or riding in does not come off: it shakes under the finger, and the walker on it holds on and squeaks.
- **A tap on a piece in the tree**, the finger lifted where it landed, makes that piece answer as itself: a plank twangs, a ladder rattles down its rungs, a rope bridge ripples from one end to the other, a bucket runs to its other end, a swing swings, the lanterns chime and glow for a moment, an acorn rattles down the slide.
- **A tap on a walker** gets that walker's own flourish when the finger lands (under "The characters"), and then it sets off: from its own door to the newest piece in the tree, or, when there is no newest piece or no way leads to it, to the nearest piece it can get to past pieces it crosses, and from anywhere else back to its door. A walker at its door with no piece it can get to keeps to its flourish.
- **A tap on a bare pair of pegs** makes them knock together, tok. **A tap on the oak** shakes the leaf cards with a rustle and drops one acorn, which bounces on the page and rolls into the gutter.

**Why repeating it is a pleasure with no goal.** One let-go is answered by the snap, the unfolding, the shaking leaves and then a small walker who comes to see what the child put there: a chain the child set going (pack: game-design, touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md). Any piece clicks into any place, so the simplest use always works and random building always makes something a walker has an opinion about. A piece that misses flutters home, which costs nothing and is pretty to watch (pack: game-design, toy-first.md). A person watching sees within three seconds that the child is hanging things in a tree for small animals to climb on.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

Seven pieces, four kinds of walker (the mouse twins are two walkers who go as a pair), and two ways a piece can be fitted: on a rise or on a span. The four middle columns are each walker on the piece fitted the way it is drawn in the basket; the last column is the wrong use every piece has, fitted the other way. Every cell is what is seen and heard, and it is the same every time.

A cell ends in one of three ways, which is the whole system: the walker **gets across** and goes on; it **stays**: when its turn on the piece is over it is back on the landing it came from, safe, looking over at where it was going; or it is **carried**, which only the child can do, by sending the bucket, and until then it waits in or on the bucket at its own landing. No walker comes to rest part-way along a piece.

**How the last column ends.** The squirrel gets across every piece fitted the other way. The twins get across the ramp, the flat ladder and the trough, are carried by the cable car, and stay at the net, the hanging swing and the hanging lanterns. The beetle gets across the flat ladder and the trough, comes down the ramp but stays at its foot, is carried by the cable car, and stays at the net, the hanging swing and the hanging lanterns. The owl comes down the ramp, is carried by the cable car and stays at everything else.

**Which ways go up, across and down** follows from the grid and never changes: a ladder for every step the beetle takes up; a slide or a ramp to come down; a bucket for anyone who waits; nothing that hangs gets the twins past it, a bucket apart; no rope under the owl's feet; and no swing on a span for the squirrel.

**Routes are remade from whatever ways exist.** A walker with somewhere to go takes a way it can get all the way along, where the tree has one, however long: every piece on it is one it crosses or a bucket it waits for. Where the tree has none, it takes the shortest way whoever the pieces suit and goes as far as the first piece that stops it. It meets each piece as the grid says. Where it has to stay, it stays, on a landing, and looks across. When the child lets a piece go, onto the tree or off it, or sends a bucket, every walker that still has somewhere to go and now has another way sets off again from where it stands.

**The wrong use of each thing works and is funny.**

- A piece fitted the other way: the last column.
- A piece let go where there is no place: it flutters back to the basket.
- A piece hung where no walker can get to it: it snaps on all the same, and the nearest walker leans out towards it from where it stands and makes its own small asking sound.
- A piece pulled at while someone is on it: it shakes and the walker holds on.
- A bucket sent with nobody in it: it runs empty, squeaking, and bumps the far end.
- A walker tapped while it is on its way: the flourish, and it goes on.

**On day 15** the child knows what each of the five will and will not use, and builds for one of them on purpose: a way home for the owl that is all planks, slides and two bucket rides; a ladder left out so the beetle sits halfway and a slide beside it to see him come down on his back again; a string of lanterns across the only way to the twins' door, so that they stop there and swing every evening, until the child gives them a plank round it; a swing where the squirrel must pass. Seven kinds of piece on twelve places, each fitted one of two ways and met by four kinds of walker, with nothing authored beyond the fifty-six meetings of the grid: the same seven pieces make things as well as solve things (pack: game-design, depth-from-combinations.md; pack: game-design, liveliness-from-causing-and-comedy.md).

## The representation

The game has no learning goal, so no school idea is represented and there is no order of object, picture and symbol to stop anywhere.

## The four mechanic questions

The game has no learning goal and its verb, fitting a piece into a tree for someone to use, carries no school skill, so the four questions have no skill to be asked about.

## The error as a consequence

A wrong attempt is a way that the walker who needs it cannot use.

- **Where and why it shows.** The walker goes as far as the piece, tries it as the grid says, and stays on the landing in front of it, turned towards where it was going. The place and the reason are the piece itself: the owl puffed up at the foot of the rope bridge, the beetle sitting at the bottom of the plank he slid down.
- **What it does.** The trying is the funniest thing the piece has, and it is about the walker and the piece, never about the child. A walker that stays is puzzled and safe: it sits or stands on a landing, or waits in or on a bucket that rests at that landing, never part-way along a piece, and nothing happens to it however long it is left.
- **The state stays.** Every piece stays where the child put it, and the walker keeps its errand. The child changes one thing, a ladder in place of the plank, a bucket sent down, a piece added round the other way, and the walker sets off again by itself (pack: game-design, errors-show-as-consequences.md).

A piece in an odd place is no error: every piece clicks into every place and does something there.

## The designed order, and what is stored

**The designed order.** There is none to climb: the game is open pretend play in one tree. `LADDER` in `config.ts` holds the single id `oak`, which names the place and no grade, groep or level, and `FIRST_VISIT` has one row.

- **What a first visit starts with, by age.** At 5 and younger, and with no age: three pieces are already in the tree, a ladder from the meadow to the low left limb, a plank across the two low limbs and a string of lanterns from the far left limb to the middle left one, so the tree shows what a piece in a place looks like before the child has carried one. At 6 and older: a bare tree. At every age the basket holds all seven kinds and the five walkers stand at their own doors.
- **What play changes.** Which piece is in which place, which end each bucket rests at, where each walker stands and where it is going, which piece is the newest, and whether it is day or night. Nothing else: the twelve places, the four doors and the grid never change, so what a child finds out stays true.
- **A harder thing is chosen by the child and looks harder in the world**: a door higher in the tree, a walker who will use fewer things, a way round that needs more pieces than the short way.
- **There are no records**: the game has no learning goal and is designed from no learning standard.

**Every field of the saved state** (version 1, plain JSON, read field by field, each repaired by itself):

| Field | Holds | Default when missing or damaged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `v` | The version, 1 | A record at any other version is unreadable and gives a first visit's tree |
| `position` | `oak` | `oak` |
| `finished` | Always `false`: open play has no finished cycle | `false` |
| `places` | For each of the twelve places, the kind of piece in it or none | None in that place |
| `lifts` | For each of the twelve places, which of its two ends the bucket rests at; read only where the place holds a bucket | The lower end of a rise, the left end of a span |
| `folk` | For each of the four kinds of walker (the twins share one entry), the landing it stands on and its errand: none, home, or the number of the place it is going to try | At its own door, with no errand |
| `newest` | The place whose piece was fitted last, or none | None |
| `night` | Whether the sun is down | Day |

- **A place that names a kind the game does not know** is empty, and an errand to a place with nothing in it is no errand.
- **Nothing is saved in the air.** A piece in the hand is saved where it came from: in its place if it was lifted off the tree, and not at all if it came from the basket, which never runs out. A piece fluttering back is already home. A put-away under a dragging finger ends as `dragCancel`, and the piece goes back where it came from.
- **A walker on its way is saved where its walk will end.** The way is worked out when the walk starts, from the pieces in the tree and the grid, so the landing it will reach or stay at is known then and is written to `folk` at once. On load every walker stands on its saved landing and nothing is walked again. A walker waiting for a bucket is on its landing with its errand kept; whether it sits in the bucket is rebuilt from `lifts`, and where two wait at one end of a bucket the load seats the first of them in the order owl, twins, beetle.
- **Short-lived, and gone on load:** the falling leaf and the acorn, a swinging swing, a rippling bridge, lanterns glowing from a tap by day, and whichever way a walker was looking.
- **Size.** The largest legal state is twelve places, twelve lift ends and four walkers, a few hundred bytes, and a test holds it under half of the 64 KB cap.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Each is a printed card figure, large in the frame, whose one want can be seen at all times, and each moves in its own way and never in another's.

- **The squirrel postman** wants to deliver. He holds a folded leaf letter out in front of him in one paw, at his door and everywhere else, and looks from it to the tree. He lives behind the round door on the middle left limb. He is quick, cannot stand still, and runs anything that will bear him: planks, ladders, bridges, ropes, strings. He will not wait for a bucket, and the seat of a swing throws him back every time. *Tapped:* he snaps to attention, salutes with his tail and drops the letter, then snatches it up.
- **The owl** wants to be carried. She stands very upright with her wings folded and looks at the nearest bucket if there is one, and at the basket if there is not. She lives in the hollow on the high right limb. She walks only on what is flat and firm, comes down anything she can sit on, never climbs, and will not set a foot on rope. *Tapped:* her head goes right round once with a wooden ratchet and she blinks one eye.
- **The mouse twins** want something to swing on. One always holds the other's tail, and both look up at whatever hangs nearest. They live behind the small double door on the low right limb. They go anywhere on planks, ladders and slides, and stop at anything that hangs, a bridge, a swing, a lantern, the rope of a bucket, because they have to swing on it. *Tapped:* they jump, bump heads, sit down and point at each other.
- **The old beetle** wants a ladder. He stands with both hands on his stick and looks up at the next landing above him. He lives behind the low door between the roots, on the meadow. He takes every step up on a ladder and no other way, will ride in a bucket with his hat off, crosses anything flat or slatted, and comes down slides and ramps on his back. *Tapped:* he lifts his hat, his wing cases pop open with a click and he shuts them again, embarrassed.

No walker's feeling is ever about the child, and none refers to the child stopping, waiting or coming back. A walker that stays is puzzled by the piece, not let down by anyone (pack: game-design, characters-with-opinions.md).

## The scenes

Every scene is the consequence of what the child just did and is a list of timed beats on attended game time. A try runs alongside the child's touches and never makes one wait: a touch during a try is an ordinary touch and the walk goes on. A carry, dusk, morning and the shake give way to any touch: the touch jumps the tree to the scene's saved outcome and is then an ordinary touch. A beat that makes a sound stays quiet when a touch is ending the scene.

- **A try** (2 to 8 seconds). *Cause:* a piece snaps onto a place, a piece that was in the tree is let go anywhere else, or a walker is tapped. *Beats:* the walker turns, goes by its way, meets each piece on it as the grid says, and stops where it gets across to or has to stay. *Saved when it starts:* `places`, `lifts` and `newest` as the change leaves them, and `folk` for every walker it sets going: the landing its walk will end on and its errand, kept while it has to stay and none once it has arrived. When a change in the tree sends a walker on, its `folk` is written again at that moment. *Filled in from the state of play:* which walker, which pieces lie on its way and how each is fitted. A try does not hold the child up: pieces can be carried while it plays, and a change in the tree sends the walker on from the next landing it reaches.
- **A carry** (2 to 3 seconds). *Cause:* a tap on a bucket. *Beats:* the pulley turns, the bucket runs to its other end with whoever waits in it or on it, they get out, and each goes on with its errand as a try. *Saved when it starts:* `lifts` for that place, and `folk` for each rider and for each walker the carry sets going: where its walk will end and its errand. *Filled in from:* who was waiting, and what lies beyond.
- **Dusk** (6 to 10 seconds). *Cause:* the sun, which stands on a pull tab at the top left of the back page, is drawn down behind the hill and let go below the hill's top. Let go above it, the sun springs back up and it stays day. *Beats:* the evening sheet slides up over the whole spread from the foot of the page, the lanterns that are hung light one after another from the lowest, the walkers set off for their doors along the child's ways, a window lights in each door as its owner goes in, and a walker that cannot get home stops where it has to stay, in the light of whatever lantern is near. After six seconds the evening is finished; a walk home that takes longer goes on as a try. The finished evening stays as long as the child likes. *Saved when it starts:* `night`, and `folk` for all four: where each walk will end, with the errand home kept by those who have to stay and none for those who arrive. *Filled in from:* which lanterns hang where, which ways exist, and where each walker stood.
- **Morning** (3 to 5 seconds). *Cause:* the moon on the same tab is lifted at least half way up its slot and let go. Let go lower, it sinks back and it stays night. *Beats:* the evening sheet slides down, the lanterns go out, each walker that is at home steps out onto its sill and stretches in its own way, and every piece is where it was. *Saved when it starts:* `night`, and `folk`: every errand is none. *Filled in from:* who got home and who did not.
- **The shake** (2 to 6 seconds: about two on a bare tree, and a third of a second more for each piece that comes off). *Cause:* the trunk is pulled to one side and back across the other way twice without letting go; a single pull and let-go only springs the tree back with a rustle and an acorn. *Beats:* the tree rocks, the pieces come off their pegs one by one from the top and flutter down into the basket, each with its own knock, the walkers fold down flat where they stand as the tree rocks and pop up again looking out of their own doors, and the tree stands bare. *Saved when it starts:* `places` (all empty), `lifts` (all at their defaults), `newest` (none) and `folk` (each at its own door with no errand). `night` is left as it is. *Filled in from:* which pieces were where.

**How a cycle ends and the next one starts.** Dusk is the ending the child chooses, and it stays: after it, if the child does nothing, nothing new starts. Lifting the sun is the obvious act that begins again, with everything in place. The shake is the calm way to tidy up, and it is the child's own act. On load no scene replays: the tree is found by day or by night as it was left, lanterns burning if it is night, every walker on its saved landing (pack: game-design, endings-and-short-scenes.md; pack: game-design, the-world-keeps-and-waits.md).

**What the idle ladder shows.** The glow lies on the pieces in the basket, and the ghost hand carries one of them to a free place, a different piece and a different place each time. Any piece does something in any place, so the hand shows a move and never an answer. When every place is full, the glow lies on the walkers and the hand taps one. Whenever a walker waits in or on a bucket, or for one, the glow lies on that bucket and the hand taps it. One time in four by day the hand draws the sun down its tab instead, and every other time by night it lifts the moon.

**Nothing covers a target.** No place, piece, walker, door or basket slot lies under the round home control at the top centre or in the top right corner. A walker that comes to rest stands on its own spot of a landing, or, where it has to stay, at the foot of the piece that stopped it: no two walkers have the same spot, and the middle of every rise that starts on a landing is never behind the card of a walker resting there, so a piece on it can always be touched. A tap that lands on a walker in front of a piece is the walker's; the rest of the piece is still the piece's. The one exception is a walker that waits in or on a bucket: a tap on it gets its flourish and sends the bucket, because a child who touches a full bucket means the ride. `tree.test.ts` holds these.

## The look

Written after the style spike, not part of the sheet.

**Pop-up book spread**, the first look reserved for the game in `docs/art-direction.md`. The spike passed on clarity at 1180×820 and pixel ratio 2, so the second reserved look, folded origami paper, was not tried. No frame rate has been measured: that is the lead's, on a production build through the shared probe. The registry row at the end of this file is a request to the lead.

### What the look is

The whole surface is one open spread of a pop-up book, seen from the reader's chair. Everything is printed card that stands up from the page. Nothing is modelled, lit or outlined.

- **A card.** A flat bright shape with no outline. It reads as card through two copies of its own shape: a pale one a little up and to the left, the cut edge catching the daylight, and a soft dark one down and to the right, the small shadow it throws on what stands behind it. `card()` in `paint.ts` is the only way a card is drawn, so every card has the same edge and the same light.
- **Print.** Detail on a card (a belly, a leaf vein, bark lines, a door knob) is flat print with no edge and no shadow of its own. Lines are few, round-ended and dark brown, never black.
- **The folds.** A card that stands on the page stands on a fold: a pale strip at its foot, a soft shadow on the page, and small paper tabs under the large cards (the oak, the stump, the toadstool, the bushes). The page has a gutter down the middle with a soft gradient across it, a crease across the base page, and the stacked edges of the book's other pages along the front and the sides.
- **The palette.** Daylight colours of a printed picture book: two sky blues, two hill greens, a meadow green, four leaf greens with gold, warm trunk browns, and a pale cream wood for everything the child builds, so a piece of the child's is told from the tree at a glance. The walkers hold the strongest colours: russet, tawny, lilac and deep blue, with one red and one teal scarf. The palette lives in `C` in `paint.ts`.
- **The pieces are plain and working.** Cream wood, pale rope with a dark core so it shows on the trunk and on the sky alike, yellow lantern glass. A piece has no face. The comedy belongs to the walkers. In the basket the plank and the slide are drawn stouter than they hang in the tree, with a dark edge, so they read on the dark felt.
- **Nothing printed is a sign.** The squirrel's letter is a folded green leaf with a rib and a stalk, a card of its own that he can drop. The owl's breast flecks and the toadstool's spots are uneven blobs of different sizes, unevenly spaced. Each door has one knob, and its owner's colour and shape.

### What stands in the frame

Filled from edge to edge, from the first still: the oak with its three doors and its hollow, six leaf cards of crown that reach both top corners, the stump on the left and the toadstool on the right (both landings of the tree's ways), two rows of hills with small houses, fences, four bushes, flowers and mushrooms along the front, a bird on a spring, a snail, two clouds, the sun on its pull tab, and the basket across the foot of the page. The four walker cards are the largest things after the oak: the squirrel postman about 120 high, the owl about 100, the beetle about 100, the twins about 70 each and always together. A walker is already funny standing still: the squirrel holds a letter out to nobody, the owl's brows meet in the middle, the beetle leans on a stick under a bowler hat, and one twin holds the other's tail.

### Evening in this look

Evening is a sheet of blue tracing paper slid up over the spread from the foot of the page, not a change of light. Every card has a second printing with the sheet's blue laid over it (`@n` in `render.ts`), and a card takes it when the sheet's edge passes its foot, so the edge is seen to travel up the page and nothing fades. Above the edge the page is still day. Three things are not under the sheet, because they are light: the lanterns that are hung (each glass with a warm halo), the window in each door whose owner is in, and the moon. The moon is a round cream card asleep in a blue nightcap, on the same pull tab the sun stands on. Small stars are pricked through the sheet where the day's sky is, and nowhere on the oak, the hills or a landing. The sun is drawn down its slot to make evening and the moon is lifted up it to make morning; the tab springs back when it is let go short of either.

### How things move in this look

- **Cards do not bend.** A card slides, tips on its fold, turns on a pin, or flips over. Squash is the whole card scaled from its foot, as a pop-up leans when the page moves. No part of a card stretches by itself.
- **One pinned part each.** Every walker has one part on a pin of its own, which carries its character: the squirrel's tail, the owl's head, the beetle's hat, and the twins are two cards. The beetle has a second printing with his wing cases swung open over his pale wings, and each twin one with an arm pointing at the other, shown in their flourishes. The pupils are the only thing drawn live, so every walker looks at what is happening.
- **A piece unfolds.** A piece that snaps on opens up from flat, past its size and back, as a pop-up does when a page is opened. A piece let go in the air turns over and over on its way back to the basket, its width closing and opening.
- **A piece answers as a piece.** Under a walker and under a tap a plank bows, a ladder jogs, a bridge ripples, a swing's seat turns about its pin, a string of lanterns sways and glows, a slide carries an acorn, and a bucket's cup runs, swings and sags. The cup and the swing's seat are cards of their own for that. What turns or travels inside a card is another printing of that card: a bucket's wheels have one peg hole that goes round as the cup runs, and a tapped rope bridge carries one bump from end to end. Nothing of the child's moves by itself: an empty cup hangs still.
- **A piece is touched where it is drawn.** The seat of a swing, a lantern's glass, the cup of a bucket and the sag of a bridge are the piece, as much as the line between its pegs (`shapeOf` in `shape.ts`).
- **A walker is touched where it is drawn.** The squirrel's tail and the letter in his paw, the owl's ear tufts, the tail of the twin behind, the beetle's hat and his stick are the walker, as much as its card (`OUTLINE` in `tree.ts`).
- **The trunk is the oak's.** A bare pair of pegs knocks at its pegs, and along the line between them only where that line is not over the trunk; on the trunk a finger rustles the leaves and can start the shake.
- **A walker that has to stay stands at the foot of the piece,** a step back from its peg, body and eyes turned to it; beside a rise it stands where the middle of the piece stays clear of its card.
- **A walker takes a way it can use.** Where the tree has a way a walker gets all the way along, however long, it takes that one; only where there is none does it go by the shortest way and stay at the first piece that stops it (`routeTo` in `ways.ts`). A new piece goes to the nearest walker with a way to it; a busy one takes it up when its try ends, and one that stood stopped goes on with its old errand afterwards. One that waits for a bucket is passed over for a piece beyond it. Where nobody has a way, the nearest walker leans out and asks, by night from its open door, and when its try is over if it is in the middle of one. A bucket sent while a walker is still getting in runs empty, and the walker stays on its landing.
- **A piece in the hand is still on its pegs for the walkers.** Nobody sets off when a piece is lifted off the tree and nobody steps onto it; the walkers look again when it is let go.
- **Someone in a bucket is in it.** The cup's ropes go up behind the rider and its tub is in front, and whatever sinks below the tub's bottom is not drawn. The owl is far too large for it: she sinks to her eyes, her wings stand out at both sides, the cup sags and the rope creaks, and she does not blink.
- **The shake folds everyone flat.** When the trunk is rocked, each walker folds down flat on its landing as a pop-up does when the book closes, and pops up again looking out of its own door.
- **The crown shakes as cards.** Each leaf card sways on its own slow pace and shakes about its own pin when a piece snaps on or the oak is tapped.
- **Each walker moves like itself, and each meeting is written.** The fifty-six meetings of the grid, the flourishes, the stretches and the rides are written one by one in `scripts.ts` as timed beats. The model steps those beats, the frame draws the pose and the piece from them, and each note of a beat is handed to the sound when the script's own time reaches it, so what is heard is what is drawn: a walker that stands still for a flourish is silent on its piece until it goes on. What a scene sounds, what a bucket sounds on its run and what each walker sounds on a piece go out through a bus each (`bus.ts`). A touch that ends a scene or a carry hushes that bus, and a flourish hushes its walker's, so nothing rings on; when the walker goes on with its piece, a long note that was cut begins again from the pitch it had reached, for the time that is left. `scripts.test.ts` walks every cell and holds that no two look or sound alike and that no note sounds while its maker is not drawn doing it. Gaits and idles are in `motion.ts`.

### Guidance in this look

The idle glow is warm light on the felt of the basket, as if the page were held to a lamp. The ghost hand is a small cream paper hand that lifts a piece from the basket and carries it to a free pair of pegs, a different piece and place each time; when the tree is full it taps a walker and the glow moves to the walkers. When a walker waits in or on a bucket, or for one, the glow lies on that bucket and the hand taps it. Now and then the hand shows the pull tab: by day one time in four it draws the sun down, by night every other time it lifts the moon. Neither the glow nor the hand is a mark printed on the scene: no arrow, ring or sign.

### How it is drawn, and what it costs

A canvas game. Every card is painted once into a sprite of its own at the density the surface is shown at, and stamped from then on; the whole standing scene (pages, hills, oak, stump, toadstool, bushes, basket) is one sprite, and it is the only thing stamped over more than a fifth of the surface. While the evening sheet slides, the page is stamped as two bands of rows, day above the sheet's edge and night below. Quality tiers change the pixel ratio only, and the cards are painted again at the new density, so the print stays sharp at every tier and nothing is removed from the scene. There are no shadow maps and no post pass. The draw count of this game is what `frameBudget.test.ts` counts: one for each card stamped and one for each fill stroked live (the pupils of a walker, the glow, and four for all the pegs together). A first visit draws 40. The most the test can reach in any state it builds is 73, under the bar's eighty: every place full of one kind of piece, by day and by night, pieces thrown as fast as a hand can throw them, every walker tapped and the glow on. While the evening sheet slides it reaches 60. Falling acorns and fluttering pieces are capped so that it stays there.
