# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

Nature Walk has no learning goal. It is a wood a child gets to know: six places to look in, four seasons turned by hand, three animals with tempers, and a table at home for what is carried back.

## The band and its age rule

The manifest band is 3 to 7, so the youngest age, 3, governs the design.

- **The cue-table row for 3 to 4** (`docs/solutions/conventions/wordless-clarity-for-the-declared-age-band.md`). What works: handling things directly, cause and effect from the world's answer, a breathing glow on what can be touched, a ghost hand or a character showing one move, characters who look and reach. Its "Avoid" column is a hard constraint: no text, numeral or picture sign that must be decoded, no spoken instruction, no verdict, not several activities live at once, and no tool on stage before it means anything. So the wood is one frame with one thing to do in it (look under a thing, then carry what is there), the season wheel is a wheel that turns the wood and never a row of signs to read, and the room with the table is a second frame the child walks into, never a panel beside the wood.
- **The pack's rule for the age** (pack: game-design, ages-2-to-4.md; pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md). Everything essential works with a tap: a tap looks under a cover, turns the wheel, pokes an animal and goes through the door. Carrying is a drag that survives a lifted finger and counts when partly done. No double tap, pinch, tilt or shake. One finger works at a time: a contact that landed on bare wood, or that has rested a second without moving, hands the work to the next contact that lands, and a finger that is carrying a thing is never interrupted while it moves. One that has held a thing still for a second hands the work on too, and the thing goes back where it came from, as it does when the browser takes the contact away or the surface loses its hold of it. Every touch is answered when the finger lands, so a tap may last as long as it likes. Each thing that answers is about 100 logical pixels across or more at the smallest surface the stills are taken at, well apart from the next, and none sits in the bottom strip, under the jam's round home control at the top centre or in the top right corner of 72 by 72 pixels, which answers nothing and does not take the idle ladder back. The one action is uncovering, which a small child repeats unprompted. From four the child supplies a plot: mischief with the animals, done on purpose, and a table set as they like.
- **The symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, optional or not. The game has no `symbols.ts`.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only the season a first visit opens in. A child of 4 or younger opens in spring, where the worm lies under the stone, the cover nearest the hedgehog. A child of 5 or older opens in autumn, the season with the most to carry. No age (`null`) opens in spring. It is a default and never a gate: the wheel reaches every season from the first touch, and a saved season always wins over the age.

## The toy

The one action the finger performs most is **touching a cover to look under it**.

The finger lands on a closed cover and the cover moves at once, under the finger, each in its own way and with its own sound:

- the long grass parts to both sides with a swish;
- the bark lid of the log tips up on its hinge with a two-step creak;
- the stone rolls over and away with a grinding rumble, and lands with a thud that squashes it;
- the big leaf afloat on the puddle (in winter a sheet of ice) glides aside with a bloop and a ring of ripples;
- the leaves of the low branch lift like a flap with a rustle;
- the bark flap over the burrow swings round on its pin with a rattle.

Something is always under it. As the cover clears, what is there comes up to be seen with a small motion and a note of its own, and all three animals turn to look. The cover stays open. A touch on what was found makes it do its own small act (the worm ties itself in a knot, the snail pulls in its horns, the button flips). A touch on the open cover shuts it again, each with its own closing move and sound (the stone rolls home and thuds, the lid claps). A cover is touched where it is drawn, shut or open, and a second touch on a cover within a third of a second of its setting off moves it no further: it is a knock on it. A touch on an animal is answered by its temper. A touch on the wheel turns the year by a quarter with three clacks, every piece of the wood that is stained with the season flips over to its next colour from left to right, and the open covers shut. A touch anywhere else is a knock on wood: a small tok, a ring that opens out from under the finger, a chip of wood that hops up there, a puff of sawdust, the hanging piece nearest the finger swings on its thread when one hangs within 260 stage units of it, and the animals glance at the spot. On the open water of the puddle, beside the leaf and whatever lies in it, a touch is rings on the water and a plip, not a knock. In the room the hanging piece is the lamp on its cord.

It is a pleasure with no goal because it is peekaboo with weight: the same touch gives six different moves and sounds, what is underneath is alive and answers back, shutting is as good as opening, and the wheel changes what is under every cover, so the child can go round again. Random tapping always produces something, and nothing a finger can do is refused (pack: game-design, toy-first.md; pack: game-design, touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md).

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

### What is under each cover

Six places by four seasons. It is the same every time, so a find can be found again and shown to someone (pack: game-design, hidden-never-counted.md). Six of the finds are **things that can be carried** (the worm, the snail, the feather, the acorn, the button, the apple); there is one of each in the world. The others are **dwellers**, who are met and stay where they live. Each dweller's own act, when touched, is given with it.

| Place | Spring | Summer | Autumn | Winter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long grass | a ladybird: opens its wing cases, whirrs up in a loop and lands (a tick and a whirr) | the snail | the worm | the button |
| Log | the snail | the worm | a mushroom: bows and puffs a cloud of spores (a soft pff) | a beetle asleep on its back: its legs paddle, it turns over and falls asleep again (a tiny snore, then a scrabble) |
| Stone | the worm | the button | the snail | the acorn |
| Puddle | a tadpole: darts a figure of eight (two plips) | a pond skater: skates a zigzag on dimples (three dry ticks) | a red leaf afloat: spins like a boat (a rustle and a drip) | a bubble under the ice: squeaks away from the finger and slides back (a rubbed-glass squeak) |
| Low branch | a caterpillar: humps along the twig and back (two stretching notes) | the feather | the apple | the feather |
| Burrow | a beetle: scuttles out, round and back in (a run of clicks) | a moth: flutters out, round once and back in (a soft flutter) | the acorn | the worm |

A thing that has been carried off is not under its cover. A woodlouse has moved in there instead: touched, it rolls into a ball, rolls round the hollow and unrolls (a small rattle). So something is always under every cover. A thing set down on the open cover it belongs under in this season goes back in, dry from then on, and the woodlouse trundles out of sight. A thing that is under its cover when the wheel turns is found in its place of the new season. A thing with no place in a season (the apple outside autumn, the snail in winter) is not in the wood then, and is back in its place when its season comes round. A thing that is out in the world stays where the child left it through every season.

**Wet things are shiny.** A thing taken out of the puddle or the bowl is wet: it carries a gloss streak and drips. The feather set against a wet thing on the table wipes it dry.

**What is new on day 15.** The child knows the wood: where the worm is in each season and so how to bring the hedgehog out of his ball; that the apple is on the branch only in autumn and the snail under no cover in winter, so either is on the table in another season only because the child carried it home in its own; that anything dunked in the puddle shines and the jay comes for it, the worm included, and that the apple is the one thing he cannot lift; that a tap on the nest brings everything back; and that the toad can be sent anywhere a thing can be fetched from, the table at home included. None of it is new content: it is the same six places, four seasons and three tempers, known well enough to play tricks with on purpose.

## The error as a consequence

Nothing in the wood is wrong, so nothing is marked wrong. A use the child did not mean still works, and shows where and why in the world (pack: game-design, errors-show-as-consequences.md):

- A shiny or wet thing left on the open path is gone, and the glint is in the jay's nest, where it can be seen; the wet apple alone is too heavy for him and lies a hand's width from where it was left. One tap on the nest tips everything in it out at the foot of his tree.
- A thing set on the toad's stone stays there, and the toad now sits where the thing came from, large and plain to see; when that is the table at home, he is seen from the wood through the door of the house, all of him, sitting on the table. One tap on the toad sends him back to his stone.
- A snail, a feather, an acorn, a button or an apple brought to the rolled-up hedgehog does not bring him out: he stays a ball and the thing does its own act on the ball. Only the worm unrolls him.
- A thing dropped on the table where there is no room rolls off the edge, across the floor and out through the open door, and lies on the path by the step.
- A thing let go anywhere else lies where it was let go if that is open ground with nothing on it: the path at the front, or the foot of the jay's tree. Let go on anything else that stands in the wood (a cover, the jay, his nest, the wheel, another thing, a stone that already holds a thing) or off the ground, it comes down on the nearest free open ground; in the room, let go off the table, it is set on the nearest point of the table. On the path it then does what its row of the grid says; at the foot of the jay's tree a dull thing lies still.

The state stays in every case: nothing is taken back, nothing resets, and the child changes one thing and tries again. No sound or sign says right or wrong.

## The designed order, and what is stored

**The order.** The game has one place in its designed order, `wood`, and it never moves: there are no levels and no harder rounds, and no cycle is judged as having gone well or badly. What a first visit starts with by age: at 4 or younger, and with no age, the wood in spring; at 5 or older, the wood in autumn. Both ends are open, and a saved season wins over the age. What play changes: nothing in the order. The child turns the season, opens and shuts covers and moves the six things about, and all of that is the world as the child left it, not a position. A harder thing to do (bringing the hedgehog out in winter, when the worm is deep in the burrow at the far side of the wood; getting the toad onto the table) is there from the first visit, can be seen in the world, and is chosen by the child.

**Every field of the saved state.** Plain JSON, versioned, read defensively field by field:

- `v`: the version of the record.
- `position`: always `wood`. An id this build does not know falls back to `wood`.
- `finished`: true while the child is in the room at the table, which is where a walk ends; false in the wood.
- `season`: `spring`, `summer`, `autumn` or `winter`.
- `open`: the places whose covers stand open, as a list of place ids (`grass`, `log`, `stone`, `puddle`, `branch`, `burrow`).
- `rolled`: whether the hedgehog is rolled up.
- `things`: for each of the six things, where it is, as one of: `home` (under its cover of the season, or out of the wood in a season where it has no place); `path` with a point on the ground of the wood; `stone` (on the toad's stone); `puddle`; `nest`; `hedgehog` (the apple on his spines); `table` with a point on the table; `bowl`.
- `wet`: the things that are wet, as a list of thing ids.
- `bitten`: whether the snail has eaten its hole in the apple.
- `toad`: where the toad sits, as one of: `stone`; `seat` with the id of the place he sits beside; `path` with a point; `foot` (the foot of the jay's tree); `table` with a point.

A pair's pose on the table is a view of two saved points and needs no field. The silver trail of the snail, the ripples, the sawdust of a knock and a thing in the air are short-lived and gone on load. So are the snail sitting on the rolled-up hedgehog and the feather stuck on his spine: each is saved `path` at the point before him and lies there on load. The worm that swam the puddle and climbed out is saved `path` at the far bank and is in `wet`; the jay comes only for a thing the child's hand lets go, so it lies there. Which things were sent in through the door on this walk is not saved: after a load none rocks in the scene "Coming home". A thing in the hand is saved where it came from: a put-away under a finger that is still carrying is no drop, and the thing goes back. A thing let go hangs where it was let go for 0.3 seconds of game time before it is set down there (let go while the view still slides out of the door, it hangs until the view is at rest, so it does not jump and the jay takes nothing while he is partly off the screen), and a finger landing on it in that time carries on; put away in that time, it is set down where it was let go. The largest legal state is a few hundred bytes.

**Nothing the game sets down covers what the child aims at.** Each place has a seat beside it, clear of every cover and every animal, and that is where the toad lands when he leaps to a place. Things tipped from the nest land at fixed spots at the foot of the jay's tree, skipping any spot the toad or a thing is on, and on the nearest free ground once every spot is taken; things sent in through the door land on free spots along the near edge of the table, and with no spot free a thing let go on the door lies on the path by the step; a thing sent in is seen sailing in through the door, and from then on it shows, small, on the table that is seen through the door from the wood, as does whatever else lies on the table or in the bowl; and a thing that rolls off the table lies on the path by the step, or on the nearest free ground to it. None of these spots lies under a cover, an animal, the wheel, the door or the jam's home control. Where a cover that opens or shuts would come to rest on a thing, or the toad would land on one, the thing is nudged clear to the nearest free ground (on the table, to the nearest free point of it, and with none, out to the step), and a thing in the puddle stays in view beside the leaf or the ice, the snail on top of it. An overlap test holds all of this at each surface size the stills are taken at.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Each has one want that is always visible, and likes and dislikes that never change, so they can be learnt and tested on purpose (pack: game-design, characters-with-opinions.md). None of their feelings is ever about the child.

- **The hedgehog wants the worm.** His nose always points at where the worm is, through its cover when it is covered, and one eye looks that way out of the ball when he is rolled up. He rolls up when poked, with a quick downward brrrip, and when poked again he huffs, swells once and turns his back on the finger: he is put out, never afraid. He unrolls only for the worm: when it comes into the open anywhere in the wood (also when its cover is opened over it or it is tipped out of the nest; while the child is in the room he is not seen or heard, and is found unrolled), when it wriggles under a touch, or when it is set down before him. He likes the apple (he wears it). The feather makes him sneeze, his own face in the button makes him start, the acorn is too hard for his teeth, and the snail is a ball he cannot work out. Tempo: quick and snuffling, small fast moves; the funniest part of him is his nose.
- **The jay wants what shines.** His eye is on the button wherever it is, and on anything wet. He steals whatever shiny thing is put down on open ground, the path or the foot of his own tree, and keeps it in his nest, where it shows. He takes nothing from a hollow, from the toad's stone, from the hedgehog, from the water or from the house. Dull things bore him and he shows it. Poked, he ruffles up by a third of his size, crest up and wings out, and scolds with a rattle; that is as far as the open flap of the burrow beside him and the top of the frame leave room for. He dislikes water and shakes it off. Tempo: sharp and stepped, a move and then a hold; the funniest part of him is his crest.
- **The toad wants company on his stone.** He sits to one side of it, the other half bare beside him, and looks from whatever the child is carrying to the bare half. He swaps places with whatever is set there: the thing has the stone, and he sits where the thing came from. He likes damp things (the worm, the snail). The feather makes him sneeze, his face in the button frightens him, he tests the acorn with a knuckle, and the apple is too heavy for his end of the stone. Poked, he blows his throat up to a great pale ball and lets it out as one long rude croak, and hops on the spot. Tempo: slow and heavy, long holds and one big move; the funniest part of him is his throat.

Dwellers and things have their own small acts and no tastes.

## The scenes

Five short scenes. Each is a list of beats over game time, filled in from the state of play, and each gives way to any touch: the touch jumps the scene to its end, where everything already is where the save has it, and is then an ordinary touch (pack: game-design, endings-and-short-scenes.md).

1. **The jay's theft.** Cause: a shiny or wet thing is let go on open ground, the path or the foot of the jay's tree. A thing that already lay on open ground and is let go again within a quarter of a second of the touch, or less than a finger's width (48 pixels at 1180 wide) from where it lay, has not been let go there: it stays where it lay, he does not come, and nothing else comes of it. Only a carry that sets it down somewhere new is the cause. Beats, about 5 seconds: his head snaps round, he drops from the nest, snatches the thing in the way that thing is snatched, beats back up, drops it into the nest and preens with his back to the wood. Filled in from: which thing, whether it is wet, and where it lay. Saved when it starts: `things` (that thing is `nest`), and `wet` (the thing is dry from then on). The wet apple is the one exception: `things` has it at `path`, at the point a hand's width toward the jay's tree where he drops it, or, where the open ground ends or is taken that way, a hand's width along it as near that way as there is free ground, and `wet` keeps it.
2. **The toad's swap.** Cause: a thing is let go on the toad's stone while he sits there. Beats, about 4 seconds: he looks at it in the way he looks at that thing, gathers, leaps in one arc to where the thing came from (the seat of a place, the puddle's seat for a thing out of the water, a point on the path, the point of the path before the hedgehog for the apple off his spines, the foot of the tree, a point on the table, and for a thing out of the bowl the nearest free point of the table), lands with a squash, and croaks once from there, while the thing does its own act on the stone. Filled in from: which thing and where it came from. Saved when it starts: `things` (that thing is `stone`) and `toad`. If he is not on the stone, a thing set there just lies there, and no scene plays. A thing lifted from the stone and set back on it moves nobody and plays no scene.
3. **The hedgehog and the worm.** Cause: the worm is let go before the hedgehog. Beats, about 5 seconds: he unrolls if he is rolled up, snuffles at the worm, the worm dives into the ground, and he stares cross-eyed at the hole and sits back. Filled in from: whether he was rolled up. Saved when it starts: `rolled` (false), `things` (the worm is `home`) and `wet` (the worm is dry from then on).
4. **The turn of the year.** Cause: a touch on the wheel. Beats, about 4 seconds: the wheel turns a quarter over three clacks, the open covers shut, and every piece that is stained with the season flips over to its next colour in a wave from left to right: the leaves, the bushes and the long grass, the patches at the front, the two sheets of hills, the meadow and the strip of blades at the front. The sheet that holds the path, the log, the bank, the house and the great trunk is stained once and does not flip. Filled in from: the season it turns from. Saved when it starts: `season` and `open` (empty). `things` is not changed: a thing that is `home` is drawn in its place of the new season.
5. **Coming home.** Cause: a touch on the open door of the house, from the wood. Beats, about 4 seconds: the view slides in through the door to the room, where the table stands under the window with whatever is on it, and the things that were sent in through the door on this walk rock once where they landed on its near edge. With nothing sent in on this walk there is no scene: the touch on the door is only the way in, the view slides in, and nothing rocks. Filled in from: the season, which shows in the window, and what is on the table. Saved when it starts: `finished` (true). The room then stays as long as the child likes, with the things to be set where the child wants them.

An unrolling for a worm that has only been uncovered or touched, a tap that sends the toad home, a tap that empties the nest and the act of a pair on the table are answers to a touch and run alongside the next touch; they are not scenes. Each is saved as it is caused. Going through the door, in or out, brings whatever was still moving in the place that is left to its end at once and silently, as a put-away would, so that nothing is heard that is not seen, and that place is found as it was left. The thing in the hand goes with the hand.

**How a walk ends and the next one starts.** A walk ends at the table, when the child chooses to go in. Nothing ends it for the child, and nothing counts what was brought. In the room the door stands open on the path, with the wood in its season seen through it. A thing dragged from the table or the bowl onto the door goes out in the hand: the view slides to the wood under the carry, the thing stays saved where it was on the table until it is let go, and it is then let go in the wood as any carried thing is, having come from the table. If the child does nothing, nothing starts. A touch on the door slides the view back out to the wood, exactly as it was left, and `finished` is false again; that touch is the whole restart, and it is not a scene. A touch that lands while the view is still sliding, in or out, brings the view to rest at once, so the touch lands on what is seen. On load no scene replays: the game opens in the wood or in the room, wherever it was put away, with every cover, thing and animal where the save has it.

**The idle ladder.** In the wood: a breathing glow behind the covers that are shut, then a ghost hand that taps once on the cover the hedgehog's nose points at, or, when that cover stands open, on the first cover that is shut. With the worm up in the jay's nest that tap is on the nest, which glows, and with the worm in the house it is on the door of the house, which glows: the hand and the nose show the same thing. Once a thing that can be carried lies in the open, the hand instead shows a carry: it picks that thing up and moves it a short way along the path, to no one. With every cover open and nothing to carry, the glow and the hand are on the wheel. Once a thing has been carried on this visit and the room has not yet been seen on it, the second showing of the hand in an idle stretch is the door of the house instead: the door glows and the hand taps it once. In the room: the glow is on the things at the table's edge and on the door, and the hand moves one thing a little way along the table, or with nothing on the table taps the door. The hand shows a move and never where a thing should go. It comes at most four times in one idle stretch, each later than the last, and the glow fades after the fourth; any touch takes the ladder back to the bottom, and an animal's own act never does.

## The records

The game has no learning goal: it is designed from no record of either jurisdiction, has no records part and claims no school skill.

## The look

Written after the style spike, not part of the sheet. The look is the first one reserved for the game in `docs/art-direction.md`: **laser-cut plywood layers**. It held on clarity in the spike and the lead passed it at the toy checkpoint, so the second reserved look was not tried.

### What it is

The wood is a stack of thin plywood sheets, cut by a laser and set one in front of the other with a gap between: a back board with the sky, two sheets of far hills, the meadow, the sheet with the path and all that stands on it, the great trunk and a strip of grass blades at the very front. Everything that stands on them is a cut piece too, and so is every animal. Nothing is drawn as if it were round: a thing has a face, a thickness and a shadow on the sheet behind it.

- **Every piece is a sheet with a thickness.** One closed outline filled with pale wood, with its grain across it and a brown burn just inside the cut. Along the side away from the light the sheet's own thickness shows: three plies, dark, pale and mid brown, between 2.6 and 6 units deep, deeper the further the piece stands off the sheet behind it. Past that lies the shadow it throws there. The cut itself is a thin burnt line, not a drawn outline: what sets a piece off from what is behind it is its thickness and its shadow (`cut` in `ply.ts`).
- **The light falls one way.** It comes from the upper left, so every piece shows its plies and throws its shadow down and to the right, however it is turned or mirrored when it is stamped. A piece is painted for each of eight ways the light can fall on it, the first time it is seen that way, and the view stamps the one that fits how the piece stands (`setLight` and `lightFor` in `ply.ts`, `put` in `view.ts`). The lid of the log and the right fan of the long grass, which are stamped mirrored, throw their shadows the same way as the rest.
- **Grain at every tier.** Fine grain is thin lines a little darker than the wood, nearly straight, no two the same distance apart; it shows through a stain in brown. Where a tier leaves the fine lines out, a few broad pale bands the same way stand in for them, so the wood is never a flat fill.
- **Detail is engraved.** A brown hairline, never a black outline inside a piece: bark, veins, feathers, the planks of the house, the path.
- **A few pieces are stained one colour.** Leaves, grass, the puddle, the stone, the animals, the things. A stain is one flat wash over the wood with the grain still showing through. Nothing is shaded and nothing has a gradient but the sky board's thin blue wash and the glow of the idle ladder.
- **Brass pins.** Where a piece turns, a brass rivet shows: the lid of the log, the flap of the burrow, the leaves of the low branch, the wheel, the jay's wing.
- **Threads.** The sun, two clouds and a bird hang on threads from the top of the frame, sway, and swing when the wood near them is knocked. In the room the lamp hangs on its cord and swings the same way.
- **Few lines are live.** Pupils slide in their sockets and mouths bend; the snail's two horns, the toad's knuckle, the hole the worm leaves, ripples, a wake, gloss and sawdust are drawn as they are at that moment. Everything else is a piece that moves whole.

### The sheets, from the back

- **The back board**: pale ply with a thin blue wash for the sky and nothing engraved on it, and the four hung pieces before it.
- **The two sheets of hills** and **the meadow** in front of them, each stained with the season.
- **What stands on the meadow**, on the next sheet: the picket fence and the bush, two far trees whose crowns are stained, the stump, the jay's tree with the fork his nest sits in, the bank with the burrow, and the house with its door open on the room. What lies on the table at home shows through that door, small, and the toad when he sits there: all of him, as wide as the doorway lets him be.
- **The path**, on the same sheet: its pebbles and its dip, the log, the dent of the stone, the puddle let into the ground, and the bed of the toad's stone.
- **The great trunk** at the left, with the low branch and the bracket the wheel hangs from, and **the front strip** of dark blades along the bottom, no two next to each other the same width, height or lean, with one tuft of taller blades at the front of the path and a toadstool behind it.

The long grass is two fans of cut blades in three layers, a few of them bent, with the head and feelers of a beetle peeping from between the blades of each fan. Open, the fans stand apart with the find between them.

### The room

The same wood, indoors: a wall of upright planks, a floor of boards, the long table under an arched window with one upright bar set off its middle, the bowl of water at the table's left end, a shelf with two jars and a tin, a rail with a coat and a hat, a framed leaf, a garland of twelve cut leaves (no two of one size, tilt and stain next to each other) and the lamp. The window and the doorway each show the wood in its season as one stained piece with four sides. The room is one painted sheet; the window, the doorway and the lamp are stamped over it.

### Palette

Wood, from pale to dark (`WOOD` in `ply.ts`): board `#f3e8cb` for the sky and the cut ice, birch `#ecd9b1` for most pieces, maple `#e1c28d`, alder `#cf9d68` and walnut `#8d603e` for the trunk, the log and the hedgehog's back. The burnt line of a cut `#6a4020`, the plies of a sheet's side `#7d532c`, `#ecd6a6` and `#a8763f`, scorch `#39220f` for the rings of eyes and inlays, engraving `#6e411d`, brass `#cda63f`.

Stains by season (`STAIN` in `ply.ts`): spring is a light green with pink blossom, summer a deep green with yellow, autumn orange with red leaves and straw-coloured grass, winter white with ice blue. The stain is the only thing that tells the season, with the picture the wheel shows at its top: a blossom, a sun, a leaf, a bare twig. A bare branch, on the wheel and wherever winter shows one (the jay's crown, the bush, the two front patches, the tree in the window and the one in the doorway), forks as a branch does: a stem that kinks at each fork, one side branch low to one side and a shorter or longer one higher to the other, each with a knee, never two on one side of the stem. There are seven of them and no two are the same (`BRANCHES` and `BRANCH_OF` in `ply.ts`). The quarters of the wheel are cut along edges bent like the blades of a pinwheel, deep enough to be seen when the wheel is at rest, with a hub over the middle.

Fixed stains: the stone grey-blue, the puddle blue, the toad green with a cream belly, the hedgehog walnut with a birch face, the jay rose-buff with one blue-barred wing patch and a blue tip to his tail, the house straw and plank. The six things are the brightest pieces in the frame: the pink worm, the yellow shell of the snail, the blue feather, the acorn, the pearl button and the red apple. A wet thing carries one pale gloss streak and drips.

### How the season turns

Each piece that is stained with the season has four sides. When the wheel turns, each flips over about its own axis (crowns and bushes about their upright, patches and the far trees about their level), one after another from the left of the frame to the right, and shows the next season's side when it is edge on. The covers that are stained flip with the wave as they shut. The two sheets of hills and the meadow flip about their foot, which the sheet in front hides, and the front strip flips about its foot in two halves, left and then right; a sheet that stands edge on shows the sheet or the back board behind it. The sheet with the path, the log, the bank, the house and the great trunk is stained once and does not flip, so the path is the same earth all year.

### Motion rules

- A piece moves whole: it slides, turns on its pin, or flips. It never bends.
- Weight shows in time and in the landing: the stone is the slowest thing and squashes when it lands, the grass and the leaves spring past where they stop, the lid of the log goes up in two steps and falls in one.
- Each animal is a few pieces pinned together, so it moves like a jumping jack: the hedgehog's face slides under his back when he rolls up, the jay's crest, wing and tail turn on their pins, the toad's throat is a disc that swells.
- No animal shares a tempo or an idle motion with another (`IDLE` in `toy.ts`, held by `motion.test.ts`), and no two cells of the sheet's tables share a motion or a sound (`cells.test.ts`).
- Every motion is an act on the one clock of the model, and every sound is a cue of an act, fired by that clock at the moment the motion it belongs to is seen. Seen means drawn: the motion is in the frame that is on screen, and the picture with it differs from the picture without it (`clock.test.ts`). Going through the door ends what was moving in the place that is left, silently.
- A thing in the hand is drawn last and outside both frames, so it stays under the finger while the view slides between the wood and the room.
- Nothing lies on or passes through anything: each piece has a footprint of discs (`footprints.ts`), and `overlap.test.ts` holds every season, every cover open and shut, every hung piece in mid swing and the whole scripted walk at rest, at 1180 by 820 and at 1024 by 768.

### The frame

The frame is the wood from edge to edge: the great trunk fills the left edge and the house the right, the grass strip the bottom and the hung pieces the top. The scenery is painted past the stage's edges, so a surface of another shape shows more wood and never a margin. Nothing that answers a touch stands under the jam's home control at the top centre, and the top right corner of 72 by 72 pixels answers nothing. A thing that is carried answers within 58 stage units of its middle, which is 100 pixels across at 1024 by 768.

The marks a small child could take for a letter, a numeral or a sign are listed, each with why it is weaker than the thing it is, in the "Reader" part of `REFINEMENT.md`.

### Cost

The sheet with the path and all that stands on the meadow is painted once for a surface and stamped whole, and so is the room. The back board is stamped as far down as it can show, and the hills, the meadow and the two halves of the front strip are bands as wide as the surface: six wide stamps in the wood beside the one whole sheet. Every other piece is painted once into a sheet of its own, for each way of the light it is seen in, and stamped. The busiest frame of a scripted walk through the whole game is 76 draws, counted by `frameBudget.test.ts` as stamps plus live paths, which is also what the grown-up overlay shows; the test holds that number and the limit of 80. It is the frame in which the view slides into the room with things on the table and the toad on it, so both frames are drawn. No pass over the whole frame, no blur, no shadow at draw time (shadows and thickness are painted into the pieces).

Painting is the heavy work. While the year turns, the coming season's sides are painted ahead of their flip, two to a frame; in the scripted walk the most painted in one frame is 7, when a touch ends the turn a quarter of a second in, and the worst there is, a second touch in the frame after the wheel was touched, is 10 (`frameBudget.test.ts` counts both). The frame in which the saved state is read paints the whole wood at once; before it only the back board shows, bare below the sky, so nothing is seen half-drawn.

Memory is held to a size (`sprites.ts`). The store keeps only the season that shows and, while the year turns, the one it turns from. At the end of every frame, while it holds more than 5.9 surfaces' worth of pixels, it lets go what that frame did not stamp: first a piece's paintings for ways of the light it is not seen in now, then whatever has gone longest unstamped, which is how the place that is off screen goes. At 1180 by 820 and pixel ratio 2 that limit is 22.8 megapixels, and `frameBudget.test.ts` walks all four seasons, both places and every cover and holds every sheet kept at the end of every frame under 24. A piece that was let go is painted again when it is next seen, which is where the 7 above comes from. Not measured: what this takes on a tablet, in bytes or in time.

Tiers (`TIERS` in `config.ts`): pixel ratio 2 with fine grain and seven motes of sawdust on a knock, then 1.5 with five, then 1.25 with broad bands in place of the fine grain and three motes, then 1 with the bands and no sawdust. The ring of a knock and the chip of wood that hops up under the finger are drawn at every tier, so no tier leaves out the answer to a touch. When the tier or the size of the surface changes, the pieces are painted again at most ten to a frame, a wide sheet counting for five; until its turn comes a piece is stamped as it was, a little less sharp, so no frame draws less than the whole wood.
