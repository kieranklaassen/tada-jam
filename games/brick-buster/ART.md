# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

## The band and its age rule

The manifest band is 5 to 9, and its youngest age, five, governs the design.

- **The cue-table row.** The row for a youngest age of 5 to 6 in the wordless-clarity convention. Its "Avoid" column binds the game: no word or letter, no numeral or symbol since the band starts at 5, no voice instruction and no hint that gives an answer. More than one thing may answer a touch at once, and the game still has one clear function: steer the ball.
- **The pack's rule for the range** (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md). The game supplies a place, props and characters who react, and the child supplies the plan. Tap and drag are the only gestures; nothing needs reading or a double tap. The funny part is slapstick where someone overreacts and is never hurt. The pack has no file for ages 7 and 8; for a nine-year-old the game offers what the file for ages 9 to 12 asks of any game: a system that behaves truly, more than one way through it, and failure that is funny and free.
- **The symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, and the game has no `symbols.ts`. The rule follows the manifest band: a nine-year-old sees none either.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only which wall a first visit starts with ("The designed order, and what is stored"). A saved position wins over the age. Age locks and hides nothing: every wall is reached by play, and the three gates are open at every age from the first frame.
- **What no age gives.** `null` starts a first visit at the first wall, as for the youngest child.

## The toy

**The action.** Slide the bumper under the ball. A finger anywhere on the table that is not on a toy, a brick, the bell or the ladybird is a finger on the bumper: the bumper slides to it and stays under it. A brick answers a finger over at least 48 px each way at any size the table is shown at, so a finger that lands just beside a brick is on that brick, or on the nearer of two, and never flicks the ball.

**In an empty scene.** A steel ball rests on a rubber bumper that stands on a drum-skin floor, under a small wall of glass bricks, and nothing asks for anything.

- When the finger lands, in that frame, the bumper squashes under it with a rubbery creak and starts to slide to the finger. The ball sinks with it.
- When the finger lifts, the bumper springs up with a boing and flicks the resting ball into the wall. A bumper that is sliding as it lets go throws the ball a little that way.
- A brick the ball hits rings its own note and cracks, and the ball comes back down. No ball stays on the wall, and none glides uphill by itself. A ball that comes to rest on a brick rolls off downhill only, as a ball would: along the top it lies on to the nearer open side and down the drop, slowly at first and faster as it falls, with a low rolling rumble of its own, and the bricks it passes flinch. It drops from under the foot of the wall to where the bumper can reach it. Where no way leads down (a pit, a shelf) the nearer of the dragon and the elephant fetches it in its own manner, seen and heard: the dragon whips its tail out over the table and curls it round the ball, quickly; the elephant, late and slowly, stretches its trunk out over the wall and hooks it in under the ball. The toy lifts the ball clear of the board, carries it over the wall and sets it on the middle of the bumper, where it rests until a finger lands and lifts. It strikes nothing on the way, and a ball at rest never cracks, rings or breaks a thing by itself. A ball that comes down on the top of a brick rings it once; landing on the same brick again it rings nothing and settles.
- A ball that lands on the bumper while the finger is on it squashes it and is sprung off again, and where it lands on the bumper is where it goes: on the middle straight up, towards an end out to that side. That is the aiming, and it is the same every time. A ball that lands on the bumper while no finger is on it sinks into the rubber with a soft "pfff", rocks once and comes to rest there, and it stays at rest until a finger lands and lifts.
- A ball that misses the bumper lands on the drum-skin floor, which booms softly and bounces it back up, as high as the top of the bumper and well over. Each boom takes three quarters of the ball's sideways travel, so within two or three booms it is bouncing straight up in place. It goes on bouncing in place for as long as the child likes, and each boom is softer than the last, whatever kind of ball it is. What moves with a boom fades with it: after about six booms no brick hops and no toy jumps, flaps or wallows for it, and after about eighteen the skin is silent, so that nothing goes on without end but the ball itself, coming up to where the bumper can be slid under it. A ball that has been on the bumper, in a gate or in a toy's hold booms in full again. Sliding the bumper under it while it is in the air catches it.
- A bumper slid into a ball that is low beside it knocks the ball along with a bonk.

**Why repeating it is a pleasure with no goal.** Every touch is answered at touch-down, and the answer is bigger than the touch: a squash, a spring, a flight, a ring and a crack from one finger (pack: game-design, touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md). The ball cannot be lost and waiting costs nothing, so the bumper is only ever for aiming. Random sliding keeps a tune going, since under a plain ball every brick sounds the note of its place in one five-note scale, each kind in its own voice (glass rings it, jelly blubs it low, ice ticks it high, a seed knocks it, a sleepy brick hums it under its yawn), and no two sound sour together. The simplest use, a tap, always flicks the ball. A person watching sees within three seconds that the child is sliding the bumper under the ball (pack: game-design, toy-first.md).

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

Five kinds of brick and the drum-skin floor, by the four kinds of ball and the child's finger. The ball is plain until the child steers it through a gate: the walrus's tub makes it wet, the dragon's mouth makes it hot, the elephant's trunk makes it heavy. Every cell looks and sounds different, and each result is the same every time.

| Thing | Plain ball | Wet ball | Hot ball | Heavy ball | A finger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Glass brick | Rings its own note like a tapped tumbler and takes a crack. A cracked one, hit again, rings once more and falls in shards with a tinkle | Squeaks like a wiped window and comes up so clean that the face behind it is seen pressed flat against it; no crack is added | Glows orange and sags into a long drip with a low glass-blower's "bwoom". Where another brick stands in the way round, the ball is let go there and drops | Melts into syrup that runs down the board to the rubble with a "glorp"; the ball comes away trailing a sticky thread that twangs | The ball sinks deep in and is flung straight up into the roof with a long "boi-oi-oing" and a thud up there, and comes off the roof at a slant towards the middle of the table; the jelly goes on shaking | Wobbles under the finger for as long as it is pressed, with a rubbery "bwub-bwub" and a bubbling titter |

| Seed brick | Knocks like a wooden block, "tock"; a short vine uncurls with a creak in the empty column beside the seed, lifts the ball one brick up and lets it go, where the ball can get to the vine without passing through a brick; a ball on the seed's far side is knocked off and not lifted | The vine shoots to the roof with a rising slide-whistle; the ball spirals up it to the top and rolls onto the wall from above, and the leaves stay | Pops like popcorn with a bang, and a puffed white flower jumps off and floats down to the rubble | Driven into the board like a peg with a "thunk"; a moment later a fat sprout comes up through the rubble below with a "pip" | Rattles like a maraca, and a leaf peeps out and ducks back |

| Drum-skin floor | A soft "doom"; the skin dips and ripples and the ball bounces straight up in place | A wet slap; a ring of drops jumps off the skin and leaves a damp patch that dries | A sizzle and a puff of steam; the skin tightens, so that bounce sounds a note higher, and a scorch ring fades | A deep boom that makes every standing brick hop in its place and the three large toys jump | Patted, it gives a small "dum" with a ripple, and the bumper slides over to the hand |

**What the bricks leave behind answers too.**

- **The pool** an ice brick melts into. A plain ball rolls through with a "plip" and leaves wet tracks. A wet ball splashes it across the bricks on either side: a splashed jelly swells to twice its size and throws the ball twice as far, a splashed glass squeaks clean, a splashed sleepy brick wakes. A hot ball boils it off with a "pshh" into a cloud that fogs every glass brick; the hot ball that melted it falls back through it and leaves it lying, and boils it off only once it has been down to the bumper, the drum or a gate. A heavy ball lands in it with a "sploosh" that soaks the three large toys. A boiled or splashed pool is gone.
- **A vine** stays where it grew, and any ball that comes to it rides up it, except a ball it has already carried, which it lets by until that ball has come down to the bumper or the drum, and a ball that could only reach it through a brick. One flick is one ride: a vine never passes a ball round and round by itself, and a ball it has carried only knocks on the seed. A hot ball curls its leaves brown with a crackle, and the vine still carries.
- **The bell** rings for the ball in the ball's manner (clear, gurgling, a tone higher and glowing, or a low gong) and for a finger with a small clear "ting".

**The wrong use of each thing works and is funny.**

- A plain ball on ice does nothing to the ice and skates off in a shower of chips. A hot ball on a sleepy brick only makes it sleep harder.
- A ball steered through the gate that made it what it is comes out plain again: the walrus rubs it dry with a squeak, the dragon blows it cool with a wheeze, the elephant shakes it empty with a rattle.
- The bell rung while bricks still stand makes the folded wall rustle and stay folded.
- A heavy ball on the bumper squashes it flat with a groan before it is sprung, and goes about two thirds as high, which still reaches the top row of the wall.
- A miss is a boom on the drum and a ball that waits.

**On day 15** the child plays a wall like an instrument, since each column is a note and the bumper aims, and takes a wall apart in an order of their own: the ice melted first so that there is a pool, then a wet ball through the pool to swell the jelly, then a heavy ball off the swollen jelly into the roof. They know what each of the five bricks does for each of the four balls and for a finger, and which large toy cannot stand which result. None of that needs new content: it is five bricks by four balls, in the order the child chose (pack: game-design, depth-from-combinations.md; pack: game-design, liveliness-from-causing-and-comedy.md).

## The error as a consequence

Nothing in the game is an error, and no try is judged. What does not work shows why in the world, and the state stays.

- **A miss.** The ball lands on the drum-skin floor beside the bumper and bounces there in place. It shows where the bumper was not. The child slides the bumper under it, at any time.
- **The wrong ball for a brick.** The brick answers as the grid says and stays: ice sprays chips at a plain ball and shivers, with its breath showing, so it is seen to be cold; a sleepy brick only shuffles over. The large toy whose gate would have done it is in plain view with its mouth, tub or trunk open. The child changes one thing, the ball, and tries again.
- **The state stays.** No brick comes back, no crack mends, and the ball keeps its kind until the child changes it.
- **As interesting as success.** A heavy ball stopped dead by ice with an anvil's donk is worth causing on purpose.
- **Never about the child.** No buzzer, cross, sad face turned to the child, praise or cheer, and no sound that means wrong.

## The designed order, and what is stored

The game has no learning goal. Its order is the order in which the kinds of brick are met.

**The order.** Six places, one new kind of brick at each and then all of them together. The ids are as they stand in `LADDER` in `config.ts`; each names a place in the game's own order. The three gates are open at every place.

| Id | The wall holds | The one new thing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `glass` | glass only | the bumper, the ball and a brick that rings |
| `jelly` | glass and jelly | a brick that throws the ball |
| `ice` | glass, jelly and ice | a brick that needs another ball, and so a gate |
| `sleepy` | glass, ice and sleepy bricks | a brick that moves |
| `seed` | glass, jelly and seed bricks | a way up the wall |
| `all` | all five kinds, laid out afresh each time | the kinds combined |

A wall is three rows of six places. Not every place holds a brick. A wall holds at most one seed, which stands in the second column, and such a wall keeps the first column empty for its vine; a sleepy brick does not shuffle into that column.

**What a first visit starts with, by age.** A child of 5 or younger, or of no known age, starts at `glass`. A child of 6 or 7 starts at `jelly`. A child of 8 or older starts at `ice`. A saved position wins over the age.

**What play changes.** A cycle is one wall, from its unfolding to its last brick falling. Every wall that is cleared counts as a cycle that went well, since a wall cannot go badly: the position moves one place up when the last brick falls, and stays at `all` once it is there. Nothing moves it down and nothing moves it inside a wall. A wall left half taken apart moves nothing. Nothing on screen shows the position.

**No harder option is offered for the child to pick.** One folded wall waits, and how a wall is taken apart is the child's choice inside every wall.

**Which wall a new position lays out.** The very next one. The folded wall that waits at the top is a concertina of plain board that shows no brick. Its one want is the bell: while the table is cleared it holds its near corner lifted and leaning towards the bell, and while bricks still stand it lies flat. The lifted corner is a view of `finished` and needs no field. Its bricks are laid out when the bell is rung, from the position as it stands at that moment.

**What is stored.** Small plain JSON under one version number, read field by field.

- `v`: the version.
- `position`: an id from the table above: the place the next wall is laid out from.
- `finished`: the wall on the table is cleared. The rubble stays as it lies and nothing replays on load.
- `wall`: the eighteen places of the wall, row by row, each empty or holding a brick as its kind and its state. The states are: glass `whole`, `cracked` or `sagged`; jelly `fresh`, `thrown-once` or `thrown-twice`, each of them also `swollen` or not; ice `whole`, each also `dimpled` or not and with an `icicle` or not, or a `pool` lying in its place; seed `whole`, `vine` or `tall-vine`, a vine also `browned` or not, and also `gone` or not: a vine whose seed was popped or driven in stays where it grew, with its seed in the tray, and is no brick and fills no place: it is stored in the first column of its row, the lane it stands in, and the seed's own place is empty; sleepy `asleep` or `deep`. A sleepy brick that has shuffled or dropped is saved in the place it reached.
- `rubble`: the bricks that have fallen, in the order they fell, each as its kind, the column of the wall it fell from and, for a kind that can lie there in two forms, its form: a jelly as `splat` or `syrup`, a seed as `flower` or `sprout`. They lie in a tray of sand set into the board under the foot of the wall, under a clear cover that the ball rolls over. Each lies where it fell, under its own column, tumbled onto what fell there before it. The tray has no slots and no order from left to right; sand is in it from the first frame and shows between the heaps whatever has fallen, so it is never seen empty or full and keeps no count. A fallen brick stored without a column lies under the column its order gives it.
- `bumper`: where the bumper stands along the floor.
- `seat`: where a ball at rest lies along the bumper, from one end to the other, so that a ball resting off the middle is found where it was left; the middle for a ball that was not at rest.
- `ball`: the kind of the ball: `plain`, `wet`, `hot` or `heavy`.
- `shown`: the first showings that have started: `hot-gate`, `wet-gate`.

**What is not stored, and is gone on load.** The ball in the air, rolling off the wall, in a gate or in a toy's hold: such a ball is saved as resting on the middle of the bumper, and on load it rests there until it is flicked. A brick in the middle of falling is saved as fallen, and one in the middle of shuffling is saved in the place it is going to; a sleepy brick that has gone over to the rail to lean on it is saved in its own place, to which it comes back. The damp patch and the scorch ring on the drum, steam, the cloud on fogged glass, the shine on wiped glass and on a jelly the wet ball slipped round, the frost cap on the ball, the sticky thread, wet tracks, a jelly's shaking, every reaction of a large toy, the way a sleepy brick has turned over under a finger, the place of the ladybird along its walk, and the upward look of the faces in the tray after a clearing are short-lived views and are not stored: on load a sleepy brick lies as it first lay, the ladybird is at the middle of its walk, and the faces in the tray look at the ball. The largest legal state is far below half of the 64 KB cap, and a test says so.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

**Three large toys stand at the sides of the table, and each is a gate.** Each has one visible want that never changes: it holds its gate open towards the ball and its eyes follow the ball wherever it goes, except over a cleared wall while the ball is not in the air, when they are on the folded wall, and while the next wall unfolds, when they are on the sheet ("The scenes"). The want is about the ball, never about the child.

| Toy | Its gate, and what it does to the ball | Body and tempo | Funniest part | Loves | Cannot stand, and what that does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The walrus, in a tin tub at the lower left | The ball rolls into the tub and is spat out wet, in a spout of water | huge, slow, sloshing | whiskers that spring out straight and twang | anything wet: a melt, a splash or a sploosh in the pool, a wet slap on the drum. It wallows in its tub for each, one way and back, so that the water slops over the rim, and snorts through its whiskers; its flippers stay on the rim, and it claps for nothing | heat: a hot ball going by makes its whiskers curl up tight and it sinks to its eyes in the tub with a "blub" |
| The dragon, at the upper left | The ball rolls into its mouth and is hiccuped out hot, in a smoke ring | small head, long neck, quick and jerky | a flame at one nostril that it keeps trying to hide | anything that breaks or pops: glass in shards, a seed popped. It puffs one smoke ring for each | wet: a wet ball going by puts its flame out with a fizzle, and it goes cross-eyed trying to light it again |
| The elephant, along the right side | The ball is sucked up its trunk and blown out heavy, grey and dull | very large, heavy, late: it moves after everyone else | ears that it uses as hands | deep booms: the drum, most of all under a heavy ball. Its ears flap once for each boom that is loud enough to move anything, as large as the boom | high tinkles: when glass falls in shards it folds its ears over its eyes and peeps through |

**The five kinds of brick are small characters with one habit each,** which the grid sets out: glass rings and breaks, jelly throws, ice wants warming, a seed wants to grow, and a sleepy brick wants to sleep and moves over. Their faces are two eyes and a mouth inside the brick, they watch the ball, and they flinch as it comes. A brick that has fallen peers out of the rubble at whatever happens next.

**The tastes never change**, so a child can learn them and test them on purpose: a hot ball taken past the walrus, a wet one past the dragon, a wall of glass broken all at once for the elephant.

**The reactions are the feedback.** A toy reacts to the exact thing that just happened on the table, within a third of a second, large enough to read from across a room. No toy thanks, praises or blames the child, complains of waiting, or remarks on the child stopping, leaving or coming back. A toy that a trick is played on is bewildered and never hurt. A finger on a toy is a poke, which it answers as itself and which changes nothing on the table: the walrus's whiskers twang and it slaps its belly, the dragon hiccups a smoke ring, the elephant toots a short raspberry and flaps both ears.

**A bystander with nothing to do with the task:** a ladybird walks along the apron at the top of the table, above the roof rail, stops, and walks on. At every size the table is shown at, its walk starts clear of the home control and stops short of the grown-up's corner. A finger on it makes it open its wings with a whirr and settle further along.

## The scenes

Each scene is a list of timed beats filled in from the state of play, built on `scene.ts`. Its outcome is saved when it starts. Any touch ends it at once with everything where the scene was taking it, and that touch is then an ordinary touch. Every answer to a touch or to the ball is written the same way, as beats that set a pose and its notes at one moment, so that sight and sound share one clock.

**A gate** (about 1.5 seconds; an answer, and short enough not to be a scene). *Cause:* the child steers the ball into a toy's gate. *Beats:* the toy draws the ball in from the rail to where it holds it, in sight all the way, in its own manner; a beat of holding; the ball comes out changed and falls towards the floor. *Saved at the start:* `ball`, as the kind it will come out.

**The clearing** (about 5 seconds, the ending). *Cause:* the hit that takes the last brick off a wall. A pool is not a brick: the hit that melts the last standing brick clears the wall, and the pool lies there until the next wall unfolds. The scene starts at that hit, while the brick is still on its way to the rubble, so its save is made before anything lands. *Beats:* the last brick lands in the rubble; the faces in the rubble look up one after another, in the order they fell; no large toy answers the clearing: none replays its poke, and none makes a sound or a move of gladness (no toot, no hop, no belly slap, no hiccup). Each turns instead, after its own delay and without a sound, to watch the folded wall, in a posture of its own: the dragon stretches its neck up, the walrus sits up in its tub and leans back, the elephant holds both ears pricked out wide. They stay turned for as long as the wall lies cleared, with their eyes on the folded wall whenever the ball is not in the air, and they watch the sheet unfold and the next wall stand. No toy claps, at a clearing or at any other time. What a toy does about the last hit itself, as about any hit (a smoke ring for glass in shards), is its taste and comes with the hit. Then the folded wall at the top lifts its near corner towards the bell and keeps it lifted, and the bell swings once and hangs still: the bell and the unfolding are the scene. The turned toys, like the lifted corner, are a view of `finished` and need no field. A touch that ends the scene leaves it silent from that frame: what is still sounding is cut, and the last brick finishes its fall unheard. *Filled in from:* `rubble`. *Saved at the start:* `finished`, the last brick out of `wall` and into `rubble`, and `position` moved one place up.

**The unfolding** (about 4 seconds). *Cause:* the bell is rung while the wall on the table is cleared, by a finger or by a ball the child has flicked or sprung since the last brick fell. The flight that took the last brick rings the bell as a bell and unfolds nothing. *Beats:* the faces duck down into the rubble and the rubble is swept out of its tray by the folded wall's own shadow, which takes with it, as it passes them, a pool or a vine the last wall left on the board: they are seen until then; the concertina drops and opens row by row; each brick stands up in its place with its own note, low to high; the ball, wherever it is, goes on as it was, and a brick whose place the ball is in, or that would close the ball in, stands up when the ball has left, and sounds its note then, once: no ball is ever shut in. The three large toys watch the sheet unfold and the wall stand, and turn back to the ball when it stands. *Filled in from:* the new wall. *Saved at the start:* `wall` as the new wall laid out from `position`, `rubble` emptied, `finished` cleared.

**The hot gate, shown** (about 3 seconds, once ever). *Cause:* a wall that holds ice stands, no scene is playing, no finger is down, the toy that shows it does not have the ball (in its gate, or fetching it from the wall), and two seconds of attended time have passed since the child's last touch. The two seconds count from the first press of a visit, never from the opening, and they do not run under a finger that is held down: a table nobody has touched since it was opened or found again shows nothing. *Beats:* the dragon coughs up a spare ball of its own, which glows; it rolls once round the dragon's feet, leaving a scorched ring that fades, and the dragon swallows it again. It shows what the gate does to a ball and melts nothing. *Saved at the start:* `hot-gate` in `shown`. A game put away before the showing starts plays nothing on load, and the showing starts two seconds after a touch of the next visit, with no finger down, while a wall with ice stands.

**The wet gate, shown** (about 3 seconds, once ever). *Cause:* the same, for the first wall that holds a sleepy brick. *Beats:* the walrus lifts a spare ball out of its tub on its nose, dripping, drops it back with a splash and snorts through its whiskers. *Saved at the start:* `wet-gate` in `shown`, by the same rule.

The elephant's gate is never shown: no brick needs a heavy ball, and what it does is there to be found.

**Secrets** play the same way every time and are never hinted at: the heavy ball's boom on the drum, a ball taken twice through one gate, the pool under each kind of ball, a sagged glass popped, and the bell under each kind of ball.

**How a cycle ends and the next one starts.** The clearing ends on a finished scene that stays as long as the child likes: the faces peer from the rubble, the ball goes on bouncing or resting, the bumper and the gates still work, and the three toys still answer. If the child does nothing, nothing new starts, and there is no countdown. The folded wall waits at the top in plain view, to the left of the middle, with the bell beside it at the top of the right-hand lane, and neither hurries the child. The next wall unfolds when the bell is rung. On load nothing replays: the table is as it was left, a wall half taken apart or a cleared one in rubble, with the ball resting on the bumper and the folded wall waiting.

## The look

Written after the style spike, not part of the sheet. The look is **Pinball table**, the first look reserved for the game, in canvas 2D. The spike held on clarity, so the second reserved look was not tried. No frame rate has been measured: the machine the game was built on cannot measure one.

**What it is.** An arcade playfield seen from straight above, in bright daylight. A varnished wooden board with printed art under the varnish, chrome rails and posts under red rubber caps, a red rubber bumper on a chrome stem, pale plastic inserts set in the board, and one steel ball. The three large toys stand in their own printed panels beside the field, the way the big moulded toys of a table stand at its sides, and a strip of sky with lamps runs along the top. Everything has one dark blue ink line of one weight and flat colour with a single highlight; nothing is brass, walnut or dim.

**Palette.** All colours are in `paint.ts` (`C` and `INK`). Ink `#1e2a52`. Board cream `#f8ecc9` with rays `#f2deae`; wood rim `#dfa95f`. Chrome `#c5d1e0` between `#64748f` and white. Rubber red `#e5433d`. Glass `#8fe0f0`. The dragon's panel is coral and flame (`#ff8a5c`, `#ffc247`, `#ff6338`), the walrus's is water (`#41c3ea`), the elephant's is leaf (`#86d660`, `#5bbd49`), and the sky is `#63bdf6`. The toys are green `#8ed43f`, brown `#b8846a` and lilac blue `#8fa0ee`: no two share a hue, and none shares the red of the bumper or the blue of the glass. The field keeps the palest colours of the frame so that the ball, the bricks and the bumper are the clearest things in it.

**Materials.** Wood is flat cream with faint grain lines. Chrome is a three-stop vertical gradient with a white highlight line. Rubber is flat red with a darker underside and one gloss stroke. The steel ball is one sprite with a fixed chrome gradient and a warm band low on it, standing for the board it mirrors; it is stretched along its flight and never redrawn.

**The five bricks.** Each kind has its own outline as well as its own colour, so that a child who cannot tell the colours apart still tells the bricks apart.

- Glass is a pale blue block with a lighter top band, a darker foot and a white glint. A cracked one keeps its face: one jagged line with no branch runs down the right end, clear of the eyes. A sagged one hangs in a long drip from its top edge.
- Jelly is a pink dome on a white plate, anchored at its foot, so that it wobbles and swells from the plate up. A swollen one stands nearly two bricks high.
- Ice is a near-white block with two pale slanted facets, a wide one at its top left corner and a narrow one at its foot on the right, and small far-apart eyes. An icicle hangs under it, a dimple is a dent in its face, and its pool is a flat blue puddle with a pale edge and no ink line.
- A seed is a brown nut with a seam. Its vine is a green tendril with leaves in the empty first column, drawn the same way whether its seed stands or not: it comes straight out of the board, its stem bends one way and the other, its tip droops over and curls into a spiral of a turn and a half, and a short one keeps two leaves, unlike in size, at different heights on opposite sides. While the seed stands, a runner lies low along the board from the seed to the vine's foot, nearly flat, and hooks nowhere; when the seed has been popped or driven in the vine stays and the runner is gone. A browned vine has brown curled leaves. A short vine lifts the ball as its tip reaches it, never ahead of it. Up a tall vine the ball winds round the stem, and the stem is drawn over the ball while the ball is behind it.
- A sleepy brick is a lilac bed with a pillow, a face with shut eyes and a chequered quilt. In deep sleep it is warm in colour. Turned over, it faces the other way. With a brick beside it and no gap, it leans on that brick; at the end of its row it shuffles over to the rail, is drawn against it with its near end on the rail, leans there and snores once, and shuffles back. The rules move it, so it is met wherever it has got to, and it waits while the ball is between it and the rail.

Every brick has two eyes and a mouth. As the ball comes, the face shrinks away from it inside the brick, the eyes widen and the mouth goes round; the brick itself stays where the rules meet it. An ice brick breathes a small pale puff every few seconds, each at a moment of its own. The shine on a wiped face is two unlike streaks: one long and wide, one short and thin, at different heights, one leaning well over and one nearly upright.

**The walrus's flippers.** Both lie over the rim of the tub. One comes down on its belly for a slap, or to its chest to rub a ball dry; the other never leaves the rim, so the two never meet and the walrus has no pose that is a clap. For anything wet it wallows: it rolls one way and back in the tub so that the water slops over the rim, and snorts through whiskers sprung out straight.

**The four balls.** Plain is chrome. Wet is blue with drops on it and a trail of drops. Hot is orange with an additive glow at the tiers that have lights. Heavy is dark grey and dull, with no glint. Each is its own sprite; the kind the ball is shown as changes when the toy gives it back, not when it goes in.

**What lies in the tray.** The tray is a trough of sand with low dunes, grains and a few pebbles in it from the first frame. Glass lies as chips, a jelly as a pink splat or a pool of syrup, a seed as a white flower or a sprout, a sleepy brick as a small bed that snores once as it lands and then sleeps on without a sound. Each piece but the sleeper has a pair of eyes that follow the ball.

**Lighting.** No light is computed. The lamps along the apron and the inserts in the board light as additive sprites when something rings, and the inserts of the lower field light as the ball passes over them. The glow under the bumper in the idle ladder is the same kind of sprite. Shadows are one soft oval sprite under the ball.

**How it is drawn.** `paintBoard` paints the whole table once into one sprite for the size and density in use, and with it as much of the table's own surroundings as the surface shows past the stage: the sky of the apron with clouds above, the sand the drum skin stands in below, and each toy's panel colour at the sides; `paintPieces` and `paintToys` cut the moving things as sprites at the same density. A frame is the board stamp, then sprite stamps for every moving thing, then live fills and strokes: the dragon's neck, the elephant's trunk, the walrus's whiskers, the eyes and lids of the toys, the faces of the bricks and of the fallen pieces batched into one path each, the drum's ripple, the shards, and two batched fills for every drop and every puff on screen. The draw count the game reports is sprite stamps plus live fills and strokes, and exactly one of them covers the whole surface. A test plays every answer the game has and holds the count at 80 or under; the fullest frame it reaches counts 73. A fetch adds the tail (two strokes, and two fills for its tuft) or the trunk, over a wall that is by then partly down.

**Motion rules.** Every moving thing has its own manner in `motion.ts`, and no two share one: the bumper is a stiff rubber spring, the drum skin rings on after a hit, the bell swings long and slow, the dragon is quick and twitchy, the walrus is heavy and slow to start, the elephant answers late (and each fetches or draws in a ball at that tempo: the dragon's tail is out in a fifth of a second, the elephant's trunk takes over half a second and carries at half the speed), a jelly wobbles on, ice shivers short and tight, a sleepy brick moves slowly and settles without a bounce. An answer is written once in `answers.ts` as timed beats, each beat setting a pose and its notes at the same instant, so sight and sound cannot drift. The view reads every pose value the rules set, and a test fails if one is set and never read. The ball squashes the bumper on the catch and stretches in flight; a brick that rings jumps before it settles; a falling brick drops with weight and wobbles as it lands. At idle the lamps chase, the toys breathe and blink each at its own rate, the dragon holds its mouth open towards the ball and opens it wider as the ball comes to its gate, and every few seconds it squints at the flame at its nostril, which shrinks to a spark and then gets out larger than before, the ladybird walks the apron, and the folded wall shifts when a wall is cleared. Over a cleared wall no toy answers the clearing: each turns to the folded wall after its own delay, without a sound and in a posture of its own, and stays so until the next wall stands. The dragon stretches its neck up, except while the ball is coming to its mouth or is in it; the walrus sits up in its tub and leans back; the elephant holds both ears pricked out wide. Their eyes go to the folded wall while the ball is not in the air, and to the foot of the sheet while it unfolds.

**The tiers.** `TIERS` in `config.ts` steps down the pixel density (2, 1.5, 1.25, 1) and nothing a child would miss: the two lower tiers leave out the additive lamp, insert and hot-ball lights, where the inserts are a flat fill instead, and throw fewer shards from a breaking brick. The board, the toys, every face and every motion are the same at every tier, and a test holds that a tier without lights draws no more than one with them.

### Where things are, and why nothing passes through anything

All of it is in `layout.ts`, in units of a stage of 1180 by 820 that is scaled whole onto the surface, and `layout.test.ts` holds each line below at surfaces of 1180 by 820, 1024 by 768 and 820 by 1180, and the ladybird's walk at phone sizes as well. A second test plays a random child for forty seconds at every place of the order, on three seeds, and checks after every step of the rules that the ball is inside no brick, the bell, the bumper, a rail, the roof or the floor.

- **The field** lies between the two chrome rails (262 to 918 across), under the roof rail (100) and over the drum-skin floor (772). The ball is 17 in radius.
- **The wall** is three rows of six places, 84 by 74 each, starting 190 from the top; a brick is 78 by 42 in the middle of its place, so there is a gap all round every brick and no two bricks touch. The gap between two rows is 32, narrower than the ball, so no ball gets in between them. The rows stand this far apart so that a brick's own place is 48 px each way on a tablet held upright as well as on its side. A swollen jelly grows upwards by 38, and it grows only when the ball is out of the way.
- **The tray.** What has fallen lies in a tray of sand set into the board under the foot of the wall (516 wide and 56 deep, just below the bottom row), under a clear cover that is level with the board. It is below the surface the ball rolls on: the ball crosses the cover and touches nothing in it. This is why the ball goes over the fallen bricks and not into them, and it was chosen over making them solid. A fallen piece lies where it fell, under the column of the wall it fell from, tumbled onto what fell there before it, at 0.75 of its painted size and at its own lean. There are no slots and no order from left to right. The tests hold that every piece is wholly inside the tray, that the heaps under two columns never touch, that no two heaps lie a like step apart or on a like line, and that with every brick of a wall fallen the pieces cover less than half of the sand.
- **A ball at rest.** No ball stays on the wall, and none glides uphill by itself. A ball that has stayed within 6 units for half a second above the foot of the wall rolls off downhill only: it drops wherever it can and goes sideways only along the top of something that bears it, never across a gap and never up, slowly at first and faster down a drop, with a low rumble that rises with its speed, and the bricks it passes flinch. It is let go under the foot of the wall. Where no such way exists (a pit, a shelf) the nearer of the dragon and the elephant fetches it: the dragon whips its tail out and curls the end round the ball, the elephant, late and slowly, stretches its trunk over the wall and hooks the tip in under the ball. The ball is lifted off the board (drawn a little larger, with its shadow apart from it), carried over the wall to the bumper wherever that stands, and set on its middle, at rest. The walrus and the ladybird fetch nothing: the wall is out of the walrus's reach. Either way the ball strikes nothing. A ball that comes down on the top of a brick rings it once; landing on the same brick again it rings nothing and keeps a fifth of its bounce. Tests put a ball at rest on every brick top of every wall of the order, with each one brick gone and with many gone, and on the last wall laid out from ten seeds, and hold that it never rises while it rolls and is in nothing unless a toy holds it.
- **No ball is shut in.** A brick that is about to stand up waits while the ball is in its place, and also while standing would leave the ball no way out to under the wall. A test unfolds every wall of the order that can unfold, and the last wall from three seeds, round a ball put in each of its eighteen places.
- **A pool** lies flat on the board in the box of the ice brick it was. It is not a brick: the ball rolls through it and is answered once as it comes in. A pool the last wall left, and a vine that still stood, are drawn until the shadow of the folded wall has swept past them as it sweeps the tray; where there is one, the shadow is drawn over the board as well as the tray.
- **The vine** grows in the first column of the wall, which a wall with a seed keeps empty, and the seed stands in the second column beside it. A short vine ends one brick above the seed's row and a tall one under the roof. The lane is clear of every other column, and a ball is taken up only where it can reach the lane and travel up it without crossing a brick; if a brick comes into its way it is let go where it is. A ball the vine has carried is let by until it has come down to the bumper or the drum, so a vine never passes a ball round by itself, in whichever row its seed stands.
- **The gates** are openings in the side rails: the dragon's at the upper left (158 to 266 down the rail), the walrus's at the lower left (546 to 664), the elephant's on the right (404 to 536). Each is wider than the ball, clear of the roof, the floor and the gate beside it, and no brick, pool or vine and no part of the tray lies in a gate or where a ball comes out of one. A ball goes in only when its whole width is inside the opening as it reaches the rail; anywhere else along a rail it bounces off. The toy draws the ball in from the rail to where it holds it, each in its own manner, and the ball is out of sight only there: the dragon snaps it up at once, straight, in under a tenth of a second; the walrus lets it roll up over the rim of the tub and drop in, gathering speed, in about a third of a second; the elephant is late, so the ball waits at the rail, and is then sucked straight up the trunk faster and faster. A gate is printed as a splash of its toy's colour with three lobes of unequal reach, and no dark bar.
- **The bumper** stands on the floor and slides only between the rails. It is a cap of rubber, 148 wide and 36 deep with round ends and its top at 676, on a chrome stem 104 wide. The shape is in `layout.ts` (`BUMPER_SHAPE`), and the paint and the rules both take it from there: the top takes the ball, the round end of the cap keeps a ball beside it, and a ball on the floor rolls in under the cap's overhang until it touches the stem, which is what knocks it along. A ball pushed against a rail stops the bumper. The tray's foot is more than a ball's width above the ball that rests on the bumper.
- **The bell** hangs inside the field at the top of the right-hand lane, beside the wall, and is solid to the ball: nothing fits where it is, so a ball that slips round a jelly is let go before it, and a ball that only lies against it rings nothing. **The folded wall** lies on the apron above the roof rail, left of the middle, where no ball goes.
- **The top edge.** The jam's home control is a round button of 48 px at the top centre of the surface. No lamp, and neither the folded wall nor the bell, lies under it. The ladybird's walk is set from the size the table is shown at: it starts clear of the home control at any size, phones included, and stops short of the top right corner of 72 by 72 px, which answers nothing. The bell's target is larger than 48 px on every surface tested.
- **A finger on a brick.** A brick's finger target reaches past its drawn box as far as the surface asks, so that it is 48 px each way at any size; where two targets meet, the nearer brick takes the finger. A finger that just misses a brick is on that brick and does not take the bumper. On a full wall a brick's own share is its place in the wall, which is 58 by 51 px on a surface of 820 by 1180, 55 by 48 px at 768 by 1024 and 73 by 64 px at 1024 by 768. On a phone the share is smaller than 48 px and the nearer brick takes the finger.

### Marks that could be taken for a sign

The band starts at five, so the kid side shows no letter, numeral or sign, and no printed mark may look like one. What was redrawn and what was kept is listed in the "Reader" section of `REFINEMENT.md`. The rules the paint keeps: no lone ring, no ring inside a ring, no cross, no tick, no arrow, no bar that fills, no even row of like marks, and no marks that take turns (large and small, high and low, one colour and another). A burst is uneven and has a face, a crack has no branch, a strap slants and is tied to its parcel, a chain has three links, a post is one disc, and so is a lamp of the apron: one yellow dome with an ink line and a streak of gloss, with no chrome ring round it. Along the whole apron neither the heights of the lamps nor the gaps between them take turns for more than three in a row. The tub's handles are solid lugs with no rivet. The tacks of the drum skin are twelve, hammered in by hand: no two gaps alike, at uneven depths, with heads of uneven size. The lamps of the lower field and the pale ones under the tray are scattered by hand in spacing, height, size and colour, and none of the four takes turns for more than three in a row; the pale ones are six and lie in no bowl. The elephant's hair is one solid lock swept to a point, a filled shape and no curled stroke. The shine on a wiped face is one streak that leans well over and one nearly upright. A vine is a curled tendril with two leaves, whether its seed stands or has gone, and the runner from a standing seed lies nearly flat and hooks nowhere, so the vine has one curl and no second one to match it. The dragon's tail ends in a tuft, a bunch of four round lobes of unlike sizes with no point. The scorch the dragon's spare ball leaves round its feet is a smudge of overlapping blots of uneven size, laid only behind the ball, with no dashes. What lies in the tray is a heap under the column it fell from. `layout.test.ts`, `view.test.ts` and `paint.test.ts` hold each of these as numbers. `paint.test.ts` reads the paint calls and fails on a closed outline stroked on bare ground.
