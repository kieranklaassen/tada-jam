# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

## The band and its age rule

The manifest band is 3 to 6. Three governs: the cue table's row for 3 to 4 applies with its "Avoid" column (no text, numeral or icon, no spoken instruction, no verdict, one next act offered at a time). Everything essential is a tap; a drag is an extra way and counts when partly done. Targets are about 100 units across, at least 105 units centre to centre, and away from the bottom edge. `ctx.childAge` changes nothing: three-year-olds and six-year-olds get the same room, so no age given is the same as any age.

## The toy

Tap a thing in the basket: it squashes and sounds in the same frame, leaps in an arc over the room and drops into the giant boot with its own thump, and the boot bulges. A bare foot hangs over the boot with its toes wiggling. The foot goes in, the owner holds still while the eyes grow (the room and the finger never stop), and then the owner goes off. The thing comes out, hops back into the basket, and the boot is empty again. Dropping a thing into a deep boot, and seeing what it does to a foot, is a pleasure with nothing to achieve. Tickling the bare toes makes the owner giggle.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

Seven things: mandarin, carrot, biscuit man, frog, mouse, porridge, bell. Three are the proper treats of the evening and four are not; all of them work and none is better. Alone in the boot:

| Thing | What the foot finds | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mandarin | juice fountains out of the top and falls into the owner's open mouth, which closes on an orange grin | squelch, pour, slurp |
| carrot | its tip pokes out through the toe of the boot; a kick sends it up and into the mouth | pop, whirl, crunch |
| biscuit | three crunching stamps, a cloud of crumbs, crumbs falling on a licking tongue | crunch, patter |
| frog | the boot hops off with the owner in it, along the room and back, and the frog ends on the owner's head | boing, croak |
| mouse | a lump runs up the leg and over the head while the owner squirms and giggles | scurry, squeak |
| porridge | the boot swells, porridge shoots out of the toe and lands on the owner's face, which licks itself clean | gurgle, splat |
| bell | every step tinkles, and the owner cannot stop dancing | bell |

Two things in one boot do something together, one result for each of eleven kinds of pair: frog and mouse chase each other round the owner's head; frog and porridge hop splattering; frog and bell leap three times as high and ring; a frog that meets a treat eats it and comes out fat; a mouse eats its way through the porridge and out of the toe, and the owner's toes follow it through the hole; a mouse steals a treat over the owner's head until the mouth catches it; a mouse wears the bell; porridge fires the bell up out of sight until it comes down on the owner's head; porridge fires a treat into the owner's mouth; the bell bounces a treat on the foot; two treats come out stuck on the toes. A third thing makes the first one jump back out.

A pair is heard as both things, one after the other as the foot finds them, and then in its own rhythm.

Wrong uses work. On the owner's head, then sneezed off: the mandarin rolls from side to side (hum), the carrot stands as a horn (toot), the biscuit rocks and crumbles (patter), the frog hops (croak), the mouse runs ear to ear (squeak), the porridge slides over the eyes (glop), the bell swings (ring). On the floor each bounces home with its own landing sound. Every owner has a different jump, so each row above looks different on each of five owners.

Day 15: what two things do together on each owner, whose favourite is what, and filling five shoes at night so that five people go off along the row, each with what the child laid there.

## The representation

No school idea. The room is the evening shoes are set out and treats are found in them, with the child as the one who fills the shoe. No saint and no helper of any kind appears.

## The four mechanic questions

- **Swap.** No school content, so there is nothing to swap.
- **Attention.** At the moment of choosing the child thinks about what this thing will do to that foot.
- **Fun.** The choice is the fun: what goes in is what goes off.
- **Guess.** Any tap works; there is no success to guess at.

## The error as a consequence

There is no wrong attempt. A thing that misses the boot bounces back to the basket.

## The designed order, and what is stored

No ladder. The room opens one thing at a time: first one boot and one foot; after the first foot has gone in, the next owner looks round the edge at the left; the sun in the window only dips and comes back up before that, and sets at a touch after it. A touch on the next owner is the only thing that changes the owner by day: things in the boot hop home, the present owner hops away and the next comes in. The next owner is always the next in a fixed order of five, so no position lays anyone out.

Stored: the version, the owner, what is in each of the five shoes, day or night, whether the whole round has been shown, and whether a foot has gone in yet. A thing in the hand or on a head is stored in the basket. Eaten, squeezed or crumbled things are whole again in the basket when the go ends; a fat frog, a swollen boot, a hole and spills last only for the go. A round's outcome, the shoe empty again, is stored when the foot starts down.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Each one's want is the same and always visible: the bare foot over the shoe; the next owner holds its bare foot out round the edge, and at night five bare feet stick out of the blanket towards the shoes. Tastes never change and show as a longer, rounder bliss at the end: the grandmother (mandarin, jumps straight up and comes down slowly under her skirt), the bear (porridge, heavy, spins), the goose (bell, many small flapping hops), the elephant (mouse, rises like a balloon and lands so that the basket jumps), the white horse (carrot, rears). Each also has one dislike, as funny as the favourite, which ends in a shudder: the grandmother the frog, the bear the bell, the goose the porridge, the elephant the biscuit, the horse the mouse. The victim is bewildered and delighted and never hurt. A taste changes nothing the child can do.

## The scenes

- **The whole round, shown once.** On a first visit the frog hops into the boot by itself, the foot goes in, the owner hops off and back, and the frog goes home. About six seconds. Its mark is stored when it starts. Any touch ends it with everything in place.
- **A foot goes in.** Caused by the child's thing in the boot, two and a half seconds after the last thing landed and no finger is down, or at once on a touch of the foot or the boot. The foot sinks and trembles meanwhile. Three and a half to under seven seconds. A thing touched meanwhile squashes in the same frame; once the foot has had time to find what is there (two seconds) any touch ends the reaction with everything home, and a touched thing leaps in. Before that the touched thing waits its turn, or a child who taps fast would never see what came of it. On load the foot never goes in by itself: with a thing stored in the boot it waits for a touch of foot, boot or thing. When the foot starts down, the empty shoe and the mark that a foot has gone in are stored together.
- **Night and morning.** Touch the sun: it goes down, the owners are five sleeping heads in one long bed, and five shoes stand in a row. When the sun sets, things in the boot hop home. Things go into the shoe they are dropped in, or on a tap into the next empty one; a shoe holds two, and with none empty a tap fills the first shoe holding one. A touched shoe tips out. Touch the moon: the sun comes up and five feet go in one after another along the row. An empty shoe lets out a puff of dust and a moth. Day and the five empty shoes are stored when the sun starts up. It lasts at most 25 seconds; a touch sends the next foot in at once, or, after the last, ends it. The room is then the one boot with the same owner as before the night, the next one waiting at the edge for a touch.

A finished round stays as long as the child likes. Nothing starts by itself but the foot, which is the answer to the thing the child put in.

## The records

No learning goal, so no records and no claim.

## The look

Thick-line primaries, the first of the two looks set aside for the game. The manner is a mid-century Dutch picture book: frontal, flat, few things, all large.

- **Colour.** Red, yellow, blue and green, white and orange; brown for the bear, the biscuit and the bed, grey for the elephant. No shade, no gradient, one flat shadow on the floor that says how high someone has leapt. By night the same room in navy, with the bed and the things at full colour.
- **Line.** One black line, 9 units on a 1180-unit stage, round everything, with a small tremble that is the same every time. A shape is stretched point by point, so a squash never thins its line.
- **Faces.** White round eyes with a dot that follows the finger and grow when the foot has found something. Eight mouths.
- **Motion.** Nothing cross-fades. Each owner leaps as nobody else does (`owners.ts`), and what a foot finds is one pure picture per moment (`acts.ts`).
- **Working pieces.** The seven things are the plainest shapes and lie on white.
- **Tiers** change the pixel ratio only. The room is painted twice at the start, once for day and once for night, past the stage on every side.

Before a touch: about a third of the frame is bare wall and floor; a very large grandmother with one bare foot in the air over a boot as high as her chest is funny; toes, eyes, sun, snow, cat and frog are alive.
