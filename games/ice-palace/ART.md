# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

Ice Palace in one paragraph: the child feels a ringing block come out of the pond along the line the finger drew, and builds a house for light. The verb is cut, carry and set. The world answers with weight and sound by day and with light by night: when the child pulls the sun down, lamps are hung in what was built and their light passes through it as the material allows, so the pattern on the snow is the child's own wall.

## The band and its age rule

- **Band.** The manifest band is 5 to 9. Its youngest age, 5, governs the design.
- **The cue-table row.** The row for 5 to 6 in `docs/solutions/conventions/wordless-clarity-for-the-declared-age-band.md`. It allows a ghost hand showing one move, a breathing glow on what can be touched now, characters who gaze and reach, materials that correct themselves, and more than one thing live at once with one clear function each. Its "Avoid" column is kept as a hard limit: no word or letter, no numeral or symbol (the band starts at 5), no voice instruction, no hint that gives an answer.
- **The pack's age rules.** For the youngest of the band (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md): a place, props and characters who react, with the plot left to the child; tap, drag and tracing, and a traced line survives a lifted finger; no reading and no double tap; the one a joke is played on overreacts and is never hurt. For the oldest, who is 9 (pack: game-design, ages-9-to-12.md): the light is a real system that behaves the same way every time, any palace stands, a better one is visibly better on the snow, and nothing is praised.
- **The symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or symbol, and the game has no `symbols.ts`. The rule follows the manifest band and never the child's age while playing.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only what a first visit finds, never what can be reached:
  - 6 and younger: one block already lies cut on the ice beside its hole, and two blocks stand as a first step on the snow near King Winter, so the first touch can be a carry or a cut.
  - 7 and older: the pond is whole and the snow is bare.
  - No age (`null`): as for 6 and younger.
  - In every case the berry block lies by its bush. A saved clearing always wins over the age, and every kind of block and every action is there for every child from the first touch.

## The toy

The one action the finger performs most is the cut: the finger lands on the pond and draws a line across the ice.

- **When the finger lands.** The saw bites in the same frame: chips fly from the spot, a white scratch starts under the finger, and there is a short bright tick. The frost child lying at the pond's edge looks up.
- **While it moves.** The scratch is a straight saw line that starts where the finger landed. For the first short stretch it points at the finger; then its direction is set, and it is as long as the finger has gone along it. A saw cuts straight: a straight line gives a block with square ends and one length, which stacks and has one note, and a wandering hand still gets a straight block. Each stretch of line makes one grain of scraping, higher when the finger is fast and lower when it is slow, and chips spit backwards from the saw. The line stops, with a wooden clonk, at open water, at a block lying on the ice, at the pond's edge and at the longest block the pond gives.
- **When the finger lifts.** The block breaks free with a crack in the frame the finger lifts: it hops out of the pond, turns level, and lands on the ice beside the dark water it left, where it wobbles and rings. A finger that comes straight back to the end of the line carries it on: the block goes back in, and the longer line gives one block of the whole length. A long block rings low and a short one high. A block from the clear middle of the pond is clear and rings long; a block from the cloudy edge is cloudy and gives a short dull note. King Winter's brows go up, and the fish under the ice darts off.
- **Why again and again.** Every line gives a block of exactly the line's length, so the hand is making something each time, and each one sounds its own note. There is no right length and no right place. Random scratching always produces a block or, when it is very short, a spray of chips and a squeak. Nothing is asked, nothing can be done wrong, and a watcher sees within three seconds that the child is sawing ice (pack: game-design, toy-first.md; pack: game-design, touch-answers-bigger-than-the-touch.md).

The second action is the carry: a finger on a block knocks it (it sounds its note at once), a drag lifts it, and it rests where it is set.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

How each thing is got: a clear block is cut from the middle of the pond; a cloudy block is cut from its edge; a snow block is a long scoop of the hand through the drift; a snowball is a short scoop; an icicle hangs on the fir bough and comes away where it is pressed; the berry block is the one red thing and lies by its bush from the start.

| | Set down on the snow or on the wall | Knocked with a fingertip | Dropped in open water | A warm thumb held on it | Met by lamp light at dusk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clear block** | Lands square with a small squash; a glassy clink on ice, a soft crunch on snow. | Rings long and clean, low for a long block and high for a short one; a shiver runs along it. | Plops, bobs and freezes back: a skin of ice closes the hole from its ends with a rising crackle, and the pond is whole there. | Sweats, drips with small ticks, and sinks into a puddle that freezes flat with a sigh and is gone into the snow or ice under it: the block leaves `blocks` and no puddle is saved. | The light goes straight through and lies on the snow in front of the wall as the block's own outline, bright and sharp-edged, leaning away from its lamp, and moves with the block in the frame the block is moved; a clear bell note. |
| **Cloudy block** | Lands with a duller knock and a puff of frost dust. | A short dull tock, the pitch still set by its length; frost dust jumps off it. | Glugs under with a deep gulp, and the hole closes with a slow creak. | The cloud melts out of it first, with a hiss and then a ting: it is a clear block now. Held on, it melts as a clear block does. | The light comes through soft and speckled, with no edge; a breathy flute note. |
| **Snow block** | Lands with a flump and sheds a little snow; it sags a finger's width. | A muffled thup, and the fingertip leaves a dent that stays. | Fizzes into slush with a long hush; the slush sets and the hole is gone. | Slumps with a wet squelch into a snowball. | Stops the light: its own shape stays unlit on the snow; a low drum beat. |
| **Snowball** | Rolls to the lowest place it can reach, with a rumble, and stops against whatever is there. | A crisp scrunch, and it rocks. | Splashes and floats as a small white island that turns slowly, bubbling. | Shrinks with a fizz to a ball half the size. | Stops the light as a round unlit spot; a soft thump. |
| **Icicle** | Stands point up where it is set, or hangs point down under a block, with a tiny tick. | A high ting, and it trembles. | Goes in with a plip, bobs upright like a float, and freezes in as a spike standing out of the pond. | Drips with plinks and shortens to a stub. | Breaks the light into a fan of thin rays; a quick glitter of high notes. |
| **Berry block** | Lands with a round knock, and the berry inside wobbles after it. | A hollow boop; the berry bounces inside. | Goes in with a bloop and will not sink: it bobs in the hole, and the fish comes up to stare at it. | Sweats and the berry blushes and squeaks; it does not melt, so the one berry is never lost. | The light that crosses it comes out red; a warm reed note. |

The wrong use of each thing works. A block set on King Winter's head is worn as a hat until he nods it off onto the snow beside him; from the moment it is set the save holds it `lying` at that place, so the hat is a view of a saved block and has no field; a cut under a lying block stops at the block; ice dropped back in the pond is taken back and splashes whoever is near.

**Day 15.** On day 15 the child builds windows, not walls: three kinds of block and a lamp make pictures in light, the one red block is placed where the picture wants its colour, and a wall is also a row of bells tuned by the length of each cut. None of that is new content; it is what the same six things do together.

## The representation

Ice Palace has no learning goal, so no school idea is represented and there is no order of object, picture and symbol.

## The four mechanic questions

Ice Palace has no learning goal and claims no school skill, so the four questions have nothing to answer; its verb is cutting, carrying and setting ice, judged as a toy.

## The error as a consequence

Nothing in the game is wrong, but things can fail to do what the child hoped, and each failure shows in the world where and why:

- **A lamp behind snow lights nothing.** The frost child hangs the lamp, the snow block in front of it stays unlit, and its exact shape lies unlit on the snow in front. Swapping that one block for ice lets the light out.
- **A wall with no hollow gets no lamp inside it.** The frost children look for a hollow, find none, and stand their lamps on the snow beside the wall, where the light crosses only the blocks beside them.
- **Nothing will bear the king.** He walks in, tries the highest block with one foot, and if it is narrower than he is, rests on too little, has no free air over it or stands higher than the fourth row he sits down in the snow in a cloud of grumbling breath, looking at the place he wanted.
- **A cut meets something.** The saw stops at water, at a lying block or at the edge with a clonk, and the block that comes out is as long as the line got.

In every case the palace stays exactly as built, by day and by night. The child moves one block and the light, the lamp or the king answers at once. Nothing gives a verdict, and the failure is as good to watch as the success (pack: game-design, errors-show-as-consequences.md).

## The designed order, and what is stored

**The order.** The game has no learning goal and so no order of challenges. Its designed order has one place, with the id `pond`, and the position never moves: every cycle is judged `mixed`, which leaves it where it is. Depth comes from what the child chooses to build with the same six things. A harder thing is one the child picks and can see is harder: a taller wall, a lintel over a wider gap, a picture in light with the red block in it.

**A first visit, by age.** 6 and younger, and no age: one cut block beside its hole and two blocks standing as a step. 7 and older: a whole pond and bare snow. The berry block lies by its bush in both.

**What play changes.** Everything in the clearing is the child's doing: which blocks exist, where they rest, which holes are open in the pond, how much of the drift and which icicles are used, the dents in snow blocks, and whether it is day or night. Nothing changes with the time played or the time away, and no clock is read.

**No one waits at the edge.** The game has no customer. King Winter and the frost children are on stage from the first frame.

**Every field of the saved state.**

| Field | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | The version of the record. |
| `position` | The place in the designed order: always `pond`. |
| `finished` | True while it is night: the ending stands and nothing replays on load. It always equals `night`, which wins if the two disagree. |
| `night` | Whether the sun is down. |
| `blocks` | Every block, at most 64. For each: `kind` (`clear`, `cloudy`, `snow`, `ball`, `icicle` or `berry`), `len` (its length in stage units), `x` and `y` (its centre at rest), `place` (`built` when it stands on the snow bank or on other blocks, `lying` when it lies on the pond or the front snow, `afloat` for the berry block or a snowball in a hole, `frozen` for an icicle frozen into the pond, `hung` for an icicle under a block), `dent` (a snow block's fingertip dents, as up to four positions along it), and `cap` (it has stood through a morning and wears fresh snow). |
| `holes` | Every open cut in the pond, at most 40. For each: `x`, `y` (its centre), `angle` and `len`. A hole that a block has frozen back into is no longer in the list: the pond is whole there. |
| `drift` | How many scoops have been taken from the drift since the last morning, 0 to 8. |
| `icicles` | Which of the bough's five icicles have been taken since the last morning. |

A block in the hand, in the air or sinking is not saved as such: the save holds it where it came from or where it will rest (pack: game-design, the-world-keeps-and-waits.md). A line still under the finger when the game is put away is no cut: the scratch is gone on load and the pond is whole there. A line is cut in the frame the finger leaves it, so its block and its hole are saved from that frame on. Whatever the game itself sets down (a block hopping out of the pond, the hat the king nods off, a thing laid on the ice at morning, the blocks of a first visit) comes to rest in the nearest free place, never on another block and never over a hole. The lamps, the light on the snow, where King Winter sits and where the frost children stand at night are not fields: each is worked out from `blocks` and `night` by rules that have no chance in them, so the same palace gives the same night every time. The fish, the bird, falling snow and breath are short-lived and start afresh on load. A largest legal state is well under half of the 64 KB cap. When `blocks` holds 64 or `holes` holds 40 the clearing gives no more: a cut answers as a very short one does, with a spray of chips and a squeak, a scoop comes away as a puff of snow and a pressed icicle as a drip. Nothing is counted or shown, and sinking or melting a block makes room.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

- **Old King Winter.** Very large, slow and sleepy, with a beard that reaches the snow and a red cushion under his arm. His one want is always visible: a high seat. He looks at the highest thing in the clearing and pats his cushion. Likes: height; a seat at least as wide as he is, resting on enough to bear him and standing no higher than the fourth row, where he can still climb, with free air over it for his whole height and width (nothing on it, no roof over it and no lamp in it); the widest gap, which he always uses to walk in; clear ice, which he polishes with his beard as he passes. Dislikes: getting wet (a splash makes his beard droop and he shakes like a dog); a point under him (set an icicle on his seat and he rises straight up with a squawk, bewildered and unhurt); having no seat, which he says in a cloud of grumbling breath to the place he wanted, never to the child.
- **The lamp child.** A frost child who sits on the lip of the snow bank, legs swinging, holding a lamp that is not lit yet. Its one want: a hollow to hang the lamp in. It holds the lamp up towards any gap under a block the moment one exists; while there is none it holds the lamp out by its hook over the bare bank, looks under it and all round for a place to hang it, and finds none. Likes: hollows; clear ice in front of a lamp (it presses its nose flat on the far side). Dislikes: a lamp shut in behind snow (it walks to the snow block, raps on it once with its mitten, jumping for a block that stands too high, and goes back to its place, where it frowns at the block and waits; it raps again only when a lamp has been let out and shut in anew).
- **The peeper.** A frost child who lies on its front at the edge of the pond, feet kicking, watching the fish. Its one want: to see the fish. Likes: clear ice to look through; splashes (it is soaked and laughs every time); the berry block afloat. Dislikes: a block laid over its view, which it leans round.
- **The sun.** Large, red and drowsy, with a face. It wants to go to bed: it sinks a little and yawns whenever nothing is happening, and comes back up. It is the handle of the day: pulled down it goes gladly, lifted it stretches. By day it hangs to one side of the top centre, and by night its sleeping top half stays showing above the ridge on that same side, so there is always a sun to take hold of. Neither place is under the jam's home control at the top centre, and no block comes to rest there: one let go under it settles on the highest free place below it.
- **The hare.** A snow hare whose tracks cross the far snow. It is a bystander: now and then it comes out from behind a fir, crosses the far snow in uneven bounds with a stop to listen, and goes in behind another fir. Poked, or caught out by the dusk, it bolts behind the nearer fir; it keeps to the day. It wants nothing of the child. It runs on the clock of attended play only, rewards no presence and punishes no absence, moves nothing, and is not saved.
- **The fish and the bird.** Bystanders with nothing to do with the task. The fish is a shadow under the clear ice that darts from a cut and comes to look at anything red. The bird lives in the berry bush and always hops to the highest block whose top is free, other than the block King Winter sits on or tries with his foot and a block right under the jam's home control, and to its bush when there is none.

No character's feeling is ever about the child, about being watched, or about leaving and coming back (pack: game-design, characters-with-opinions.md).

## The scenes

**Dusk: the lamps.** Cause: the child pulls the sun down behind the ridge. About nine seconds.

1. The sun yawns and sinks; the blue ink floods the sky and the snow.
2. The frost children skate out over the pond, one lamp for each hollow, and hang them from left to right; each lamp lights as it is hung. They carry two lamps of their own: with fewer than two hollows, each lamp that has no hollow stands on the snow beside the wall.
3. From each lamp the light crosses the wall and lies on the snow in front: straight through clear ice, soft through cloudy, red where it crossed the berry block, a fan where it crossed an icicle, stopped by snow.
4. King Winter walks in by the widest gap at the foot of the wall that is open to the sky, squeezing himself narrow where it is narrower than he is, or round the end of the bank by his own place when there is no such gap; he never passes through a block, climbs to his seat from behind the wall, polishes a clear block with his beard on the way, and sits on the highest block that will bear him, or in the snow with his grumble.

- **Saved when it starts:** `night: true` and `finished: true`, at once. Nothing else changes: the scene moves no block.
- **Filled in from the state of play:** the hollows and so the number and places of the lamps; every block the light crosses and so the whole pattern; the widest gap; the highest block that will bear him.
- **A touch:** ends the scene at once with every beat landed, lamps hung and lit, the king seated, and is then an ordinary touch. At night the child goes on cutting, carrying and setting, and the light answers each change as it is made. Nothing the scene brings to rest covers a block, a free place on the wall or the pond: the frost children stand on the front snow clear of every lying block, and go on their feet round open water and round whatever lies; the lamp child leaves its place for its two tastes only, walking round the left end of the bank and behind the wall to press its nose on the lowest lit clear block, where it is drawn behind the wall, and to the front of a snow block that shuts a lamp in; a lamp is never a target, and a block set into its hollow takes the place and the lamp goes with the hollow, one of the children's own two then standing on the snow beside the wall while fewer than two hollows are left; a touch on the seated king above the fur at his hem is never taken for the block under him, which is touched from that fur down, and the hat on his head is touched before he is, and a block set on his seat, or over it within his own height, or set higher so that a lamp comes to hang within his own height, makes him stand on it, wait, and go to sit on the highest block that will bear him; his seat pulled from under him, melted or slumped into a snowball under a warm thumb, or fallen because what bore it was taken, drops him onto the snow behind the wall, where he lands sitting with a bump, gets up, and goes to find the highest block that will bear him; a block or a snowball let go with its middle over him as he sits lands on his head and is worn as a hat, and an icicle let go there is no hat: on his seat it goes to the seat, point up, and he rises with his squawk, and over him in the snow it comes down on the bank beside him; nothing else that is taken, set, cut or scooped makes him stir.

**Morning: fresh snow.** Cause: the child lifts the sun back over the ridge. About five seconds.

1. The sun stretches and rises; the paper comes back to the sky and the snow.
2. The frost children skate home with their lamps; King Winter stands, shakes, and walks back to his place.
3. Snow falls, a cap settles on the top of every block that stands in the open, the holes in the pond skin over, the drift fills and the bough grows its icicles.

- **Saved when it starts:** `night: false`, `finished: false`, `holes` emptied, `drift` set to 0, `icicles` emptied, `cap: true` on every block with open sky above it, an icicle frozen into the pond set `lying` beside where it stood, and anything afloat set `lying` on the ice where it floated. All at once.
- **Filled in from the state of play:** which blocks get a cap, and where the king and the children walk back from.
- **A touch:** ends it with every beat landed.

**How a cycle ends and how the next one starts.** A cycle is a day and its night. It ends when the child pulls the sun down, and the lit palace stays as long as the child likes: nothing follows by itself, and nothing counts down. The next cycle starts when the child lifts the sun, which is an act the child performs on a thing in the scene. The palace is still standing in the morning, and a block leaves it only when the child carries it off, drops it in the pond or melts it. On load no scene plays: a clearing left at night is found at night with the lamps lit and the king seated, and one left by day is found by day (pack: game-design, endings-and-short-scenes.md).

## The look

Written after the style spike, not part of the sheet. This part is the art guide.

**Claimed look: two-colour print** (the first reserved row). One sheet of cream paper and two bright inks, a blue and a berry red, which overprint to a third colour, a deep indigo. That is the whole palette:

| Tone | Value | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper | `#f4ead4` | Snow, beards, clouds, light. Whatever is not printed. |
| Blue ink | `#2a9ee6` | The sky, ice, the pond, shade on snow, the frost children. |
| Red ink | `#ff4f72` | The berry block, the sun, noses, hats, mittens, the bird, the king's cushion and crown. |
| Overprint | blue times red, an indigo near `#2a315b` | Fir trees, the king's cloak, open water, eyes and mouths. |
| Blue twice | the blue pulled a second time, `#2891bf` on paper and deeper on ink | The night, and ice lying on ice. |

- **Inks, not paints.** Every shape is a solid flat ink with no black line, no gradient, no gloss and no cast shadow. The red plate sits about two units off register from the blue, so an indigo shape shows a blue sliver on one side and a red one on the other. Ink grain is a fleck pattern of bare paper baked into the still layer and into tiles used as fills; nothing is grained per frame.
- **Ice is ink printed over what is behind it.** A block of ice is laid with `multiply`, so the king's cloak or a fir seen through it turns to the overprint, as a second ink would. Ice over ice prints the blue twice and comes out deeper: that is the overprint, and it is kept. So that a clear block is still seen on the clear middle of the pond, bare paper shows all round it as a thin rim, and its shine is a thin sliver of paper along its top edge that turns the corner and thins out down one end. Which end it turns down, how far it runs and how far it comes down follow from where the block is, so a wall is not a row of one mark. Snow is bare paper with a blue shade along its foot and one side and a thin blue line round the rest, and it hides what is behind it.
- **The night is printed, not lit.** Night is a second pull of the same blue over the whole sheet: paper becomes the blue, the blue becomes blue twice, the indigo deepens. It comes down the sheet behind one hard slanted edge as the sun goes, and lifts the same way. There is no black and no navy ground beyond the overprint the look already has, and no lighting effect of any kind: no glow, no gradient, no blur, no soft edge. A lamp's light is bare paper left out of that second pull, in hard-edged shapes: the lamp's own glass, the ice a lamp reaches, and on the ground in front of the wall the outline of each thing the light crossed. The wall lies there as it stands, row by row outward from the foot of the bank: each thing's own outline, most of a block deep, leaning away from the lamp its light came from, with a dark seam between two rows. Clear ice lies there whole, cloudy ice broken by flecks, what the berry block coloured printed red, an icicle as three uneven shards that start apart and meet nowhere, and a snow block or a snowball as its own outline left in the night's blue, seen by a rim of light on its two far sides only (away from its lamp and away from the wall): an arc, never a closed ring or frame. A block moved at night takes its light with it in the same frame, and a block fresh from the pond comes to lie clear of the light. The characters are left out of the second pull as well, as figures of paper and bright ink on the flooded sheet, so that their faces are read at night.
- **The frame.** A place from edge to edge: a blue sky with paper clouds and a large red sun with a face; a fir bough with five icicles at the top; a ridge of indigo firs with snow on them, in clumps of unequal height; the far snow with drift lines and a hare's tracks in uneven bounds along a wandering line, and now and then the hare that made them, which comes out from behind one fir and goes in behind another (the firs are printed again over it, and over the ring of paper round the sun); King Winter, about a third of the frame high, at the right; the lamp child on the lip of the bank at the left; the peeper lying at the pond's near corner; the drift, the berry bush and the bird at the other side; and the pond across the foot of the frame, deep blue in its clear middle and flecked pale round its cloudy edge, with the fish under it.
- **What stays plain.** The six things and the pond are the plainest things in the frame: a block is one flat blue bar with its shine, or the same bar broken with flecks, and has no face and no idle motion. The snow bank where the palace stands is bare paper on purpose, because it is the surface the child builds on (the exception in pack: game-design, a-full-frame-with-large-funny-characters.md); the setting goes round it.
- **Motion.** Each character moves like itself and shares no curve with another: the king is slow and heavy and sags into sleep; the lamp child swings its legs and tilts its head; the peeper kicks its feet out of step; the sun breathes and droops; the bird is all quick hops; the fish glides and darts; the hare goes in uneven bounds along its own tracks with a stop to listen, and bolts behind the nearer fir when touched or when the dusk comes. The six things only move when the child moves them, and then with weight: a hop out of the pond, a fall, a squash, a wobble that dies away, a roll.
- **How it is drawn.** What does not move (sky, clouds, ridge, firs, far snow, bank, the pond with its cloudy rim, the bush, the grain) is painted once for a size, by day and by night, and is one stamp a frame. Each character's still parts are sprites painted once in three versions (day, night, left out of the night), and only eyes, mouths and a few lines are live. All things at rest are laid as at most six fills, one for each pull of ink, and the things in the hand or in the air as six more. Whoever is behind the wall (King Winter on his way to his seat, the lamp child with its nose on lit ice) is printed between the paper of what stands on the bank and its inks, clipped to the air above the bank less every standing thing, so a block hides him and clear ice shows a face through it. A frame is at most 52 stamps, fills and strokes in every state the tests reach (`frameBudget.test.ts`).
- **The ghost hand** is a frost child's red mitten with a thumb and a blue cuff, in the look's inks.
- **Tiers.** Each tier in `config.ts` sets the pixel ratio and how many snowflakes drift. The lowest tier is still the same print: only the edge of each shape is coarser and fewer flakes fall.

**Who is where.** The front snow is in front of the wall and the bank is behind it. King Winter changes side only at a gate, walks behind the wall and climbs to his seat from behind. The gate is the widest stretch of the bank with nothing of the wall over it at any height, where he makes himself as narrow as the gap. A block that hangs over a gap narrows it by as much as it hangs over, and roofs it only when less than 56 units are left open: he squeezes to 52, a quarter of himself as he is drawn at the wall, cushion and all (203 units), and keeps two units of air each side. Before a gap narrower than that he stops on the side he is on, makes himself as narrow as the gap, and only then steps into it; he is drawn no wider than the gap in every frame he is in it, going in and coming out. A roofed gap is passed over for the next open one, and with none open he goes round the end of the bank by his own place. His seat is a top no higher than the fourth row, with free air over it, 130 units wide and 180 high from the top, which is King Winter seated, crown and all: nothing on the block, no block in that air and no lamp in it. The fourth row is as high as the frame lets him sit: a row higher his crown would be among the bough's icicles and, on his feet, under the home control. A lamp that comes to hang in that air after he has sat down makes him stand, wait and go, as a block set there does. A block or a snowball is a hat only when its middle is let go over him, at his own place by day, on his seat by night, and sat in the snow at his own place by night; by night an icicle is no hat, and on his seat it goes to the seat. Under a hat he dozes, by night as by day, and nods it off within eleven seconds; he takes no hat with him when he gets up to walk. The block he sits on is touched from 12 units above its top, and the hat on his head is touched before he is, so each is 48 px high on a surface 820 px wide. With nothing to bear him he stands on the front snow clear of the highest block and puts one leg out to it, drawn over him so that the whole leg is seen. The lamp child goes round the bank's left end, and at dusk comes in off the pond to the foot of the bank under each lamp's place in turn, from left to right: a lamp is hung, and lights, when the child stands under it. The two frost children go on their feet round open water and whatever lies (`route.ts`). The bird perches on a top that is free and never right under the home control, which is taken as large as it is on the shortest surface the game is held to, 375 px high: 105 stage units wide and 127 deep. Things are touched in a shape at least 70 stage units each way, which is 48 px on a surface 820 px wide; the drawing stays as small as it is.
