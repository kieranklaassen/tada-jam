### How the game meets the quality bar

As built. Nothing here is a measured frame rate, and no physical iPad was measured.

- **Alive at idle.** The fire flickers; the badger breathes, blinks and has a routine of its own; every customer keeps showing its want in its own way (the goat knocks its horns on the post, the bear puffs up and pats its belly, the dachshund stretches long, the crow preens and holds out a wing, the hen's chicks peck round her feet, the duck slaps its flat feet, the mole hugs its basket, the sparrows peck at nothing). All on the attended clock; all stop when the game is unattended or hidden. None asks, beckons or looks at the child.
- **Motion and sound on every touch.** A test presses a grid of points over the whole sheet in three states and holds that every press queues a voice in the same call. The stuff dents under the finger before any time passes. `grid.ts` names a voice for every act on every thing, and `voices.ts` with `voicesCast.ts` holds all of them. Each cell of the sheet's grid is drawn as well: steam, a shimmer, a cloud, smoke and a seed that rolls or hops are small flat-ink wisps (`wisps.ts`, `lookWisps.ts`).
- **Weight, squash and follow-through.** The stuff is a ring of sprung points that keeps its amount; a brick does not give, an airy loaf squashes and springs back, risen dough wobbles. Every figure has its own gait, anticipation and settle (`motion.ts`, `cast.ts`), and tests fail if two reactions are the same curve.
- **Kid-clear.** Cream stuff on a blue peel on a dark bench; gold only for what the oven made and for fire; the customers at the hatch are the largest figures in the opening and those who wait are smaller and behind. Nothing on a working object reads as a face.
- **Wordless clarity for the declared age.** No word, letter, numeral or symbol anywhere, no recipe card, no thought bubble. Each customer looks like the bread it wants and carries a basket that bread fits. A new idea is shown once by the badger on a lump of its own. `npm run -s wordless:check` passes.
- **Wordless guidance.** The whole ladder: after three idle seconds, cut marks grow round the one thing that can be touched next; from five seconds a printed hand shows one possible move with it (a tap, a rub, or a carry to where it could go), alternating between the possible places. It never shows a recipe and knows nothing of what the customer wants. With the hatch empty it marks someone waiting. Any touch ends it; a scene or a reaction is not idleness.
- **No engagement mechanics.** No score, count, streak, timer, praise or reward. A wanted bread is eaten, an unwanted one is handed back unharmed with its reason acted out at the bread. Nothing starts by itself: after an ending the hatch stands empty until the child calls someone in. The position is stored and nothing shows it. A test holds that no voice is a rising major arpeggio.
- **Found as left.** The bakery is saved as it changes, and at once when a scene starts. A test puts the game away in the middle of an ending and opens it again: the hatch is empty, the position has moved, and nothing replays. Rising and baking move only with attended seconds handed in.
- **Nothing passes through anything.** `intersections.test.ts` plays the real controller at 60 frames a second through every state and over every staging of the hatch and the lane, with each budget and its measured worst stated in the file: the stuff stays on the blade (a pulled lobe allowed, capped); it stays over the ledge in the nook and on the sill and inside the oven's mouth; every waiting animal is at least 93% seen and 80% within reach; no touch on one animal is answered by another; a held thing stays in the opening and off the badger's head; a bread fits its place on the rack; the badger's lump never lies in the child's load; every touch target is at least 48 logical pixels at 1180 by 820 and at 1024 by 768. Its first run found seven faults, all mended in the game.

### The learning claim

As the sheet has it. Standings and check states were read through the lookup on 2026-10-03 and must be read again on the day of the pull request.

Bread Day is designed from four of California's preschool and transitional kindergarten learning foundations (foundations published by a state department, not standards; each confirmed), which reach to age five and a half, and from four fase 1 goals of the Dutch curriculum institute (guidance, not law; each confirmed). What it takes from them is this and no more. From both, each in its own records: exploring what materials are like. From the California foundations alone: finding out what an act does by trying it, seeing that a material has changed after something is done to it, and keeping up to three wants in mind through a task of several steps while the customer goes on showing them. From the Dutch guidance alone: that food is usually prepared before it is eaten, heat as something to discover and wonder about, and putting acts in an order of time by doing them. That dough rises, and why, is in none of the eight records named here, and neither are mixing and baking as such: the game shows them as changes a child can see. For a child older than five and a half the game is designed from the Dutch guidance alone, and the parts taken from California alone are then the game's own choice.

### Defaults taken for the owner

From the guide, each kept as written:

- No symbol, numeral, letter or word on the kid side (the band starts at 4), and no `symbols.ts`.
- No reading on the object: how well a bread came out shows only in how it looks, lands and is eaten.
- No camera shake and no impact pause. A brick's thunk is carried by the peel jumping, the flour hopping and the sound.
- No speech. Every creature voice is invented and synthesized.
- The demo keeps its fantasy and its feel in the hand (kneading dough with a finger), and its verb is kept too, since it was already the skill.

From the game's own sheet:

- The cast is animals, with a badger as the baker. The demo had a human family with no tastes of their own.
- A jar of bubbly starter was added, because flour and water alone do not rise and the model has to be true.
- A bread darkens the moment it goes back into the oven, with no second wait.
- Rising takes 6 attended seconds in the nook and baking 3.5, both in `stuff.ts`. They are a first guess: nobody has tuned them with a child or on a device.
- A first visit starts at `dough` under age 6 or with no age, and at `shapes` from 6.
- A touch on a customer moves it: the one at the hatch steps back to the lane, one in the lane steps up. So a poke of a customer is answered by its walk, not by a reaction in place.
- The peel is carried by its handle; by its blade it is carried only when the finger is not heading for the stuff, since a push inwards from beside the stuff gathers it.
- A give is something only a carried thing does: a bread in the hand, or the peel carried by the finger, to the hatch or up in front of the badger. A rub or a pull that ends in front of the badger feeds it nothing: the stuff springs back and settles. The one exception is kept on purpose: raw stuff dragged from the peel all the way to the hatch is handed to the customer, who hands it back unharmed.
- Put away, or with the touch taken by the browser, a thing in the hand goes back where it came from: nothing is dropped where the finger happened to be.
- The dough showing stops as the oven door shuts, and the showings for seeds and for batter leave their lump unbaked: whatever comes out of the oven is some customer's bread, and the sheet lets no showing end on one.

### What the next builder should know

- The toy was built by three writers at once, each on files of its own (`voices.ts`; `motion.ts`; `doughBody.ts` with `lookStuff.ts`) against signatures written down first, while the Mount and the toy's controller (since grown into `game.ts`) were written against the same signatures. It fitted at the first try: sixteen of seventeen toy tests passed before anything had been seen on screen. What tests could not see was all on screen: the stuff at half size, two dots that read as eyes, a hand that read as a rude gesture. Take stills of the whole toy as soon as it runs, before the parts are polished alone.
- Anything with two similar marks side by side at one height is a face to a four-year-old. Check every working object for it.
- The game was built the same way as the toy, with more writers: the room, six painters' figures, the customers' motion, the voices and the breads were each written against numbers and signatures fixed first (`stage.ts` for where everything stands), while the controller was written against the same. Random play through the controller then found a fault in the rules that their own tests had not: froth tipped full of flour kept its air.
- Keep one module of plain numbers for where everything stands, shared by the controller and the look. Without it the hit boxes and the drawing drift apart at the first layout change, and this game changed its layout three times in an afternoon.
- Have the folder read against the sheet by someone who has not seen the build before calling it done. The first such read here found thirteen promises unkept that eight passes of stills and seven hundred tests had not: a first visit that was never saved, a put-away that finished a drag, a showing that gave the answer away. The reader asks of every sentence "where is this drawn, and where is it sounded", which is not a question the builder asks of its own sheet.
- A scene that draws something where the saved state says it is not (the peel at the hatch with seeds on it, while the bakery's peel is home and empty) needs one switch for the view and one place that turns it off, called from the scene's last beat, from a touch that ends the scene, and from nowhere else. Here that is `home` in `game.ts`.
- Where the rules can lay the same customer out again at once, let it finish leaving first.
- Write the overlap tests before the passes, not after: they found seven faults that five rounds of stills had not, because stills show the states one thought to stage and the test stages them all (7,358 of them here).
- The first stills of a crowd are the ones to take early: one customer at the hatch said nothing about what three do to a 470-wide opening.
- The rules were written before the toy, as six pure modules: `stuff.ts` (the material), `tastes.ts` (the customers), `bakery.ts` (the world and the cycle), `save.ts` (the saved state), `grid.ts` (the cue for every thing and act) and `consequence.ts` (reactions, and where a mend lies). Each has its test beside it, and none imports a renderer, the DOM or a clock.
- Every act returns `{ bakery, happened }`. The view plays `happened`; it never needs to compare two states to know what to animate.
- Finding the breads the rules can make by running the rules (`reachableBreads`, `canStillBecome`) caught two design faults that reading did not: a full peel could never become batter, and a pair of customers could ask for a bread nothing can make. Both are now held by tests.
- A search over the rules is cheap here (a few milliseconds) only because every number in the model is coarse. Keep rise and bake out of any search key that must stay small.
- A test helper that picks "a bread the customer wants" must leave out the secrets, or the hen is served loose seeds for ever and the order never moves.
