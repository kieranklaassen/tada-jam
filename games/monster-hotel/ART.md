# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

## The band and its age rule

- **Band.** The manifest says 9 to 12. The design is held to a nine-year-old.
- **The cue-table row.** The row for 7 and up governs. Its "Avoid" column is taken as a hard limit: no written word or letter, no symbol that stands alone so that play depends on reading it, no timer, points or verdict chrome, and no long chain of hints. Several things may be open at once as long as each reads at a glance.
- **The pack's rule for the age** (pack: game-design, ages-9-to-12.md). The hotel is a real system that behaves the same way every time: noise goes through walls, warmth rises, cold sinks, a smell drifts along a corridor. Any arrangement that leaves every guest content stands, and there are always several. A wrong arrangement is large, funny and free. The humour is dry and is not explained. Nothing is stored as a best, and nothing is compared. Help is something the child fetches: touching a guest shows what reaches it and where from. The idle ladder shows what can be touched, or one possible move such as a guest lifted a little and put back, and never an arrangement that settles the house. A house that is too hard has a way back in: carrying a guest to the coach sends the lot away, and the next coach-load comes from one place earlier in the order where there is one.
- **Symbols.** The band starts at 9, so numerals and mathematics signs are allowed, each laid on or beside the quantity it stands for and drawn only in `symbols.ts`. This game lays numerals in one place: beside the flames on the dial of the stove and beside the icicles on the dial of the ice box, where the numeral names the step the child has just set. The flames and the icicles say the same thing without it, so play never depends on reading a numeral. No letter and no word is drawn anywhere. The numerals on the two dials are drawn through the game's own `symbols.ts`, the copy of the template's module, and nowhere else.
- **Guided discovery** (pack: game-design, guided-discovery.md). The band lies across the rule's line at ten. The game takes the older form for every child: the child tries first, and once at each place the porter shows one neat way with that place's new thing, on a settled day, after the child's own arrangement has settled the hotel, whether or not the child used the new thing, and unless that arrangement is already the neat way.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only where a first visit starts in the designed order: the first place, `two-guests`, for a child of 10 or younger, and the second, `heat-and-snow`, for a child of 11 or older. No age (`null`) starts at the first place. A saved place always wins over the age. Every guest, room and thing can be reached by play at every age, and nothing is hidden or locked by age.

## The toy

**Touch a guest, and the whole page is drawn again from where that guest stands.**

- **What the finger does.** It lands on a monster. In the same frame the monster squashes and gives its own grunt, and the redrawing starts at its feet and sweeps outward across the hotel in about a third of a second. The finger does not have to lift for any of this.
- **What the page becomes.** The same hotel, as that guest takes it. Its own room is drawn large and in full ink, and the rest of the house goes to faint pencil. A guest with no room yet has none to draw large: the room it asks for keeps its ink, at its own size. Whatever reaches its room is inked by how this guest takes it: a thing it loves in the one spot colour with curls and flourishes, a thing it minds in heavy black scribble that shows the wall or floor it came through, and everything it does not care about in faint pencil. Each guest has its own hand: the bat hangs from its ceiling, so its page is upside down; the yeti's lines drip wherever it is warm; the blob with many eyes sees the page several times over, slightly apart.
- **What the ear gets.** While the page is one guest's, the hotel is heard as that guest hears it. The tuba next door is a round tune from the troll's own place and a flat blare from the place of the blob trying to sleep. Each guest's grunt is its own, with several variants that follow where and how fast the finger landed.
- **Back and across.** Touching another guest sweeps the page straight to that one's view. Touching the same guest again, or the paper margin, sweeps it back to the plain page.
- **Carrying is looking.** A finger that lands on a guest and moves picks the guest up, and for as long as it is carried the page stays that guest's, so the child sees what would reach it in each room it is held over: that room keeps its ink, at its own size, with nothing enlarged under the finger. Setting it down returns the plain page. A carried guest dangles, stiff and unbothered, with its luggage swinging behind.
- **Why it is a pleasure with no goal.** In a scene with one hotel, two guests and nothing to settle, flipping the page between two creatures who take the same building in opposite ways is funny on every touch: the building turns over, swells, drips and changes its tune. Nothing can go wrong, random touches always redraw something, and a person watching can tell in three seconds that the child is looking through the monsters' eyes. Everything else in the scene answers a touch too: a wall knocks and every guest looks toward the knock, a room's lamp swings, the bell on the porter's trolley rings.
- **In the first build of the toy** there is the hotel, two guests and the day-and-night wheel on the roof, and no arrangement is asked for.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

Six things the child handles, five things done with each. Every cell is a different sight and sound, and none is refused. "Wrong" uses are marked ✗: each works, changes the hotel truly, and is at least as funny as the right one.

| | set down in a room | given to a guest | fixed to a wall or a floor | tapped | left through a day and a night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **a guest** | moves in: the bag thuds, the bedsprings squeak as the bed is tested, and from now on what it gives off starts from here | in a room with a spare bed the two share it, with a double creak of springs; otherwise they swap rooms, passing in the corridor without a glance, to two sets of footsteps | ✗ sticks half through the plaster with a crunch while both neighbours stare, then steps out into the nearer room with a pop | the page is drawn again from where it stands, to its own grunt (the toy) | awake it does its one thing, asleep it sleeps in its own way, each with its own sound (a tune from the tuba, a snore that is each sleeper's own); what it gives off reaches the others only at its own hours |
| **the quilt** | ✗ lies on the bed with a soft flump and stops nothing; whoever sleeps there tucks in | ✗ wraps the guest from head to foot with a long rustle: a wrapped guest makes no noise, which a sleeper next door likes and a musician minds | hangs padded, pressed on with four soft thumps: no noise, warmth or cold crosses that wall or floor, and what was heard through it goes dull | puffs feathers with a whumpf, and the nearest guest sneezes | stays, and the marks of what it is stopping at that hour bunch up against it with a dull patter; the bunch is drawn from the arrangement and the hour and does not grow with time |
| **the pipe** | ✗ stands in the corner like a hat stand with a hollow clang and carries nothing; a guest peers down it and hears its own breath come back | ✗ becomes a trumpet with a honk: whatever that guest gives off carries one room further | joins the two rooms with a turn of the last bolt and a gurgle: smell, warmth and cold pass through it both ways | toots a puff of whatever is passing through | carries what is being made at that hour with a low whoosh, and nothing at the other, when it is quiet |
| **the stove** | lands with an iron clunk, pings as it heats and warms the room by its dial; the warmth rises to the room above, one step weaker with each floor | goes into that guest's room, and the guest takes to it in its own way and with its own sound: one hugs it and purrs, one sits on it and sags with a long wheeze | ✗ scorches a patch of wallpaper with a sizzle, and the patch fades as the stove slides into the nearer room | the dial turns a step with a clack and the fire roars up or down to match: one flame, two, three, then one again | burns the same by day and by night, with a steady low rumble |
| **the ice box** | lands with a slosh, settles with a clatter of ice and chills the room by its dial; the cold sinks to the room below, one step weaker with each floor | goes into that guest's room: one uses it as an armchair and the lid squeals, one goes stiff as a plank with a single knock | ✗ frosts a patch of wall with a spreading crackle, and the patch melts as the ice box slides into the nearer room | the dial turns a step with a glassy chink: one icicle, two, three, then one again | chills the same by day and by night, with a steady hum and a slow drip |
| **the alarm clock** | ✗ stands by the bed, ticking, and gives a short trill at dawn and at dusk; a sleeper opens one eye, glares and sleeps on | a guest who will change its hours keeps it, winds it with a whirr and swaps its day for its night; one who will not stops its bell in its own way, with a muffled clonk, and keeps its hours | ✗ hangs as a wall clock with a slow loud tock and changes nothing | rings its full bell, and every sleeper in the house opens one eye | a guest who holds it and will change its hours wakes and sleeps the other way round, with a yawn at each turn of the wheel; a guest who will not sleeps and wakes at its own hours |

Beside the six: the day-and-night wheel on the roof, which the child turns by hand and which turns nothing by itself, and the coach at the kerb.

**How each thing travels** is the hotel's whole rule language, and it never changes:

- Noise goes through every wall, floor and ceiling of the room it is made in, and loses a step each time.
- Warmth rises through ceilings and cold sinks through floors, each losing a step a floor. Warmth and cold in one room add up.
- A smell drifts sideways along its own floor and loses a step a room. It does not cross a floor.
- A quilt on a wall or floor stops noise, warmth and cold there. A pipe lets smell, warmth and cold through there.

**On day 15** the child knows the eight guests' tastes by heart and places from knowledge; has found pairings that turn a nuisance into a treat (the fly beside the cook, the cook humming to the tuba through the wall); pipes a smell or a warmth across the house to the one guest who wants it; lets a day sleeper and a night sleeper share a room with two beds, each awake while the other sleeps; has met the guest who is content only when somebody hears her; and builds, on purpose, the hotel in which everyone is as cross as can be. Some pairings always set off a small scene of their own (the yeti and the stove at three flames; the tuba and the singer through one wall). They are never hinted at, counted or listed.

## The representation

- **The idea.** People take the same thing differently because of who they are; a quarrel comes from wants that collide; and it is settled when the worry behind each side's demand is met, which is not always what either side first asked for.
- **How it appears in the objects.** Two views of one scene, extended to as many views as there are guests. The hotel is one building and one set of facts: this noise goes through this wall. Each guest's page draws those same facts in its own way. The tuba's noise is one set of marks crossing one wall; from the troll's place the marks are a flourish, from the sleeper's place a scribble, from the yeti's place faint pencil. The difference between two guests is therefore something the child sees by changing places, with nothing said.
- **Demand and worry are two different things in the world.** A guest's demand is a room: in the lobby it stands with its bag and stares at the door it wants, and two guests often stare at the same door. Its worry is what must or must not reach it: cold, quiet while it sleeps, an open nose. The demand is visible on the plain page; the worry is visible only from the guest's own place. An arrangement settles the hotel when every worry is met, whichever doors were asked for.
- **Why this shape.** A want is drawn as something that physically travels and arrives, so "it bothers me" always has a place and a path, and the child can change one thing on that path. Feelings are read from what a creature does (it turns to the wall the trouble comes through, with a pillow on its head), never from a row of faces to choose from.
- **Evidence.** The table in `research/learning-games-that-work.md` of the game-design pack gives, for another's perspective, "two viewpoints on one scene" with the mechanic of swapping seats and giving each what that one would want, and names ages 4 to 6 for it. The studies behind it trained children of about five. Using it at 9 to 12, with many viewpoints and with wants that collide, is this game's own extension and has no trial behind it. The pack also says the evidence in this strand is thin and that such a game is rehearsal, with the real learning off screen. The sheet claims no more.
- **Object, picture, symbol.** The order stops at the picture. A feeling or a point of view has no school symbol, and none is invented: no face icons, no hearts, no meters. The only symbols in the game are the numerals on the two dials, laid beside the flames and icicles they count, and they belong to the stove and the ice box, not to anyone's feelings.

## The four mechanic questions

- **Swap.** No. What the child does is look from one guest's place and then another's and arrange the house so that what each minds does not reach it. Put sums or spelling in place of the guests' wants and no game is left.
- **Attention.** At the moment of setting a guest or a thing down, the child must look at what reaches which room and think about how the guest in that room takes it, which is different from how the guest next door takes it and from how the child would.
- **Fun.** The skill is used in the two most enjoyable moments: the redraw when the finger lands on a guest, and the hotel's reaction, room by room, to a new arrangement. Play never stops for a question.
- **Guess.** In the first two places of the order, often yes, and that is meant: with two or three guests in four rooms, between one way in eight and every way of giving out the rooms settles the house. From the third place on at most one way in eight settles it with nothing placed, and once things can be placed a house has from about two hundred to many thousands of arrangements. Trying them blind is far slower than looking, and each wrong one shows where and why. Trying is never refused or punished, so it stays a legitimate way to play. A test holds both shares for every cast.

## The error as a consequence

There is no wrong move, only an arrangement that leaves somebody cross. The hotel runs every arrangement truly and shows what it does.

- **What it does.** A guest reached by something it minds shows it at once, in its own room and in its own way: the blob drags the pillow over all of its eyes, the lizard goes grey and stiff, the yeti sags into the shape of a puddle, the bat wraps itself tighter.
- **Where.** The guest turns to the wall, floor or ceiling the trouble comes through. On the plain page the marks of that air are drawn crossing it. In the first three places of the order they are drawn the whole way from the one who makes them; from the fourth place on, the plain page shows only the last crossing, and the whole path is seen from the guest's own place. So feedback is fullest while the game is new and thins afterwards.
- **Why.** From the cross guest's place the page inks the path in heavy black, back through each wall to the guest or thing it starts from, at the hour it is made. Turning the wheel shows whether it is a day trouble or a night trouble.
- **The state stays.** Nothing is undone, nobody storms out, nothing is taken away and nothing resets. The child changes one thing and the house answers again.
- **It is about the thing.** A cross guest glares at the wall and never at the child. Being cross is as good to watch as being content, and a house full of cross guests is the funniest house.
- **Success is a consequence too.** A content guest simply does its one thing undisturbed: the tuba is played, the stew is stirred, the bat sleeps.
- **No verdict.** No tick, cross or buzzer, no praise, no meter of contentment, no count of content guests, and no face turned to the child.

## The designed order, and what is stored

**The order.** Ten places, one new thing at each and then combinations. The ids below are the ones in `LADDER` in `config.ts`. They name what is in the house and never a grade, a groep or a level.

| Place | Id | New here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first | `two-guests` | a house of four rooms; two or three guests; noise, and who sleeps when |
| second | `heat-and-snow` | warmth that rises and cold that sinks: the boiler under one column, the snow hole over another, the lizard and the yeti |
| third | `quilt` | the first thing to place: the quilt |
| fourth | `corridor` | the long house of two floors by three rooms; a smell along a corridor; the cook and the fly |
| fifth | `stove-and-ice` | warmth and cold of the child's own making, with the dials |
| sixth | `alarm-clock` | time as something that can be asked for: guests who will change their hours and guests who will not |
| seventh | `tower-and-pipe` | the tower of three floors by two rooms, and the pipe |
| eighth | `twin-rooms` | more guests than rooms, and rooms with two beds |
| ninth | `listener` | the singer, who is content only if someone awake hears her and does not mind |
| tenth | `full-house` | everything at once: five guests and three things |

- Each place has three **casts**, written by hand in `casts.ts`: the house and what is fixed in it, the guests, the things in the cupboard, and one guest on the bench outside. A test solves every cast and fails unless at least two different ways of giving out the rooms settle it, so "there are several" is checked and not hoped for.
- **The harder option looks harder and is chosen.** On the bench outside sits one more guest with a mountain of luggage, plainly awkward, and sometimes one the child has not met in a coach yet. The child may carry it in at any moment and carry it out again. It is never needed, every house keeps a bed spare for it, and a test holds that the house can still be settled with it in.
- **A cycle** runs from a coach-load arriving to the hotel being settled: every guest in the house content by day and by night.
- **Goes well:** settled within three set-downs for each guest in the house. Only set-downs that change the arrangement are counted. Looking from a guest's place, tapping and turning the wheel are free, so looking more is never held against the child. **Mixed:** settled with more set-downs. **Badly:** the child carried a guest out to the coach before the house was settled, which sends this lot away. A visit put away before either leaves the place where it was. The count is kept only to judge the cycle and is shown nowhere.
- **Which customer a new position lays out.** The coach waits with its blinds drawn, and who is in it is decided when the child touches it, from the place as it stands then. So a place that moved when the last cycle was judged shows on the very next coach-load, and nobody who was already visible is changed.
- Nothing shows the place: no number, no label, no map, and no sign that the next house is easier.

**What is stored**, every field, as plain JSON through `ctx.storage`:

| Field | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | the version of the shape |
| `position` | the id of the child's place in the order |
| `finished` | the cycle on screen has been judged; nothing replays on load |
| `cast` | the id of the cast on screen |
| `round` | how many coach-loads have begun, wrapped at 10,000; it only rotates the casts of a place |
| `at` | for each guest of the cast, where it is: a room, the lobby, the bench, or gone with the coach |
| `kit` | for each thing of the cast, where it is (the cupboard, a room, a wall or floor, a guest) and, for the stove and the ice box, its dial from 1 to 3 |
| `phase` | day or night, as the wheel was left |
| `from` | the guest whose place the page is drawn from, or none |
| `moves` | the set-downs of this cycle that changed the arrangement, capped at 999 |
| `shown` | the ids of the places whose neat way the porter has already shown |

A thing or a guest in the hand is saved where it came from. The marks of noise, warmth and smell, every guest's mood, and the hour's goings-on are worked out from these fields and are never stored. A largest legal state is under 2 KB, and a test holds it under half of the 64 KB cap.

**The numerals in the rules.** The dial of the stove and of the ice box is a whole number from 1 to 3 in `kit`. A pure function lists every numeral the page may draw as a value and the thing it lies on, and it lists the two dials only.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Eight guests. Each has one want that can be seen from across the room, and tastes that never change, so a child can learn them and test them on purpose. "Minds" and "must have" make a guest cross; "loves" makes a content guest plainly happier and is never needed.

| Guest | The want you can see | Awake | Gives off | Must have | Minds | Loves | Will change its hours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **the troll with the tuba** | to play all night; it carries the tuba everywhere with its lips already pursed | night | noise, two rooms' worth, while awake | to be free to play (not wrapped in the quilt) | nothing: it sleeps like a log | | yes |
| **the bat** | to sleep all day in the cool, hanging; the eye mask is already on its forehead | night | nothing | a room that is not warm | noise while it sleeps | | never |
| **the blob in pyjamas** | to sleep all night in peace; it clutches its pillow | day | nothing | a room neither warm nor cold | noise while it sleeps; a smell at any hour | being wrapped in the quilt | yes |
| **the yeti** | to be cold; it leads its own snow cloud on a string | day | cold, always, which sinks | a cold room | nothing else | | yes |
| **the lizard in a scarf** | to be warm; it shivers and hugs a hot-water bottle | day | nothing | a warm room | noise while it sleeps | | never |
| **the cook** | to stew all day; the cauldron is on its back and the spoon in its fist | day | a smell while awake; a little warmth always | a room that is not cold, or the stew will not simmer | nothing | noise, which it hums along to | never |
| **the fly** | to smell something rich; its nose is in the air and its napkin tucked in | day | a small buzz while awake, heard only in its own room | a room that is not cold | nothing | a smell | yes |
| **the singer** | to be heard; she is a ghost with her mouth open and her music held out | night | noise, three rooms' worth, while awake; a little cold always | someone awake within reach of her voice who does not mind it | a smell at any hour; a warm room | | never |

- **Who else is in the scene.** The porter, an old tortoise with a luggage trolley, carries things and shows the neat way; the coach driver waits. Neither has an opinion of the child or of an arrangement.
- **Feelings are about the house.** A guest is cross with a wall, a smell or a neighbour's tuba. No guest is ever disappointed in the child, thanks or praises the child, hurries the child, or notices the child leaving or coming back. A guest left in the lobby waits beside its bag for as long as it takes and does not complain.
- **A joke is never cruel.** The guest a wrong use lands on is bewildered and never hurt: the lizard that goes stiff as a plank thaws as soon as it is warm.

## The scenes

Every scene is a list of timed beats on the attended clock, filled in from the house as it stands. Its outcome is saved when it starts, any touch ends it with everything at its end state, and none plays on load.

1. **The settled day** (the ending, 8 to 10 seconds). Cause: the finger lifts from the set-down, or from the turn of a dial, that leaves every guest in the house content by day and by night. Beats: the page shows one night and one day go by; at night each night guest does its one thing in exactly the room the child gave it, by day the others do; each love plays (the fly leans into the smell coming through the wall, the cook hums to the tuba); every sleeper sleeps through; the wheel comes to rest where the child had left it. Filled in from: who is in which room, what is fixed where, and who loves what reaches them. Saved at the start: `at`, `kit` and `moves` as the set-down or the turn of the dial leaves them, `finished` as true, and `position` as the judgement moves it. `phase` and `from` are not changed: the night and the day that go by are a view.
2. **The porter's neat way** (6 to 9 seconds, joined to the settled day as one scene). Cause: a settled day of a cast whose place is not yet in `shown`, whether or not the child has used the place's new thing, when the child's arrangement is not the cast's neat one, which uses it. The place is the one the cast on screen belongs to, not `position`, which the settled day has by then saved as the judgement moves it. The neat one settles the house with the new thing of that place put to use and with nothing placed idly. Beats: the porter trundles in and rearranges the house into it, the guests take it in their own ways and, where the cast allows it, one of them is plainly happier than before; a held moment; the porter puts everything back exactly as the child had it. It is a second arrangement beside the child's own, shown after the child's has already stood, and never a correction. It is shown once for each place and changes nothing that is saved except the mark in `shown`. Saved when the porter comes in: the id of the place the cast on screen belongs to, added to `shown`, and nothing else. A settled day whose arrangement is already the neat one, or that a touch or a put-away ends before the porter comes in, adds nothing to `shown`, and the porter comes on the next settled day of a cast of that place. Filled in from: the child's arrangement, which the porter's differs from and returns to, and the cast's neat way.
3. **The coach changes over** (about 6 seconds). Cause: the child touches the waiting coach after a cycle has been judged. Beats: the door opens; the old guests file out with their bags past the new ones without a glance; the porter trundles the things back to the cupboard; each new guest plants its bag in the lobby and turns to stare at the door it wants; the coach pulls away and the next one pulls up with its blinds drawn. Filled in from: who leaves, who arrives, and which doors they stare at. Setting a guest down on the coach after the cycle has been judged is this same touch. Saved at the start: `cast` and `round` for the new coach-load, `at` with each new guest in the lobby and the spare guest on the bench, `kit` with each thing of the new cast in the cupboard and each dial as the cast gives it, `finished` as false, `moves` as 0 and `from` as none. `position` and `phase` are not changed. The old guests filing out are a view and are in no field.
4. **Sent away** (about 5 seconds). Cause: the child sets a guest down on the coach before the cycle has been judged. Beats: the rest follow it out in a line, unbothered; the things go back to the cupboard; the coach pulls away and the next pulls up and waits. Saved at the start: `at` with every guest of the cast gone with the coach, `kit` with every thing in the cupboard, `finished` as true, `position` as the judgement moves it, and `from` as none. `phase` is not changed. Filled in from: who was in the house, in the lobby and on the bench; the guest on the bench joins the line.
5. **Pairings** (about 4 seconds each). Cause: one exact combination, every time it is made, such as the yeti in a room with the stove at three flames, or the troll and the singer on two sides of one wall at night. Beats: the two do one absurd thing together. Never hinted at, counted or listed. Saved at the start: nothing. The set-down, the turn of a dial or the turn of the wheel that made the combination is already in `at`, `kit` or `phase`, and the scene changes no field. Filled in from: which two meet, in which rooms and at which hour.

**How a cycle ends and the next starts.** A settled house stays as long as the child likes and can be played with freely; it is not judged twice. If the child does nothing, nothing new starts: no countdown and no next round. The next coach-load is visible and waiting at the kerb, blinds drawn, and comes in only on the child's touch. Nobody who waits complains, hurries the child or refers to the child leaving or coming back. On the very first visit two guests already stand in the lobby staring at the same door, and a coach waits behind them. On load the house is as it was left: the same guests in the same rooms at the same hour, the page drawn from the same place, and the coach waiting.

## The records

Read from the pack with the lookup on 2026-10-03, one record at a time by its id. What each record asks is given in the pack's own Summary or in this game's words. No official wording of either jurisdiction is in this file.

### us-ca

Levels, as the lookup prints them for ages 9 to 12: `grade-4` at 9 and 10, `grade-5` at 10 and 11, `grade-6` at 11 and 12. Age mapping: derived. Beside every level the lookup returns the `cross-grade` lane, labelled cross-grade: its statements hold for every grade, not for this age in particular.
Gaps, as printed: at 9, "Grade 3 is not in the pack. A third grader turns nine during the year; grade 4 starts at nine." At 12, "Grade 7 is not in the pack. A sixth grader turns twelve during the year; a child who starts the school year at twelve is in grade 7."
No record is named in `grade-5`, so for a child in grade 5 the game rests on the cross-grade records below only.

- `edu.us-ca.grade-4.practical-life-feelings.objective.4-4-2-s` (`us-ca 4.4.2.S`, Injury, Prevention, and Safety), level `grade-4`: state-board-adopted-standard, confirmed. Summary: the child tries out, together with other people, ways of settling a disagreement that work.
  Limits taken: it asks for practice with other people, so talking about the steps alone does not meet it. The game is not that practice: the child settles the quarrels of invented guests on a screen, with no other person. The record shapes what a settlement is here (a way that works) and nothing more. ### Where the two differ

- **What is asked at 9 and 10.** The California grade 4 record asks for trying out ways of settling a disagreement together with other people. The Dutch fase 2 records ask for taking others' feelings and wishes into account, and for talking about where a conflict comes from and which solutions are possible. The game follows nl for what the child does at that age: see what each side wants and minds, and find possible solutions. It does not follow the California record there, since arranging monsters is not practice with people. It also stops short of the Dutch records, which ask for talk and for words.
- **Solutions that suit every side.** Of the nl records named here, the fase 3 one (`nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase3`), for ages 10 to 12, names this, and the fase 2 one (`nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase2`) asks only which solutions are possible. Of the us-ca records named here, the outcome for all involved is in voluntary guidance tied to no grade (`us-ca 5.F.2`). The game lets only an arrangement that suits every guest settle the house, at every place in its order and at every age. On this point it follows us-ca, whose guidance ties the idea to no grade; for a child of 9 that goes beyond what the nl fase 2 records named here ask for.
- **Whose conflict.** Neither adopted California record says in its Limits whose conflict is meant; the grade 4 record asks only that the practice is with other people. The Dutch fase 3 record leaves open whether the child is a party or helps others. Here the child is a go-between and never a party, which is the game's own choice inside what the nl fase 3 record leaves open. Nothing in the play depends on a difference between the two on this point.
- **Standing.** Of the records named here, California's two adopted standards, for grades 4 and 6, are both on conflict, and its three competencies are voluntary guidance: one on taking another's point of view (`us-ca 3.B.2`), one on the outcome for all involved (`us-ca 5.F.2`) and one on an answer both sides can accept (`us-ca 4.E.3`), so on conflict the game is designed from voluntary guidance as well as from the two standards. The Dutch records named here are curriculum-institute guidance and a draft that is not in force, for both parts; no Dutch core goal in force is named here for either part. The two sets are listed apart and nothing in one is taken to stand for anything in the other. Nothing in the play depends on this difference: it shows only in the words of the claim.
- **"Fair."** No record named here, in either jurisdiction, defines fair as equal shares, and the game does not use the idea. A settlement is an arrangement every side can accept. The two do not differ here, and nothing in the play depends on it.

### The claim

Monster Hotel is designed from two content standards adopted by the California State Board of Education (`us-ca 4.4.2.S` for grade 4 and `us-ca 6.4.4.M` for grade 6), neither of which the game carries out: the first asks for practice with other people and the second asks the child to show the use of the steps for resolving a conflict, and the game has only invented guests and keeps no record of what a child shows; from three of California's Transformative Social and Emotional Learning competencies, which are voluntary guidance tied to no grade (`us-ca 3.B.2`, in part, and `us-ca 5.F.2`, Late Elementary, and `us-ca 4.E.3`, in part, Middle School); from five fase goals of the Dutch curriculum institute, which are guidance on what a school can offer and not law (`nl ojw/ja/1/07/fase2`, `nl ojw/ja/1/06/fase2` in part, `nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase2` in part, `nl ojw/ja/1/06/fase3` and `nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase3` in part); and from two items of the Dutch draft core goals for 2027, a draft not in force (`nl 20 A d`, in part, and `nl 20 B e`). Every one of the twelve records is about other people or about talking with them, and in that place the game offers only invented guests, monsters in a pretend hotel: the child deals with no person and talks with nobody in the game. Of the two standards the game takes only what a settlement is, a way that works, and that steps are used and not described. Of `us-ca 3.B.2` it takes trying to see a thing from another's side, and not the naming of what makes people take it differently; of `us-ca 4.E.3` the answer both sides can accept that meets the worry behind each demand, and not the working on it with peers and adults; of `nl ojw/ja/1/06/fase2` that a conflict comes from wants that pull against each other, shown and not discussed; of `nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase2` how a conflict arose and which solutions are possible, shown and not put into words, and of `nl ojw/ja/3/08/fase3` the solutions that suit every party, and of neither the child's own surroundings; and of `nl 20 A d` dealing with conflicts, in pretend, and not dialogue or treating people as equals. All twelve records were `confirmed` when read on 2026-10-03. The game says nothing about what a child has reached, practised or can do, and keeps no record of it.

## The look

Pen-and-ink crosshatch, the first look reserved for this game, on canvas 2D. A hotel drawn like a page from an old book of odd stories: black pen on cream paper, a cut-away front with a monster in every room, and one printed second colour. It was spiked on the game's real scene (the long house on a full night, six guests in rooms, one in the lobby and one on the bench), and that scene is a true state of the rules: `page.test.ts` builds it from them. The spike is still reachable with `demo=spike` in the address.

- **Black pen only.** Every tone is hatching, never a grey fill: light single hatch, medium, crosshatch and dense crosshatch, as pattern tiles built once from a seeded generator and clipped to shapes. The roof, the foundations, the night sky and the cut edges of walls and floors are dense; room interiors stay light, so a guest reads against its room at a glance.
- **A trembling line.** No ruler line anywhere. Every outline is a polyline with small seeded offsets, and a moving figure switches between two drawings of itself about six times a second, so its line boils a little and a still thing stays still.
- **One spot colour, vermilion, flat.** No gradient and no wash. On the plain page it is only on what can be touched: each guest's body or its one signature thing (the tuba, the striped pyjamas, the lizard's head, the cook's apron), the five things in the cupboard, the day-and-night wheel, the coach door.
- **Stiff, deadpan figures.** Upright, small blank eyes, no smiles. Each guest is its own silhouette with its want in view: the troll behind its tuba, the blob under its pillow, the yeti with its cloud on a string, the bat with its eye mask up. In the lobby each has its bag stood at its feet. Tools look like tools. Nothing is pastel or rounded for cuteness.
- **Airs are marks.** Noise is jagged zigzags that leave the tuba's bell and cross the walls, thinner once across; every mark has six teeth or more, finer where it is short, since three teeth alone would read as a letter and a toothless bow as a bar. The airs are laid over the figures, so what reaches a lodger is never hidden behind it; through a floor or a ceiling a noise crosses on the open side of the room, clear of the bed. Warmth is rising wavy lines, cold is hanging icicle strokes and fine falling ones. They are drawn where the rules say they travel. An air is also kept in the one room it is made in, where it has crossed nothing: the fly's small buzz is short zigzags to either side of it and goes no further.
- **No sign by accident.** A shut eye is a lid, a thin filled crescent, and never a bent stroke; a curl has five loops or more; no two strokes cross at right angles by themselves anywhere on the page: the night's stars are round specks of bare paper, a feather's barbs start on its quill, a wheel has three spokes or ten.
- **No writing.** The clock has hands and no numerals, the dials show flames and icicles (the stove's flames stand in a small fire basket, kept clear of the numeral beside the dial), the singer's music is scribble lines, the luggage has no labels.

### The page from a guest's place

Touching a guest redraws the page as that guest takes it, in a circle that spreads from its feet with a rough ink edge.

- The whole page goes to pencil: a second layer of the same drawing under a veil of paper, with every other figure faint. The guest whose page it is stays in full ink and colour.
- Its own room is stamped from the full-ink layer through a lens, 1.3 times as large, in a heavy trembling frame. A guest with no room yet, or one in the hand, has no large room: the room it asks for, or is held over, keeps its ink at its own size. The room a guest with no room yet asks for has no ruled frame, which would read as a room chosen: a ring of dots in the spot colour goes slowly round it, each dot on a spot of bare paper. On the page of a guest that has a room which is not the one it asked for, the room it asked for is ringed the same way for as long as that page is open.
- Each of the eight guests has a hand of its own. The bat's page is upside down, and the bat hangs in it the right way up, by a slack cord from its ceiling with a knot at its feet. The troll's line is heavier and will not keep still. The blob's page is seen twice more, a little aside, by its other eyes. The yeti's lines run where a room is warm: tongues of melt with round ends hang from the ceiling, and none ends in a bead (a dot with a tail is a mark of writing). The lizard's is thin, spidery and shivering. The cook's has steam curling off every line of warmth. The fly's large room is ringed with small facets of itself. The singer's lines waver like a held note. Only the bat's hand turns the page, and not while the bat is carried: the house never moves under a carrying finger.
- An air is drawn as the guest takes it: what it loves or makes itself in the spot colour, as round looping curls; what it minds as heavy black scribble that shows the wall it came through, black the whole way back through every room to the guest or thing it starts from; warmth and cold as much as noise and smell, with curls where a loved one ends and the floor, ceiling or pipe mouth scribbled over where a minded one came through; what is nothing to it in pencil. What a guest must have and has not got, with nothing in the house to blame, is on its page too: rows of dots in the spot colour rising in its room where warmth would be, for a guest that needs a warm room, and, for the singer nobody hears, bows of large dots coming into her room through every wall and floor that has a room behind it and thinning away before they reach her. While nobody hears her, her own song is drawn only where it has left her room, so the room holds nothing but her and the answer she has not got. A trouble made in the guest's own room is inked there in the same heavy black: the boiler's warmth from wall to wall of the room over it, the cold of the snow hole falling the height of the room, a room-mate's stew under the ceiling, the fly's buzz beside the fly.
- The lens and the turned page are two numbers shared by the page and by the finger (`inkPlaces.ts`), so a touch always lands on what is drawn.

### Glow, hand, knocks and lamps

- **The glow** lies on every guest, on the wheel and on each of the things wherever it is. It is a ring of short radiating pen strokes in the spot colour over a thin halo of paper, so it reads on paper and on dense hatching alike. No blur.
- **The ghost hand** is a pointing hand with a hatched cuff, in the same pen, half as large again as a guest's own hand. Its fingertip lands on the guest's near shoulder, so the hand lies beside the figure. It shows a tap as one press: a second tap, copied, would turn a guest's page back, turn the hour back, or land the changeover unseen.
- **A knock** is three short curved strokes that widen and thin for half a second, and every guest looks toward it: one who is up turns its eyes, and a sleeper opens one eye and no more. **A lamp** swings on its flex about its hook and dies away. A room's door, a bed, the boiler in its bay, the luggage by the bench and the bird cage on top of it each take the marks where the finger is and answer in their own sound (a rap on wood, bedsprings, rung iron and a spit of steam, a thump on a trunk, wires ringing); the air of a room is its lamp's. None of them turns a guest's page back: only bare paper does.
- **The spot colour marks what can be touched**, so a finger on anything a guest holds out in it takes that guest: the troll's horn, the blob's arms, the yeti's hands, its cloud and the string to it, the lizard's head, the singer's music, the bag at a guest's feet in the lobby. A body comes first where two guests stand close.
- **A stare.** From the eyes of each guest who waits in the lobby a row of small pen dots runs to the door it asks for, closer together near the door, and its eyes go that way. Two guests who ask for one door have two rows that meet on it. A guest given another room than the one it asked for glances at that door, with the same row of dots, for about two and a half seconds: when it is given the room, each time its page is opened, and, every such guest, as a settled day begins, on whoever's page it is. With the ring of dots round the room it asked for on its own page, the door asked for and the room it is content in are seen side by side.
- **A carried guest** hangs from the finger by the top of its head, stiff, its bag swinging under it the other way, with a small ink shadow on whatever is below. In the hand it has left its room: nothing it makes is drawn there, nobody there is cross at it, and the yeti's cloud goes with it on its string. A thing in the hand has left its place the same way: the quilt lifted off a wall stops nothing there until it is put back.

### What a touch sets off for a moment

Every cell of the grid is seen as well as heard. What is seen lasts a second or two, changes nothing in the house and is never saved.

- **A wrapped guest who loves it** (the blob) has its toes curled up off the floor, loose feathers standing out of the roll at its neck, and its open eyes wide. A wrapped guest that is cross turns its eyes to the wall, floor or ceiling its trouble comes through.

No cross guest looks out of the page at the child.
- **A cross sleeper** has one eye open on the wall, floor or ceiling its trouble comes through. **A guest made happier** leans to the one its delight comes through (the fly into the smell); the cook hums where it stands, its cauldron at its back.
- **The lizard** goes grey and stiff when anything it minds reaches it. For want of warmth it has an icicle on its nose and a shiver as well; kept awake by a noise it has neither, and one eye open under a heavy lid.
- **The blob** has its pillow over all of its eyes when it is cross, and tips back or forward under it toward the ceiling or the floor its trouble comes through. **The bat** asleep in a room hangs head down by a cord from its ceiling, on whoever's page it is.

Set down on a wall or a floor, a guest is drawn in it, pressed thin and shaking, while whoever lives on either side stares, and pops out into the nearer room. A sleeper tucks in when the quilt is laid on its bed. The guest nearest the feathers leans back and back and sneezes forward. A guest leans an ear to the pipe stood in its corner and starts when its breath comes back. With the pipe at its mouth it fills its cheeks; with the alarm clock in its hand it winds it, rocking, or brings a fist down on the bell, a heavy guest slower and deeper than a light one. Given the stove, a guest whose comfort has room for warmth hugs it and one whose comfort has none sits on it and sags; given the ice box, a guest at home in the cold sits up on it and leans back, and one that is not goes narrow and stiff and tips over until its head knocks on the wall.
- **A guest given a bed** has its bag land at its feet and tests the bed, bouncing twice, and where two come to share a room each bounces once. A keeper of the alarm clock who has changed its hours yawns, up on its toes, when the wheel is turned.
- **A thing as it lands** has a small move for each place it can be put: the quilt flumps wide on a bed and is pressed on four times against a wall; the pipe rocks like a hat stand in a corner and takes the turn of a last bolt through a wall; the stove lands flat and jumps twice as it pings; the ice box goes on sloshing from side to side; the alarm clock jerks to its ticks by a bed, swings on its nail on a wall, and shakes too fast to follow when it rings.
- **Marks** (`inkMoments.ts`, a few pen strokes each, drawn afresh every frame). Feathers thrown up out of the quilt, each a narrow leaf with a short quill, turning as they drift down. The spray of a sneeze. Three small bows of breath out of the mouth of the standing pipe. The puff of a tooted pipe: a small cloud with a mark in it of what was passing through (a bow of jagged teeth for noise, a lazy coil for smell, three wavy lines for warmth, five short falling strokes for cold), and empty when nothing is. A scorched patch hatched two ways with a wisp of smoke, and a frosted patch of cracks that drips as it melts: each fades while the stove or the ice box, which starts against the wall it was put to, slides across the floor to its place.
- Bare paper creases where a finger lands on it: a few short bent strokes that fan out and are gone, on the plain page as on a guest's.
- The numeral of a dial is turned back about its own middle on the bat's page, so it is always the right way up.

A guest whose room another takes goes out by one door and comes in by another, and the one who takes it walks in from that door as the other walks out. A guest that leaves its room for the lobby or the bench hands back what it held.
- The porter stands at the back of the lobby by the cupboard's ladder, a bell on a bracket at the top of his trolley's handle. To show the neat way he trundles well out of his corner into the lobby, which is empty then, and back; with the things on his trolley he goes only the few steps to the wall of the house. His way is shown at the hour at which the new thing of its place is at work (the tuba played, the quilt with the noise against it, the stew stirred, the singer singing), and after that at the hour with somebody plainly happier: the hour the wheel was left at when that shows as much, and otherwise the other hour, as a view, after which the page comes back. A small coach-load queues clear of him, and the lobby has a sixth standing place just inside the front door, for the guest from the bench when five already wait.
- Things travel as the guests walk, by doors and never through a wall, hopping. In the neat way each thing the porter uses goes from where the child had it to where he puts it, down or up his ladder and over his trolley when that is the cupboard, and back again. When the coach changes over or a coach-load is sent away he rings his bell, each thing that was out goes by its room's door to his trolley and rides on it while he trundles to the wall and back to his ladder, and then it goes up the ladder and along the loft to its shelf. A thing the next coach-load has no use for is still seen going back, and is put away when it gets there. A thing given to a guest who has no room yet is seen in its hand for a moment and then goes the same way back.
- A guest on the bench sits, sunk under a travelling rug, beside its luggage.
- Two who share a room with two beds have a half of it each: one before the room's own bed, one by the door and the second bed, facing each other unless turned to a wall, and a sleeper that lies in bed lies in the bed of its own half. The fly is drawn first, so its wings go behind the other.
- **The coach that waits** for the child, the house judged, is the one sign that a cycle has ended, and a large one: the door's leaf stands wide beside the doorway in the spot colour, the step under it is in the spot colour, three pairs of eyes look out of the dark doorway toward the house and blink in turn, the whole coach shudders on its springs and its exhaust puffs behind it.
- **The sky is alive** (`inkSky.ts`). Three crows keep the bare tree, in flat ink: by day they turn about and hop on their boughs and one after another take a turn round the sky; by night they sleep, heads sunk; a finger on the tree sends all three up with a caw, and turns no page. The chimney smokes: puffs of bare paper with a line round them that trail off along the ridge. No crow is drawn as two strokes that meet, which would read as a letter.

### Motion

Everything runs on the attended clock, and which fidget comes next from a seed. Numbers come from `motion.ts`; the page only applies them.

- Each guest has a tempo, a weight and a depth of breath of its own, and three fidgets nobody else has: the troll puffs its cheeks, taps a foot and leans back; the bat rocks as if still hanging, flicks its ears and wraps itself; the blob wobbles like a jelly, sags and hugs its pillow. A test fails on a shared or near-copied motion.
- A finger landing squashes a guest at once, and it springs back past its height when the finger lifts: the bat far and fast, the troll hardly at all.
- A carried guest swings behind the finger, a light one wide and a heavy one barely.
- In the house: the tuba pumps and its marks travel outward, sleepers breathe, snow drifts, warmth rises, the wheel sways. Nothing flashes or beckons.
- A pairing is several beats, not one. In the sauna the yeti goes over to the stove, gets up on it and melts lower and wider to a puddle with a face, a puff of steam leaves it on each beat while the stove hops and its lid rattles, and at the end it is up again as if nothing had happened. In the duet the troll and the singer sway against each other in time, the wall between them takes a knock on each beat and the lamps of both rooms swing, and for the last note the two lean in and bow to the wall, no further than the wall.

### How it is drawn, and the tiers

- The paper and the house are painted once per resize into one cached layer and stamped once a frame. Each figure and thing is a cached sprite. A frame of the spike draws 27 sprites and figures, and that count goes to the grown-up handle.
- The pixel ratio is capped at 2 and the sprites are made at the surface's density.
- Tier 0 is the full page. The tier table in `config.ts` holds the look's own switches beside the pixel ratio: from tier 2 the line stops boiling, the snow is halved, the figures on the blob's page are drawn once (its page and its room are still seen again by its other eyes) and the fly keeps three of its seven facets; at tier 3 the paper loses its speckle. Every guest keeps a hand of its own at every tier.
- A page drawn from a guest's place costs one whole-surface stamp (the pencil layer) and the room; a sweep costs two whole-surface stamps for a third of a second. Up to four whole-surface layers are kept. The first frame of a new view paints its pencil layer, which may be a long frame on a tablet: to be measured.
- `frameBudget.test.ts` counts, with a recording surface: a full house of five guests with three things placed draws one whole-surface stamp at rest and two during a sweep, fewer than 80 sprites and figures in every case (the largest is 69, a view under a sweep), also with six of a touch's moments in the air, no text call but the numerals', and a second identical frame builds no new surface.
- `overlap.test.ts` is the game's own check that nothing passes through anything: every cast at three sizes, every place a thing can be put, with each meant overlap allowed by name, reason and cap.
