# Design sheet

Written before any game code, and checked by someone who did not write it before the game is built on the toy. The headings stay in this order. The look follows the sheet at the end of this file.

What each heading asks for is in the section "The design sheet" of `docs/solutions/conventions/building-a-jam-game.md`.

Lantern Walk has no learning goal. It claims no school skill and names no record.

## The band and its age rule

- **Band.** 4 to 8, as in `manifest.ts`. The youngest age, 4, governs every cue.
- **Cue-table row.** The row for a youngest age of 3 to 4 in `docs/solutions/conventions/wordless-clarity-for-the-declared-age-band.md`. Its "Avoid" column is kept as a hard limit: no text, numeral or pictorial icon to decode, no spoken instruction, no verdict, never several activities live at once, and no tool on screen before it means something. One next act is offered at a time.
- **Pack rule for the age.** (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md): a place, props and characters who react, tap and drag, a traced line that survives a lifted finger, no reading and no double tap, mischief that works, and nobody hurt by a joke.
- **Symbol rule.** The band starts below 6, so the kid side shows no word, letter, numeral or sign, and the game has no `symbols.ts`. Nothing in the setting is drawn that reads as one.
- **What `ctx.childAge` sets.** Only which sheet a first visit finds on the table (see "The designed order, and what is stored"). It gates nothing: every sheet can be cut further by any child, and a saved position wins over the age.
  - 5 and younger: a sheet with three windows already cut and backed, one in each colour.
  - 6 and 7: a sheet with one window cut and backed in yellow.
  - 8 and older: a sheet with no window.
  - No age (`null`): as for the youngest, three windows.

## The toy

The one action the finger performs most is **carrying the lantern**: the finger goes down anywhere in the dark wood and the lantern comes to it and follows it.

- **When the finger lands.** In the same frame the candle flame jumps, the lantern kicks on its wire, and a soft struck-glass note sounds; the lantern is already on its way to the finger. Nothing waits for the lift or for a move.
- **What carrying does.** The lantern hangs from the tip of a short pole on a wire. It lags the finger, swings out behind a fast move and swings back through when the finger stops, and it turns a little on its wire. Each window throws one patch of light onto the wood beside the lantern: where the window sits round the paper decides on which side the patch falls, the patch has the window's own outline, and it is filled with the window's paper colour. The patches swing and turn with the lantern. Outside them the wood is black wax with thin scraped lines; inside them the same things stand in full, with bodies, faces and ink.
- **Its sound.** Every swing of a lit lantern that goes through the middle faster than 1.1 radians a second rings one quiet note, lower on the left of the wood and higher on the right, so a carried lantern plays a slow tune of the child's own path and a still one is silent. The wire ticks at the turn of a swing wider than 0.3 of a radian; a lantern that only trembles on its wire is silent.
- **Why it is a pleasure with no goal.** The light is a brush that paints the wood back in wherever it goes, in three colours and three shapes at once, and it has weight in the hand. Sweeping it about uncovers things (a mushroom, a snail, the face that belongs to a pair of eyes). Every creature's eyes follow it, and whatever a patch falls on answers at once in its own way; a creature drawn lit is one that a patch touches somewhere, and it answers wherever it is touched. In the short rest after its last answer it does not answer again, and shows in the same step that the light touched it: a blink and a start, or a flick of every wing, again every 1.4 seconds while the light stays. Random carrying always shows something, a tap anywhere brings the lantern there, and nothing can be done wrong. A lantern let go or tapped onto a creature comes to rest beside it, the shortest slide that leaves its paper and its flame on nobody, and never on it. A lifted finger does not end a carry at once: the lantern, and at the table a paper, the candle, the scissors or a fresh sheet, waits where it was let go for 0.3 seconds of the game's clock, and a finger that lands on it again in that time carries on; one that lands on it and lifts without moving is a tap. A finger that landed where nothing is, or has been down a second without moving, gives the work to the next finger that lands, and a finger that is carrying or cutting is never interrupted: a second finger that lands meanwhile takes nothing, and is answered where it lands, at the table with a knock on the wood and the cat's ear, in the wood with a nick scraped in the wax and a tap. A finger the browser takes away is not a letting go: nothing is dropped, hung, lit or cut by it. The lantern it was carrying stays where it was held, or, held over a creature, comes to rest beside it as a lantern let go does.
- **In the toy stage** the lantern is the one a youngest first visit would roll: three windows, one red, one blue, one yellow. The table, the cutting and the hook are not in the toy.

## The object-by-action grid, and what is new on day 15

The objects are the six creatures of the wood. The actions are the five things a carried lantern can do to one of them: lay yellow, red or blue light on it, rush past it, or come to it with the candle out. Each cell is what is seen, then what is heard. The cell in bold is the creature's rule; every other cell is a wrong use, and it works. A rush is the light moving at more than 640 stage units a second while a patch passes over the creature or within 70 stage units of doing so: a rushed lantern swings out behind its pole, so a rush is taken a little wide.

A window with no paper behind it throws plain light: the owl, the hedgehog and the badger, whose rules are for any light, answer it as they answer yellow. The moths walk down the bark in a line to the patch's edge, look in and walk back up, with a patter of tiny feet. The fox tilts its head further and further at it until it tips onto its side, with a rising whine and a soft thump. The glow-worms knock on their unlit lamps as if to wake them, with dull knocks and no chime.

The shape of a window matters in the same cells: the moths draw the yellow window's outline, the glow-worms that light are exactly those the blue patch touches while it is the topmost light on them and moves slower than a rush, each keeping its lamp when the patch has gone, the hedgehog has as much room to walk as the window gives it, and a narrow window lights one creature without lighting its neighbour.

**Day 15.** The child cuts a lantern for a walk they have in mind (a long red window low on one side to lead the fox past the badger, a blue one opposite it for the fern), because by then they know three colours by shape by six creatures; on day 1 they carried a light and watched what happened.

## The error as a consequence

Nothing is wrong in this game; what a child did not intend shows in the paper and in the wood.

- **At the table the paper shows it.** A cut is a hole from the moment the scissors close it, and the scrap falls. A window cut too small is a small hole. A window left without paper behind it is an empty hole through which the table shows.
- **In the wood the light shows it, where it falls.** The small window throws a patch too small for the hedgehog to walk in, and it sits down at the edge. The unbacked window throws plain light, and the fox tilts its head at it until it tips over. Red laid on the moths sends them circling. Each answer is on the creature the light is on, and it follows from that creature's rule.
- **The badger** is the largest consequence: the wood goes dark, with only the scraped lines and the eyes left. The lantern, the creatures and everything the walk changed stay where they are, and a tap on the lantern lights it again.
- **The state stays.** The lantern is as it was cut for the whole walk. Hung on its hook and taken down, it is unrolled on the table as it was, so the child changes one thing (cuts one more window, lays another paper over one) and walks again.

## The designed order, and what is stored

The game has no learning goal and its order is short: it is the sheet that lies ready when a fresh sheet is taken.

- **What a first visit starts with, by age.** 5 and younger, or no age: `three-windows`. 6 and 7: `one-window`. 8 and older: `blank-sheet`. Both ends are open and `null` takes the youngest.
- **The positions,** as they stand in `config.ts`: `three-windows`, `one-window`, `blank-sheet`. They name the sheet and nothing else.
- **What play changes.** A walk is a cycle, and it is over when the lantern hangs on the hook. A walk made with a lantern that has at least one window the child cut goes well, and the position moves up one. Any other walk is mixed and leaves it. No walk goes badly, so the position never moves down. The position is read only when a fresh sheet is drawn from the pile: the lantern in hand is never changed by it.
- **A harder option** is always in the child's hand: any sheet can be given more windows, up to eight. Nothing harder arrives unasked.
- **Nothing shows the position.**

Every field of the saved state:

| Field | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | The version of the record, 1. |
| `position` | The id of the sheet a fresh draw from the pile gives. |
| `finished` | The lantern hangs on the hook and the walk is over. |
| `place` | `table` or `wood`: where the game is found. |
| `made` | How far the lantern is made: `flat`, `rolled`, `candle` (rolled with the candle in) or `lit`. |
| `sheet` | The windows of the sheet in hand, eight at most, in the order they were cut. Each has its outline as up to twenty points in whole hundredths of the sheet, its paper (`red`, `blue`, `yellow` or none), and whether the child cut it. |
| `lit` | Whether the candle burns. False after the badger has blown, until the tap. |
| `lantern` | Where the pole's grip rests in the wood, in stage units. A lantern in the hand is saved where the finger last held it, or beside a creature it was held over, so that it is never found standing on one; one on the hook is saved at the hook. |
| `fox` | Where the fox sits or stands. |
| `hedgehog` | Where the hedgehog sits or stands. |
| `worms` | Which glow-worms have their lamps lit, one bit a worm. |
| `moths` | `bark` or `lantern`: whether the moths are on their tree or riding with the yellow patch. Moths in the air are saved as where they are going. |
| `met` | Which creatures this walk has answered with their rule, as a list of their names. It fills in the ending and is never shown or counted on screen. |

What is short-lived and gone on load: the swing of the lantern, the owl's turned head and its fallen feather, a curled hedgehog, a puffed or tipped-over fox, a badger's held breath and its nap, what the hedgehog has spiked on its back, moths asleep or circling, a glow-worm that dropped off the fern (it is found back on it), glow-worms hanging on threads round the lantern, the scraps under the table, and a fox that was following (it is found sitting where it stood). A ninth cut in a sheet closes again behind the scissors and stores nothing.

The toy stage stores `v`, `position`, `finished`, `lit`, `lantern`, `fox`, `hedgehog`, `worms` and `moths`; the other fields arrive with the table and the hook.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

Each creature's eyes are on the lantern whenever it is lit, and its want shows in its body before any light reaches it. No taste ever changes, and no feeling is about the child.

- **The moths** want the yellow light: their feathered feelers lean towards the lantern from the bark. They like yellow, will not enter red, and fall asleep in blue.
- **The fox** wants to follow without being seen: it sits half turned away with one eye on the lantern. It likes red, sneezes at yellow, and takes blue for water.
- **The owl** wants the dark: it sits with its eyes narrowed against the candle. It dislikes every light and turns from each in a different way; an unlit lantern is the one thing it leans towards.
- **The glow-worms** want to answer blue: they hold their unlit lamps up. They like blue, hide from red, and are muddled by yellow.
- **The hedgehog** wants a light to walk in: it stands at the dark's edge with one foot lifted. It likes any slow light and dislikes hurry.
- **The badger** wants the candle out: it sits by its hole with its cheeks half full already. It dislikes every light and likes the dark it makes.

Two bystanders have no part in the rules: a spider on a thread in the wood, which pulls itself up when light touches it, and a cat under the table, which bats at falling scraps.

## The scenes

Three short scenes. Each gives way to any touch: the touch ends the scene where it is, the world is as the scene's start saved it, and the touch is then an ordinary touch. Beats that make a sound stay quiet when a touch ends the scene, what the scene was already sounding is cut in that same frame, and putting the game away closes every sound.

**At the table** there is no scene, only the child's acts in any order: the finger cuts (the scissors follow it and snip, and lifting the finger closes the cut; picked up where they lie, the scissors go with the finger and cut from where they come onto the sheet), a red, blue or yellow paper is dragged behind a window, the sheet's edge is dragged across to roll it (by the edge only, a strip beside the sheet: a finger that lands on the sheet itself cuts), a rolled sheet is dragged back the other way to unroll it as it was cut, with the candle sliding back to its place if it stood in it, until the wick is lit, the candle is set in, and a tap on the wick, or a finger that lands on the lantern and only slips, by no more than 60 stage units, lights it. A candle set on the flat sheet is rolled into the lantern. A sheet with no window makes a dark lantern that still walks: the creatures answer it as the grid's last column says, and a tap on it wakes the badger without changing the candle. A paper let go on a window, or within a small finger's width of the middle of a window too small to hit, goes behind it; a paper dropped anywhere else slides back with the cat after it (a cat already sitting where it fell stays where it is and makes no sound). While the sheet lies flat, a fresh sheet can be dragged off the pile at the table's edge onto the table, as far as the sheet's place, further than any slip of the finger (let go nearer the pile, the fresh sheet slides back onto the pile with a soft slide, and nothing changes): the sheet in hand slides off to the cat and is gone, `sheet` becomes the sheet that `position` names, `made` stays `flat`, and this is saved when the fresh sheet lands. A drag taken away before it lands changes nothing.

**What stays clear.** The hook, the hung lantern, the wick, the candle, the three papers, the pile and the sheet's rolling edge all lie outside the round 48 px home control at the top centre, 10 px from the top. Nothing the game brings to rest covers one of them: moths on an unlit lantern sit along its roof and round its wire with their wings half shut, glow-worms hang on their threads at its two sides and under it, and both leave the paper and the candle bare, a tap anywhere on the lantern, or a drag that begins on it, lights it, the fox and the hedgehog stop a body's length short of the hook and the hung lantern, each keeps to a lane of its own in which it stands across no other creature, the fern, the stump or the step, the fox stops short of a glow-worm lying on the ground, a glow-worm drops off the fern only at one of its ends and only where the fox is not standing under it, a glow-worm lets itself down to an unlit lantern only where its place there is clear of the fox and the hedgehog and is whipped up its thread when the lantern is carried at them, a lantern let go comes to rest beside a creature and not on it, sleeping moths lie in two loose heaps of their own at the foot of their tree, some with a wing across a neighbour and all clear of everything else, and moths on the yellow patch sit along its outline, the ring grown just wide enough that none sits on the next or on the lantern, its handle, its flame or its paper, with every seat inside the frame, and the cat and the scraps stay under the table. An overlap test holds this at 1180 by 820 and at 1024 by 768.

**The working pieces are plain.** The sheet, the three papers, the scissors and the candle are one flat colour each on the bare table top, with no face, no pattern and no motion until the finger moves them; the cat, the room and the wood hold the life. On the table lie only the things the sheet in hand can use now: the scissors and the candle always, the three papers once the sheet has a window, and the pile at the edge. The idle guidance points at one of them at a time: after 3 idle seconds a ring of scraped ticks round the one thing to touch, after 5 a ghost hand that shows one move with it, four times at most and further apart each time, and then the ring fades. At the table the hand shows a cut that closes into a window, a paper carried behind a window that has none (a different paper each time), the edge rolled, the candle set in, or the wick tapped once, and the third showing of a stretch is the candle set on the flat sheet. In the wood the ring is round the lantern and the hand carries it to the nearest place from which the sheet in hand lays a light on a creature and the lantern stands on nobody, a creature whose place it has not yet shown since the wood was opened; once it has shown every such place, or the walk has gone on for a minute, it carries the lantern to the hook instead. It taps a lantern that is dark once, and pulls a hung one off its hook. The hand only shows and presses nothing, and the tap it shows is one press, so a child who copies it starts a scene once and does not end it with a second. A finger at work and a running scene keep the guidance down; a touch in the grown-up's corner and anything a creature does by itself do not take it back.

1. **Out of the door** (about 6 seconds).
   - Cause: the tap that lights the wick.
   - Beats: the flame catches; the room darkens round the lantern; the door swings open; the lantern is carried to the step and its windows fall on the door in their own shapes and colours; in the wood six pairs of eyes open, one pair after another.
   - Saved when it starts: `made` is `lit`, `lit` is true, `place` is `wood`, `lantern` is the door step, `fox` and `hedgehog` are their home places, `worms` is none, `moths` is `bark`, `met` is empty, `finished` is false.
   - Filled in from the state of play: the windows of `sheet`.
2. **Home** (about 8 seconds), the ending.
   - Cause: the lantern is let go on the hook by the door: with the tip of its pole on the hook or within a finger's width of it, or with the finger itself, or the lantern it carries, on the hook, which is anywhere from the hook down to where the grip of a hung lantern rests and a finger's width to either side; each is a round at least 48 px across at either size. Let go above its place, the lantern is seen to drop onto the hook. Letting go anywhere else leaves the lantern in the wood, and a tap never hangs it, wherever the tap lands.
   - Beats: the lantern catches and swings to rest; if the candle burns its windows glow on the wall, and a lantern hung unlit hangs dark and is not lit by the scene; then each creature named in `met` answers once more from where it is (the moths settle round the yellow glow, the fox comes towards the glow as far as its place a body's length short of the moths' tree, which is 30 stage units short of the end of its lane, sits and looks away for two seconds and more (a hedgehog that stands in its road steps aside for it, as it always does, whether or not it is named), the owl hoots and turns back, the lit worms blink in turn, the hedgehog walks to the mat, the badger, whatever it was at, sits up and blows from far off, nothing happens, and it shrugs; where the lantern was hung with its candle out there is nothing to blow at, and the badger stays as it is and does not blow).
   - Saved when it starts: `finished` is true, `position` as the walk moved it, `lantern` is the hook, `fox` and `hedgehog` are where the scene will leave them, and `moths` is `lantern` if the moths are named in `met` and the hung lantern burns and has a yellow window for them to settle round, or if they were riding the yellow patch when it was hung; otherwise it is `bark`. `lit` and `worms` are not changed.
   - Filled in from the state of play: `sheet` and `met`.
3. **Taken down** (about 4 seconds).
   - Cause: the child drags the hung lantern off its hook.
   - Beats: the door opens; the lantern goes in; the flame is pinched out with a curl of smoke (a lantern that hung dark has no flame: nothing is pinched and no smoke curls); the paper unrolls flat on the table.
   - Saved when it starts: `place` is `table`, `made` is `flat`, `lit` is false, `finished` is false.
   - Filled in from the state of play: `sheet`.

**How a cycle ends and how the next one starts.** The walk ends when the lantern is hung. The finished scene stays for as long as the child likes: the lantern hangs, its windows glow on the wall and light nobody (a creature the glow lies on is drawn as the dark shows it, and only the moths that ride the yellow glow are drawn lit by it), the creatures idle, and nothing new starts or counts down. The idle guidance comes as in any other idle stretch (the ring round the hung lantern after 3 seconds, the ghost hand showing the pull after 5, four times at most, and then the ring fades), and nothing else asks. The next cycle begins by the child's own act of taking the lantern down. Until the save has been read only the black wax is drawn, and neither place. On load no scene replays: the game is found at the table as the sheet was left, in the wood with the lantern where it was let go, or with the lantern on its hook. In the wood every creature waits as it was found, whatever light lies on it, until a finger lands: nothing starts and nothing sounds on load, or after the first scene, before the first touch. A game put away is the game a load of its save gives: a thing held in the hand at the table (a paper, the candle, the scissors, a fresh sheet, the sheet's edge) goes back where it came from, the lantern held in the wood stays where it was held, or beside a creature it was held over, a thing let go that is still waiting out its 0.3 seconds is let go for good where it was (the cut is a window, the paper is behind its window, the fresh sheet has landed, the lantern on the hook is hung), a scene lands where its start saved it, every sound closes, and the idle guidance starts again.

## The look

**Rainbow scratch card**, the first look reserved for the game. This part is the art guide and is not part of the sheet.

- **The idea.** The wood and the room are a sheet of matte black wax over colour. Everything in the dark is drawn in thin rough scraped lines, and a line takes its colour from the band of rainbow under the wax at the place it crosses, so one long line changes colour along its length and a creature that walks changes colour as it goes. Where the lantern's light falls the wax is scraped clear in the window's own outline: the patch is flat paper colour, the wood that stands in it shows in black ink, and every creature a patch touches is shown whole, with a pale body, black ink and wide white eyes. A patch never cuts a creature in two. The one lantern that lights nobody is the lantern on its hook: its windows glow on the wall, a creature the glow lies on is scraped again over it as the dark shows it, and only moths riding the yellow glow are shown lit. Nothing glows, nothing has a beam and nothing shines: light is only where the wax is gone.
- **Palette.** Wax: near black with a little violet, with a dusty grain. Under it: magenta, orange, yellow, green, turquoise, blue and violet in a slanted band across the whole frame. Paper colours, each shifting slightly across the frame as a crayon layer does: red from crimson to vermilion, blue from ultramarine to a greener blue, yellow from lemon to amber. Plain light, from a window with no paper, and a glow-worm's own lamp show the rainbow band itself; a lamp is drawn under the creatures, so it shows its own worm and the ground and nobody else. Ink in the light: the wax black. Bodies in the light: one cream, the colour of the paper under the crayon, whatever the patch.
- **Materials.** Lines are rough: every stroke wobbles, a second fainter scrape runs a hair beside it, and loose crumbs of wax lie beside the lines and along the edge of each scraped patch.
- **Lighting.** None. A patch is a hole in the wax, and a put-out candle leaves only lines and eyes.
- **The room.** The same card. The wall, the window with its branch and moon, the curtains, the pegs with a coat and a scarf, the shelf of jars, the door, the floorboards, the rug, the basket of rolled sheets, the broom and a framed moth are scraped lines. The table top is the one place scraped clear, in the colour of bare paper. On it the working pieces lie plain, one flat colour each and no face: the white sheet, three papers, slate scissors, a peach candle and the pile. The sheet's right-hand edge curls up in a lip beside it: that strip is the edge that rolls the sheet, and the sheet itself only cuts. The cat under the table is drawn like the creatures of the wood and holds the room's life.
- **Motion rules.** The setting stands still and is drawn once. The lines of everything alive shiver six times a second. Each creature moves like itself and no two share a move: the owl is slow and heavy and turns its whole head; the fox is quick in the ears and brush and still in the body; the badger rocks and sniffs; the hedgehog jitters and ripples its spines; the moths twitch, each on its own count; the glow-worms inch; the cat's eyes follow the finger and its paw bats. The lantern is the one thing with real weight: it lags, swings and settles.
- **What fills the frame.** A great trunk with the owl's branch at the left, with creepers, bracket fungi and a frond under the branch; a canopy of uneven clumps and loose leaves across the top with the moon behind it; creepers over the middle; the house wall with its eaves, door, mat and hook at the right; the moths' tree beside it with the glow-worms' fern growing from its side and arching over the middle; the badger's mound with a toadstool and the mouth of its hole at the lower left; a stump with a snail on it; fallen leaves, pebbles and twigs lying flat on the ground between the two ground lines, where the fox and the hedgehog walk; stones of a path and grass along the foot; and the spider on its thread. Nothing in the setting is drawn as a thing to pick up, and nothing is a row of equal marks. Every cell of an eight by six grid over the stage holds lines, and a test holds that.
- **What stands where.** Every creature has a room of its own in `layout.ts`, the model keeps it there, and tests draw it and hold it inside: nothing stands on or passes through anything else that stands. Things on their way are not held apart: moths in flight and riding a patch, and a glow-worm on its way down its thread, may cross what they pass.
- **Marks a child might read.** Listed with the reason each stays under "Reader" in `REFINEMENT.md`.
- **The tiers.** A tier changes the pixel ratio and whether the wax grain and the crumbs are drawn. Lines, patches, creatures and every answer to a touch are the same on every tier, so the lowest tier is still a scratch card.
