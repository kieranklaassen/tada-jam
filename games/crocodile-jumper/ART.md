# Design sheet

## The band and its age rule

Ages 4 to 7. The youngest age governs: the 3 to 4 row of the cue table, with its Avoid column as hard constraints (no text, numeral or picture to be decoded, no spoken instruction, no competing activities), and pretend play with characters who react (pack: game-design, ages-4-to-6.md). Tap and drag only; everything that matters can be done with taps alone; essential targets are about 100 units across and none is in the bottom strip. The band starts below 6, so there is no word, letter, numeral or sign anywhere on the kid side.

`ctx.childAge` sets where a first visit starts. 5 and younger: `croc-alone`. 6 and older: `snail-in-ducks`. `null`: `croc-alone`. A saved position wins, and no age opens or closes anything.

## The toy

A finger drags a foil-wrapped sausage along a paper strip. The wrapper crackles and flashes as it lands under the finger; sliding, it skids, bumps the piece in front of it nose to tail with a clack, and pushes the whole train along. Let go nearly touching, two pieces close up with a soft kiss. Lifted and let go on another piece, it flops over it. A tap makes a lying piece scoot off by itself until it bumps something. In an empty shop with nobody on the table, shoving a train of sausages, shoes and ducks to and fro until it clacks against the pincushion is a pleasure with no goal.

The window opens with a creak and a gust. The mice at the end of the table hold a cake: a tap makes them squeak and juggle it. A covered tray's foil tears off with a long crisp rip. The waiting customer waves its favourite thing with a call; what it holds stays its own.

On day 15 a child can: look at a customer and choose, before laying a piece, the measure that will come out exactly and the one that customer loves; leave a hole on purpose where a customer is ticklish; and lay two customers end to end and measure one jumper for both.

## The representation

Length is the stretch of strip that pieces fill, laid end to end from a fixed start, beside the thing itself. The jumper is the answer as an object, and it grows with the row: the moment a piece lies on the strip, the magpie snips and a puffed ring of that piece's foil leaps up from it and closes round the animal straight above, as long as the piece and just where it lies. Shove the train and the rings slide along the animal with it. The same row, ring for piece, gap for gap. The unit stays visible in the result, so two jumpers for one crocodile, one of few long rings and one of many short ones, are seen to be equally long. Laying objects end to end is school practice; this form of it has no trial behind it. The pieces are everyday things on purpose, because the named California foundation measures with real objects and the Dutch guidance with informal measures. Each is otherwise plain: one foil colour for its kind, an outline whose length is the only property that matters, no face, no pattern and no idle motion; it moves only when a touch or a gust the child let in moves it. They lie on a plain paper strip of a contrasting hue, and nothing tappable that is not a piece, the customer or the jumper is on the strip. The game stops at the object: no picture of a length and no symbol.

## The four mechanic questions

- **Swap.** Other content cannot be put in: the pieces are lengths and the rule of play is that they add up along a line.
- **Attention.** At the moment of decision the child looks at the end of the row and the end of the animal, and at the join between two pieces.
- **Fun.** Shoving the train together is the measuring, and the jumper that comes of it is the joke.
- **Guess.** Random tapping lays pieces with gaps or on top of each other and makes a draughty or lumpy jumper; it fits closely only when ends are brought together and the laying stops at the tail.

## The error as a consequence

Nothing is refused and nothing is marked wrong. A gap between pieces, or between the pincushion and the first piece, is a hole with skin in it: a little wind whistles through and the skin there goes blue and dimpled. Two pieces on one spot are a double ring that bulges and squeaks when the customer breathes. A row that stops short leaves the tail out: blue, shaking, with a drip that freezes. A row that runs on leaves empty rings on the table past the tail, and the mice trip over them and wear the cake. Each shows exactly where it happened. The row stays on the strip under the jumper, so the child moves one piece and the ring moves with it. A close fit gets no cheer: the customer stops shivering, stretches and goes pink at the cheeks. When the tail tip is covered, by any row, the one at the door sees that it is its turn and hops and waves what it holds. While a tail is bare nobody gets up: a touch on the door makes the waiting one call and the bare tail shake hardest.

## The designed order, and what is stored

Lengths, in ducks for this sheet only: a duck is the shortest piece, a shoe is one and a half ducks, a sausage two. Crocodile ten, dog six, snail five, seal seven and a half, python eleven and a half.

1. `croc-alone`: the crocodile; only the sausages are uncovered. New: laying end to end along somebody.
2. `dog-shorter`: the dog. New: the row can be too long for somebody.
3. `snail-in-ducks`: the snail; the ducks are uncovered. New: a length that sausages do not come out to.
4. `seal-in-shoes`: the seal; the shoes are uncovered. New: a third measure.
5. `python-mixed`: the python. New: a length that comes out only with two kinds together.
6. `snails-share`: two snails in one jumper. New: two customers and one row.
7. `dog-and-snail`: a dog and a snail in one jumper. Combination.
8. `full-shop`: the customers and the pairs in turn, for as long as the child likes.

A covered tray can be torn open by the child at any position, and stays open: that is the harder option, seen and chosen in the world. Every customer and pair has at least one close fit from the trays its position uncovers.

The position moves when the child calls in the next customer. Well: the one who leaves wears a jumper with no hole, no bulge and not long (a short one cannot leave). Badly: it wears one with three or more of those faults. Mixed, which moves nothing: anything else. A close fit is judged with a little room on purpose. The waiting customer was laid out before that, so a new position lays out the one after next: the touch that moves the position brings in the one who stood waiting, and a new one, from the place after the new position, comes to the door.

Stored: the version; the position; who lies on the table (one or two); the row (each piece's kind and place, in the order they lie on each other); which is the jumper as well; who waits; the place in the turn of `full-shop`; which trays are open; who was last dressed, with that jumper, which is the one seen outside the window; which new things have been shown. `finished` is always false: the touch that ends a cycle begins the next. Not stored, and gone on load: a gust, wriggles, an act, a thing in the hand (saved where it came from), the cake on a mouse.

## The characters and their fixed tastes

The want of every customer is to be warm, seen in its shivering bare skin; the one who waits at the door shivers too, and holds up the thing it loves, turned to the child.

- **The crocodile.** Long, vain, easily tickled. Loves sausages, is afraid of ducks, does not mind shoes.
- **The dog.** Long and low, eager. Loves shoes, takes sausages for relations and is shocked, argues with ducks.
- **The snail.** Slow, hopeful. Loves ducks for going so fast, dislikes shoes and shuts itself in, pokes sausages.
- **The seal.** No feet and wishes she had. Loves shoes, dislikes sausages, balances ducks.
- **The python.** Forgets how long she is. Loves sausages, has no use for shoes, and cannot look at a duck without crossing her eyes.
- **The magpie tailor.** Wants foil, sits on her pile of it, and snips at anything laid in a row.
- **The two mice.** Want their cake upright.

No one is disappointed in the child.

## The scenes

A ring that closes round the customer, and the magpie's hop along the rings, are answers and not scenes: under 1 s, and at most 4 s. Neither changes anything stored beyond the row, and a touch lands them.

- **Next, please**, caused by a touch on the waiting customer once the tail tip of the one on the table is covered: the pieces hop home, the one on the table gets down and goes off in the jumper in its own gait (a hole whistles, a long end drags the mice along), passes outside the window in the snow, and the next lies down; another comes to the door. If a tray is uncovered by the new place, the magpie tears it open last. 5 to 6 s. Everything it changes (position, who lies, the empty row, who waits, the turn, open trays, who is outside) is saved when it starts. A touch lands it. Before that, a touch on the waiting one only makes it call and wave.
- **Lying down together**, caused by a waiting customer let go behind the one on the table, or by a pair's place in the order: bellies bump, the second rests its chin on the first. This is a move of about 1 s and not a scene. When a waiting customer was let go there, the next in turn comes to the door at once, shivering and holding up what it loves. Who lies, who waits and the turn are saved when it starts. The row stays, and is now short. A touch lands it.

- **Showing**, caused by the child's first touch in a first visit, and after that by the touch that brings in the first customer of a position whose new thing has not been shown: on the magpie's own bench, never on the strip, she does it once with scraps of her own foil, for a mouse and never for the customer on the table. Laying end to end: two scraps pushed together along the mouse until they touch. Too long: a scrap past its tail is slid off. A new tray: one scrap of that piece's length is laid against another, once for the ducks and once for the shoes. Two kinds together: a long scrap and a short one, closed up. Two customers: the two mice lie nose to tail along one row. 4 to 5 s, after Next, please where both follow one touch. Its mark is saved when the showing itself starts; put away before that, it is still owed and starts at the child's first touch. A touch ends it, and it never shows the answer for the customer on the table.

A cycle ends only with the child's touch on the one who waits, and that touch starts the next. If nobody touches, the dressed customer lies there and nothing starts. No scene plays on load.

## The records

### us-ca

Level, as the lookup prints it: age 4 is `preschool-tk`, age mapping official; the two printed bands overlap for the whole of age 4, so both statements of a foundation apply. At age 5 the lookup returns `preschool-tk` (Later) and `kindergarten`, with the `cross-grade` lane beside them, labelled cross-grade; the game names records of the first only. No gap is printed at either age. At age 6 (official) it returns `kindergarten` and `grade-1`, and at age 7 (derived) `grade-1`, both with `cross-grade`; at age 7 it prints that grade 2 is not in the pack. The game names no record of these, so for a child past five and a half it rests on nothing here. No California record is named for thinking about a measure that turns out against expectation.

- `edu.us-ca.preschool-tk.mathematics.objective.mathematics-strand-3-0-measurement-and-data-3-3` (`us-ca` 3.3, Measurement and Data): department-published-foundation, confirmed. In the game's words: at the later age, laying real things one after another along something to find how long it is.
  Limits taken: later age only, so for a child at the earlier age the game rests on nothing here; length only; the units are physical objects placed one after another; no ruler and no standard unit; a row of mixed lengths is allowed. Taken in part: the game never asks for the number. Not in Limits, and the game's own choice: the three lengths, and how many pieces a row has.
- `edu.us-ca.preschool-tk.mathematics.objective.mathematics-strand-3-0-measurement-and-data-3-1` (`us-ca` 3.1, Measurement and Data): department-published-foundation, confirmed. In the game's words: seeing that one thing is longer than another, here the row and the animal it lies beside.
  Taken in part: length only, not weight or capacity, and by looking; the game never asks for it to be said. Limits taken: at the earlier age only noticing, with no procedure; at the later age two things; no unit, number or measuring tool in either. How they are compared is left open by Limits, and side by side is the game's own choice. Of what the foundation asks, noticing (earlier) and comparing (later) are taken, and communicating it is not.

### nl

Level, as the lookup prints it: ages 4, 5 and 6 reach `fase-1`, age mapping convention; the statement is for groep 1 to 3 and no year is named. At age 4 `peuters` is returned as well, up to the fourth birthday, and the game uses no record of it. The `einde-po` lane is returned beside these, labelled end-of-primary goals; none of it is used. No gap is printed at ages 4 to 6. At age 7 the lookup returns `fase-1` (groep 3) and `fase-2`, which it labels as not an age the pack set out to cover; no record of `fase-2` is used. No Dutch record is named for comparing two things by length by looking; that part rests on the California foundation alone.

- `edu.nl.fase-1.mathematics.objective.725c4b88-a86b-4980-a44b-0dfa14387370` (`nl` rw/m/1/05/fase1): curriculum-institute-guidance, confirmed. In the game's words: measuring with things at hand that can stand for a length.
  Limits taken: what a school offers, not what a child must do; informal measures; no metre, centimetre or ruler; length only. Taken in part: the game never asks for the result to be told. Not in Limits: sausages, shoes and ducks are the game's own choice.
- `edu.nl.fase-1.mathematics.objective.5898839b-c220-48ef-aa5d-c7e12eb9c599` (`nl` rw/m/1/07/fase1): curriculum-institute-guidance, confirmed. In the game's words: thinking about length where a situation makes it matter or turns out against what was expected, here a jumper that does not come out.
  Taken in part: length only, not the distance around a thing, and shown by doing; the game never asks for reasons. Limits taken: what a school offers, not what a child must do; which situations are suitable is left open by Limits, and these are the game's own choice. Whether a conflict situation is an outcome against expectation or a disagreement between children is left open by Limits too; taking the first is the game's own choice.

### Where the two differ

- **Measuring with loose objects.** The California foundation has it at the later age only; the Dutch guidance names no year within fase 1. The game follows the Dutch guidance and offers it to everyone; for a child at California's earlier age it is the game's own choice.
- **Mixing lengths in a row.** The California foundation allows it; neither Dutch record named here speaks of it in its Limits. The game follows California and lets every row be mixed.
- **A measure that does not come out.** It is taken from the Dutch guidance (rw/m/1/07/fase1) alone. Neither California record named here speaks of it. The game follows the Dutch guidance.
- **Standing.** The California records are foundations published by a state department and the Dutch ones guidance by the curriculum institute. Nothing in the play depends on this, and the two are not equated.

### The claim

A Jumper for the Crocodile is designed from two California preschool and transitional kindergarten learning foundations, published by a state department, in part (measuring length by laying real objects one after another, a later-age foundation, without the telling of a number; and comparing two things by length, by looking), and from two goals of guidance by the Dutch curriculum institute for fase 1, in part (measuring with informal measures, and thinking about length in situations that make it matter, both by doing and never by telling); all four records are confirmed.

## The look

Foil sweet wrappers, the first look taken; the backup (String and pins) was not needed. 2D canvas.

- **Foil** (`foil.ts`). A silhouette is clipped and filled with a jittered mesh of flat triangles, each with a tilt of its own and one of three tones of its metal, near white where it faces the light. Every picture is baked in three lights. A thing that moves steps through them, so it flashes as it turns; a thing at rest keeps one, so the working pieces have no idle motion.
- **Cellophane** is a translucent fan of pleats: the crocodile's tail tip, the ends of a covered tray, the curtains.
- **The dull side** of the wrapper, matte paper with soft creases, is the wall, the table and the strip. Nothing tappable is matte.
- **Colours.** Sausage red `#d8402f`, shoe blue `#2f74cf` and duck yellow `#f0b41c` belong to the pieces, their trays and their rings. Customers: crocodile green, dog bronze, snail violet with a mint house, seal silver, python pink. Bare skin is the same facets mixed towards ice blue.
- **Rings** are the piece's own metal and the piece's own length: sausage smooth with a long shine, shoe laced, duck feathered and frilled. Their height follows the body at the ring's middle; past the tail they lie flat.
- **Movers.** Heads are baked with their whites; pupils, lids, mouths, jaws and tongues are drawn each frame. Each customer has its own tickle, its own three ways to take a piece, and the shiver they share.
- **The room** is painted once for a size of the surface and past the stage's edges.
- **Tiers** set the pixel ratio and the number of snowflakes. No tier drops an answer.
