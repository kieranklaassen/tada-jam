# The gentle set: briefs

Twelve prototypes in the spirit of Montessori and Waldorf (see `GENTLE.md` for why and for the rules that apply to all of them). Each brief fixes the activity, the work the hand does, and where it ends. The builder owns everything else. If a detail here fights the calm or the delight, say so in the report and follow `GENTLE.md`.

All are for ages 3 to 7 unless the brief says otherwise, and all set `meta.set` to `'gentle'`.

---

## Practical life: real work with real steps

### bread-day
**Bread Day** · 🍞 · Waldorf baking day; Montessori food preparation

A farmhouse kitchen table in morning light. Flour in a crock, a jug of water, a pinch-bowl of salt, a wooden bowl. The child tips flour into the bowl (drag the scoop, it pours where it is tilted), pours water from the jug, and stirs with a wooden spoon until it comes together. Then the dough is turned onto the table and kneaded: push it with a finger and it squashes, folds and springs back, getting smoother the more it is worked. Shape it: pull pieces off to make rolls, roll one long and twist it into a plait, press seeds on top. Cover it with a cloth and it rises slowly while the child watches or peeks. Slide the tray into the wood oven; the loaves swell and turn golden behind the glass, and they come out when the child opens the door (leaving them longer makes them darker, never burnt). The ending: the bread is carried to a table where two or three family figures sit, it is broken and shared, steam rising. Then the kitchen is quiet, with the empty bowl waiting.

The hand's work is the dough. It must feel soft, heavy and alive under the finger. Next time is different because the child shapes different loaves.

### tea-time
**Tea Time** · 🫖 · Montessori pouring and table setting; Waldorf doll play

A low table under a tree with three guests waiting (a doll, a bear, a wooden rabbit: simple, friendly, patient). A tray holds a teapot, cups and saucers, a plate of biscuits, a small jug of milk, napkins. The child sets a place for each guest (cups click onto saucers), then pours: drag the teapot over a cup and tilt it by dragging its handle down; tea streams from the spout, the cup fills, and steam curls up. Pour too long and it spills over into the saucer and onto the cloth, which is no trouble: there is a small sponge on the tray, and wiping the spill is part of the work. Add milk and it clouds the tea. Hand out biscuits. A guest lifts the cup and sips when it is served, with a small nod; each one drinks at its own pace. When everyone has finished, cups can be carried back to the tray and the cloth shaken out.

The hand's work is the pour: the stream must follow the tilt exactly, with the sound of water rising in pitch as the cup fills. That rising note is the control of error; nothing else tells the child when to stop.

### wash-day
**Wash Day** · 🧺 · Montessori cloth washing; Waldorf washing day

A garden with a wooden tub of water, a washboard, a bar of soap, a basket of muddy play clothes, and a washing line between two trees. Take a garment from the basket, dunk it, rub it on the washboard: suds build, the mud lifts off where the finger rubs, and the water slowly clouds. Lift it out and it drips; wring it by dragging across it and water runs out. Carry it to the line and peg it up (two pegs from the peg bag, each pressed on with a small click). The wind moves the washing and the sun dries it: colours brighten and the cloth lightens as it dries, in its own time. Dry clothes are unpegged, folded in two drags (in half, in half again) and laid in the basket. The ending is a full line swaying in the sun, then a neat basket carried indoors.

The hand's work is rub, wring and peg. Cloth should hang and swing like cloth. A bird may land on the line; a sock may blow off and be picked up again.

### flower-table
**Flower Table** · 💐 · Montessori flower arranging; Waldorf seasonal table

A cutting garden beside a table with three empty vases of different heights, a jug of water and a pair of scissors. Snip flowers and leaves from the garden bed (tap or draw the scissors across a stem where you want it cut: long or short), pour water into a vase, and stand the stems in it one by one. Stems lean and settle against each other the way real ones do. Too long for a small vase and it tips its head; trim it shorter. There is no right bouquet. When a vase is finished the child carries it to a place in the room behind: the windowsill, the dining table, beside a sleeping cat; the room looks more cared-for with each one. Fallen leaves and petals can be swept from the table with a small brush. The garden regrows slowly, and its flowers change with the season the prototype opens in.

The hand's work is cutting to length and placing. The pleasure is in composing something beautiful and then seeing it in the room.

### farm-morning
**Farm Morning** · 🐓 · Montessori care of animals; Waldorf rhythm of the day

A small farmyard at sunrise: a henhouse, a pony in a stall, a goat, a water trough, a feed bin. The animals are waking and each plainly needs one thing. Open the henhouse door and the hens come out; scatter grain from the scoop by dragging across the yard and they peck where it falls; lift the nest-box lids and gather the warm eggs into a basket one at a time. Work the pump handle up and down to fill the bucket, carry it and pour it into the trough; the pony drinks. Brush the pony with long strokes and dust lifts off its coat until it shines; it leans into the brush. Fork hay into the goat's rack. Nothing is urgent and any order works. The ending: every animal fed and content in the full morning light, the basket of eggs by the door, the yard quiet except for the hens.

The hand's work is scattering, pumping, pouring and brushing. Each animal answers care with its own small sign of comfort. No animal is ever distressed, only waiting.

### goodnight-teddy
**Goodnight, Teddy** · 🧸 · Montessori care of self, shown through doll play; Waldorf evening rhythm

Evening in a small bedroom and bathroom. Teddy is grubby from the day. Run the bath (turn the tap, the water rises, test it with a hand), lower Teddy in, lather with the soap, rinse with the jug, lift out and rub dry with a towel until fluffy. Choose pyjamas from two or three in the drawer and dress Teddy (arms into sleeves by dragging, buttons pushed through one by one). Brush teeth with little circles. Carry Teddy to bed, pull the blanket up, choose a small friend to tuck in beside. Wind the music box; draw the curtains; turn down the lamp. Teddy's eyes grow heavy during the lullaby and close. The ending is real: the room is dark and still, a night-light glows, stars show through the gap in the curtain, and nothing more happens until the child chooses morning by opening the curtains.

The hand's work is washing, drying, buttoning and tucking in. The prototype gets quieter and darker with each step. This one ends in sleep on purpose.

### dress-for-weather
**Dress for the Weather** · 🧥 · Montessori dressing frames and care of self; Waldorf outdoor play in all weathers

A hallway with a window, a coat rack and a boot bench. A child figure stands ready to go out. Look through the window: today it is raining, or snowing, or windy, or hot (it changes each time). Choose what to wear from the rack and dress the figure: arms into the coat, the zip pulled up from the bottom, buttons pushed through, boots pulled on with a tug, a hat, a scarf wound round, mittens. The fastenings are the work: a zip that follows the finger, buttons that pop through holes, a buckle. Then open the door and step out: in rain there are puddles to jump in and a boat to float down the gutter; in snow a snowball to roll and a snowman to build; in wind a kite; in sun a watering can and a paddling pool. If the figure went out without boots, its socks get wet and it comes back for them: the weather is the control of error, not a voice. The ending is coming home: boots off, coat hung on its hook.

The hand's work is zips, buttons and buckles, then free play outdoors.

## Handwork and art

### wet-paint
**Wet Paint** · 🎨 · Waldorf wet-on-wet watercolour

A sheet of damp watercolour paper on a board, a wide brush, a jar of water and three jars of colour: red, yellow, blue. Dip the brush in a colour and paint: the paint flows from the brush and bleeds softly into the wet paper, edges blooming outward. Where yellow meets blue it turns green by itself; red into yellow glows orange; all three make earthy browns. Rinsing the brush clouds the water jar. A sponge lifts colour back off. There are no outlines, no shapes to fill and no tools beyond these. When the child is finished they lift the sheet and peg it on a line across the top of the room to dry beside their earlier paintings (the last few stay up for the session), and a fresh damp sheet is laid down.

The hand's work is the brush stroke. The whole prototype is the diffusion: it must look like real pigment in water (a small grid simulation of wetness and pigment that spreads and settles), slow enough to watch. The colour mixing is the discovery.

### lantern-walk
**Lantern Walk** · 🏮 · Waldorf lantern festival (Martinmas); paper craft

Late afternoon at a craft table: a sheet of paper already washed with sunset colours, a pair of scissors, a candle stub, a stick. Fold the paper into a cylinder by dragging its edge across; snip little windows into it (each cut pops out a star, a moon or a leaf shape and lets light through); set the candle inside; hang the lantern on the stick; touch a match to the wick. Then the scene opens onto a path through a dusky wood, and the child carries the lantern along it by dragging it. Its circle of warm light shows what the dark was hiding: an owl on a branch, a hedgehog in the leaves, mushrooms, a fox who watches and then walks alongside for a while, other children's lanterns bobbing far off on the hill. A soft pentatonic tune follows the lantern's swing. The walk ends at home: the lantern is hung by the door, and its window shapes glow on the wall.

The hand's work is cutting the windows (what is cut is what shines later) and carrying the light. Nothing in the wood is frightening.

## Imaginary play: open-ended small worlds

### rainbow-world
**Rainbow World** · 🌈 · Waldorf open-ended toys: stacking rainbow arches, peg dolls, play silks, wooden animals

A play mat on a wooden floor in afternoon light. On it: a nested wooden rainbow of seven arches, a handful of peg dolls in felt hats, a few carved wooden animals (horse, sheep, fox, bird), three play silks (blue, green, gold), some building blocks and a few pine cones and stones. Everything can be picked up, turned and put down anywhere, and stays where it is put. An arch stood on its feet is a bridge or a tunnel; rocked on its back it is a cradle or a boat; two together make a house; stacked they make a tower. A blue silk spread out is a pond or a river, a green one a hill when it is draped over blocks. Peg dolls can stand, ride the horse, sit in a boat, sleep under an arch. Nothing in the prototype says what any of it is.

It is quietly alive: a doll set down in a cradle-arch rocks gently; one placed on the horse rides when the horse is moved; the bird hops onto whatever is highest; an arch tipped on its back and nudged rocks until it settles. There is no ending because pretend play has none; instead there is a toy basket: tipping things back into it is its own satisfying act, and the mat is clear for the next story.

The hand's work is arranging. The physics should be simple and kind (things rest where placed; stacks hold unless truly unbalanced).

### fairy-house
**Fairy House** · 🍄 · Waldorf nature play and fairy tales; building with natural materials

The mossy roots of a big tree, with a hollow at the bottom. Around it lie things gathered from the wood: bark slabs, twigs, moss cushions, acorn caps, pebbles, leaves, a feather, snail shells, a puddle. The child builds a home in and around the hollow: lean bark for a roof, twigs for a ladder or a fence, a leaf for a bed with a moss pillow, an acorn cap for a bowl, pebbles for a path, a shell for a bath filled from the puddle. Pieces lean and rest naturally and stay where they are put. There is no plan to follow.

When the child is ready, they pull the evening down (drag the sun below the hill). In the dusk, small lights come out of the wood: two or three fairy or gnome folk, simple and soft-shaped, who find the house and use exactly what was built: one climbs the ladder, one curls up on the leaf bed, one washes in the shell, one sits on the pebble path with a lantern. If there is no bed, they sleep in the moss. Fireflies gather. That is the ending: the house lived in, lights in the dusk. Morning comes when the child raises the sun; the folk have gone and the house is as the child left it, ready to be changed.

The hand's work is building. The payoff is seeing someone else make a home in what you made.

### nature-walk
**Nature Walk** · 🍂 · Waldorf nature table and the seasons; Montessori care of the environment

A woodland path in the current season (it opens in the real season and the child can turn the year by swiping a small wooden wheel: bare branches and snow, blossom, deep green, falling leaves). The child walks a small figure along the path by dragging ahead of it, and the wood answers attention: lift a fallen leaf and there is a beetle under it; part the long grass and a hare sits up; tap the stream and a frog jumps in; look up into a tree and a squirrel looks back. Along the way are treasures to pick up and put in a basket: an acorn, a striped feather, a smooth stone, a conker, a snail shell, a pine cone, a sprig of berries. The basket holds a handful, not everything.

The path ends at home, at a low shelf by the window: the nature table. The child takes each treasure out and arranges it on a cloth the colour of the season, beside a candle. Nothing is counted and nothing is "found: 3 of 12". The table stays as arranged; the next walk, in another season, brings different things to set beside it.

The hand's work is noticing, gathering and arranging. Animals are never caught, only met.

## Fantasy building: the Waldorf fairy-tale world

Added after the owner played the first gentle prototypes and asked for more that are "calm and building games, and really fantasy", leaning into Waldorf. All of `GENTLE.md` applies, plus its section "Fantasy building". In each of these the child builds something with their hands out of simple natural pieces, and then the fairy-tale world answers: someone comes to live in it, or it comes alive. Building is never tested: nothing topples as a punishment, nothing is scored, there is no right design.

The folk are the old elemental ones of Waldorf stories: gnomes of the earth, water sprites, wind and leaf children, King Winter. They are small, simple, kindly and a little shy.

### block-castle
**Block Castle** · 🏰 · Waldorf wooden blocks; imagination turning a block into a tower

A wooden floor in window light with a basket of plain, natural blocks: cubes, long planks, columns, arches, a few triangles and cones, some cut from branches with the bark still on. The child builds whatever castle they like by carrying blocks out and setting them down; a block clicks softly into place on what is beneath it and stays (stacks are stable; a block set where nothing can hold it simply slides down to rest). Tap a placed block to turn it. There is a small brass bell beside the basket.

When the child rings the bell, the room dims to story-light and the blocks become what they were pretending to be, in the same shape exactly: columns and stacks rise as stone towers with pointed roofs where a cone or triangle was placed, planks become walls and drawbridges, arches become gates, and pennants unfurl from the highest points. A tiny king, a baker, a child with a kite and a sleepy, friendly dragon arrive and use it: someone looks out from the tallest tower, the dragon curls round the base of whatever is widest, a light comes on in every enclosed space. It holds as a living picture, slowly, with a pentatonic tune. Ringing the bell again turns it back to blocks in the afternoon light, as the child left them, to be changed or tipped back in the basket.

The hand's work is building. The payoff is seeing your own shape, kept exactly, turn into the castle you meant.

### treetop-village
**Treetop Village** · 🌳 · Waldorf nature play and fairy tales; building for others

A great old oak fills the field, with strong branches at several heights and a hollow or two. At its foot is a pile of woodland building things: plank platforms, twig ladders, rope bridges, little round doors, acorn-cap lanterns on strings, leaf awnings, a bucket on a rope, a swing. The child drags pieces into the tree: a platform settles onto a branch, a ladder leans between two levels, a rope bridge sags between two platforms, a door fits a hollow, a lantern string hangs in a curve between any two points. Pieces fit where they sensibly can and stay.

The tree folk (a few small elf children in leaf caps, a squirrel, an owl) watch from among the leaves. Whenever something new makes a place reachable, one comes out to try it: climbs the ladder, crosses the bridge with arms out for balance, hauls the bucket up, sits on the platform edge swinging its legs. Pulling the evening down (drag the sun behind the hill) lights every lantern the child hung; the folk gather on the platforms, and windows glow behind the doors. That is the ending, and it stays. Morning returns when the child lifts the sun, and the village is as it was built, ready for more.

The hand's work is fitting pieces into the tree. The payoff is small folk using exactly the ways you made.

### bark-boats
**Bark Boats** · ⛵ · Waldorf nature craft; water sprites and the journey down the brook

A mossy bank beside a clear brook. On the bank lie boat-makings: curls of bark and half walnut shells for hulls, twigs for masts, leaves of different shapes for sails, a feather, acorn caps and berries for cargo. The child builds a boat on a flat stone: choose a hull, press a mast in, thread a leaf on as a sail, load a little cargo. A gnome no bigger than a thumb wanders up, climbs aboard and sits. Carry the boat to the water and let go.

The brook takes it. The view follows the boat downstream at the water's pace past small wonders: a frog on a stone who watches it go by, a water sprite who rises to give it a gentle push, a little waterfall it tips over with a splash, reeds, a kingfisher, stepping stones. The child can help with a finger: stir the water to steer it off a snag, blow a breeze into the sail by stroking behind it. Different boats behave differently (a big sail runs fast, a walnut shell bobs and spins). The journey ends at a still pond at dusk where the boat nudges in among the lily pads beside the boats sent earlier, and the gnome steps off and lights a tiny lamp. Then the bank again, and the makings for another.

The hand's work is building the boat and then tending its journey. Nothing sinks; a boat that snags waits to be nudged.

### wool-picture
**Wool Picture** · 🧶 · Waldorf wool fleece pictures and felting

A wooden frame holding a sheet of soft felt, sky-blue fading to green. Beside it are baskets of carded wool fleece in plant-dyed colours. The child pulls a tuft from a basket (it draws out thin and wispy) and lays it on the felt, where it clings: green wool laid in a mound is a hill, white wisps are clouds, a yellow tuft teased round is a sun, brown rolled thin is a tree trunk. Pressing and stroking a tuft felts it down and shapes it; pinching pulls it thinner; laying one colour over another blends them softly like mist. There are a few small felt figures in a dish (a sheep, a shepherd child, a cottage, a bird, a gnome) that can be set anywhere in the picture.

The picture is always a little alive in a woolly way: clouds drift as far as their wisps reach, the sun's rays breathe, sheep set on green wool nibble, the bird hops to the tallest thing, smoke curls from the cottage in grey fleece. When the child hangs the frame on its nail (drag it up to the wall), evening light crosses the room and the picture glows; it stays there. Taking down a fresh frame begins another, and the last picture or two stay on the wall.

The hand's work is drawing out and laying wool. It must look and move like real fleece: soft edges, visible fibres, never a hard outline.

### sand-kingdom
**Sand Kingdom** · 🐚 · Waldorf sand and water play; the tide as the day's rhythm

A quiet beach at low tide: firm damp sand, a bucket and spade, a few moulds, a rock pool, and a scatter of shells, pebbles, driftwood, seaweed and gull feathers. Fill the bucket with sand (scoop by dragging the spade) and turn it out for a tower that holds its shape; pat it with a finger to firm it; draw a finger through the sand to dig a moat or carve a road; drip wet sand from a height for lacy spires; press shells in for doors and windows; stand feathers as flags; lay driftwood as a bridge. Sand heaps, holds and crumbles softly at the edges the way real damp sand does.

The sea is far out and coming in, only when the child is ready (drag the sun low). As the light turns gold the first thin wave runs up and fills the moat. Small sea folk come with it: a hermit crab moves into the best shell doorway, a tiny mer-child sits on the highest tower and sings, starfish settle on the walls. The water rises slowly round the castle, takes the outer walls gently, and the kingdom stands as an island in the sunset. That is the ending. By morning (lift the sun) the tide has gone out and left the sand smooth and new, with different treasures on the tideline.

The hand's work is shaping sand. The sea taking it back is part of the play, never a loss.

### leaf-creatures
**Leaf Creatures** · 🍁 · Waldorf autumn nature craft; the wind children

A woodland floor in autumn with a patch of bare earth in the middle and treasures all around: leaves of many shapes and colours, twigs, conkers, acorns and their caps, pine cones, sycamore wings, feathers, berries, seed heads. The child makes a creature on the bare earth by laying things together however they like: a big leaf for a body, twigs for legs or antlers, sycamore wings for ears or wings, a pine cone for a tail, and two acorn caps wherever its eyes should be. Pieces overlap and stay.

When the creature has eyes, a breeze comes through (the child can call it by stroking the air across the scene). The wind children, barely seen, lift the creature, and it wakes: the acorn-cap eyes blink, and it moves the way its parts suggest. Many twig legs scuttle; wings flap and lift it; a long leaf body slithers; something round rolls. It explores the clearing, noses the treasures, follows the child's finger, and plays with any creature made before it. After a while it settles under a fern and sleeps, and is there next time. The earth patch is clear for another.

The hand's work is composing a creature from found things. There is no right animal; the delight is that whatever was made comes alive as itself.

### ice-palace
**Ice Palace** · ❄️ · Waldorf winter festivals; King Winter and the frost children

A snowy clearing under a pale afternoon sky, with a frozen pond. The child cuts building ice from the pond (draw a line across the ice and a clear block lifts out with a ringing crack) and gathers snow (scoop with a mitten: it packs into white bricks and balls). Blocks are set down to build a palace: ice blocks are clear and catch the light, snow bricks are soft white, icicles snapped from a branch make spires, and a wet mitten rubbed along a joint smooths and freezes it. Blocks rest solidly where they are placed. Breathing on an ice block (hold a finger on it) frosts it, and a finger can then draw ferns and stars in the frost.

As the child pulls the dusk down, the low sun shines through the ice and throws coloured light across the snow. The frost children come skating over the pond, hang tiny lights inside the palace so that every clear block glows from within, and old King Winter, tall and kindly in a white cloak, walks through the gate the child built and sits; the northern lights unroll overhead. It stays that way. Morning (lift the sun) brings fresh snowfall on the palace and the pond frozen smooth again.

The hand's work is cutting, carrying and setting ice and snow. The payoff is light: the palace is plain by day and a lantern by night.

### mushroom-village
**Mushroom Village** · 🍄 · Waldorf gnome stories; the earth folk at home

A mossy forest glade after rain. Where the child presses a finger into the moss and holds, a mushroom pushes up: a short press makes a small round one, a longer press a tall one with a wide cap (it stops by itself at a generous size), and the cap takes its colour from where it grows (red with spots in the sun, brown in the shade, pale under ferns). Then the child makes each mushroom a home from a small tray of gnome-made parts: a round door, windows, a chimney, a balcony, a tiny ladder, a lantern, a washing line. Draw a finger across the moss between two doors and a pebble path appears along the line. A stream runs through the glade; a plank or stepping stones laid across it join the two sides.

The gnomes are waiting politely at the edge with their bundles. Each time a mushroom has a door, one moves in: carries its bundle up the path, opens the door, and a light comes on inside. They visit along the paths the child drew, hang washing on the line, sit on a balcony, sweep a doorstep. At dusk (pull the sun down) the lanterns and windows glow across the glade, smoke rises from the chimneys, and a snail with a lamp makes its slow round. It stays. Morning comes when the child lifts the sun; the village is as it was built and can grow.

The hand's work is growing, fitting and path-drawing. The payoff is a village that visibly lives along the lines you laid.

## By request: the Waldorf advent market

### advent-market
**Advent Market** · 🕯️ · the advent fair of a Waldorf school: candle dipping, window stars, wreath binding, gingerbread, and the advent spiral

The owner asked for this one directly: "a game where we are at a Waldorf advent market and whatever they do there you can do too, like candle making". It is a small place to wander with several real crafts in it, so it is bigger than the other prototypes; put each stall in its own file.

The market: a school hall and courtyard on a December afternoon, snow at the windows, fir garlands, paper stars, wooden stalls with cloth awnings, a few simply-drawn families moving quietly about. It is one wide scene the child can slide along with a finger. Each stall shows what is done there by someone doing it, slowly, and touching the stall brings the child up close to do it themselves. Stepping back out is always one obvious touch (the edge of the stall, or the basket). There is no order, nothing is required, and nothing is sold or paid for. The child carries a basket, and whatever they make goes into it.

The stalls (build at least the first four well; the others if they can be done to the same standard):

- **Candle dipping.** A warm pot of golden beeswax and a rack of wicks. Take a wick by its top, lower it into the wax, lift it out, let it drip, and walk it round to cool (a short carry), then dip again. Each dip adds a visible layer: thin as a pencil at first, fat and tapered after ten or so, with a drip forming at the bottom. The child decides when it is done and hangs it on the rack. Dipping too fast just gives a thinner coat. This is the centre of the prototype: the wax must look warm and thick, and the candle must visibly be the one the child made (taller or stubbier, straight or wobbly).
- **Window stars.** Squares of coloured transparent kite paper. Fold a square's corners to the middle with drags along the creases, then fold again to a point; do this for eight, and set them round in a ring where each overlaps the last, pressing each joint. Hold it up to the window and the afternoon light comes through, darker where the folds overlap.
- **Wreath binding.** A straw ring, a pile of fir sprigs, a spool of wire or ribbon. Lay a sprig on the ring and wind the ribbon over its stem by circling a finger; work round until the ring is green. Then add what you like: four candles, pine cones, dried orange slices, red berries, a bow.
- **Gingerbread.** Plain gingerbread hearts, stars and a little house on a board, and a piping bag of white icing: drawing a finger squeezes a line of icing that sits up on the biscuit. Almonds, raisins and sugar pearls to press on.
- **The gnome's crystal cave** (optional): a dim moss-hung corner where a lantern shows a gnome who quietly hands the child one small crystal for the basket. Once per visit; no surprise, no rarity.
- **Apple candle holders** (optional): core an apple with a twist of the finger, stand a small candle in it, tuck a sprig of green beside it.

The ending is the **advent spiral**, in a darkened room off the hall, and the child goes there when they choose (a doorway with one candle glowing beyond it). A spiral of fir boughs is laid on the floor with a single large candle burning at the centre. The child carries their own candle (the one they dipped, in an apple, if they made one; otherwise one is waiting by the door) along the spiral path by drawing a finger slowly along it, lights it from the centre candle, and on the way back out sets it down anywhere along the boughs. Its light stays. Other children's candles are already here and there on the spiral, and a quiet pentatonic melody on a lyre follows the walk. Then the room is still: the spiral glowing, a little brighter for the child's candle. Leaving by the door goes home, where the basket is unpacked onto a windowsill: the star in the window, the wreath on the table, the gingerbread on a plate. A new visit begins by taking the empty basket from its hook; the spiral remembers the candles set down on earlier visits in this session.

The hand's work is different at every stall and each one is a real craft with its real steps. Everything in `GENTLE.md` applies: no words or numbers, no coins or prices, no praise, no hurry, hand-drawn in warm candle-lit colours.

## More looks: four building toys

Added when the owner asked for a wider spread of looks. `GENTLE.md` applies in full, including "Fantasy building" where it fits and the look assigned to each in its "Looks" table.

### glow-pegs
**Glow Pegs** · ✨ · the light-peg board toy (Lite-Brite), dressed as an arcade marquee

A black peg board fills a dark arcade cabinet, with a tray of glowing pegs in eight neon colours beneath it. Touch a colour, then touch or drag across the board: pegs push in one by one with a soft click and each one lights and blooms. Touching a lit peg pulls it out and it drops back to the tray. A chunky physical switch on the bezel turns on mirror mode, so each peg is echoed across the middle (and a second position gives four-way symmetry): pictures become patterns without effort.

The picture is also a tune. A big lever on the side starts the show: a soft light sweeps across the board column by column, and each lit peg sounds as it passes, higher pegs higher, on a pentatonic scale, while the colours pulse and chase like a marquee. It loops gently for as long as the lever is down. Lifting the lever stops it. Sliding the tray out and back clears the board: every peg falls into the tray with a clatter, and the board is dark and ready.

The hand's work is placing light. Nothing is scored and there is no picture to copy. The look is as loud as an arcade; the play is as quiet as a peg board.

### chalk-town
**Chalk Town** · 🖍️ · pavement chalk and toy cars; the rain as the ending

A patch of pavement seen from above on a warm afternoon, with a bucket of fat chalks and a shoebox of toys: three wooden cars, a few little people, a dog. Take a chalk and draw on the ground. Lines are roads: set a toy car down on a chalk line and it drives along it at a toy's pace, takes the turns, chooses a way at junctions, and stops politely at the end of the line to turn round. Closed shapes are places: put a little person inside one and they treat it as home, pottering about inside the outline and walking the chalk roads to visit other shapes. Scribble blue and the dog paddles in it; draw a zigzag and the people hop along it. The chalk wears down as it is used and leaves dust; a rag rubs lines out.

When the child drags the cloud across the sun, the first fat drops darken the tarmac. The rain washes the drawing away slowly, colour running into the cracks and down to the drain, while the toys wait in their shoebox under the lid. Then the sun comes back and the pavement dries from the edges, clean. That is the ending and the beginning.

The hand's work is drawing a town and setting it going. The rain is part of the play, never a loss.

### box-fort
**Box Fort** · 📦 · the cardboard-box den

A living-room floor on a rainy afternoon, with a heap of cardboard boxes of different sizes, a roll of tape, child's scissors, fat markers, a blanket, cushions and a string of fairy lights. Drag boxes together and they stand and stack solidly; a strip of tape pulled between two joins them. Draw the scissors across a box face to cut a flap door or a window (the cut-out piece falls and can be kept as a drawbridge). Scribble on the cardboard with the markers: it stays. Throw the blanket over the top for a roof, push cushions inside, and drape the fairy lights anywhere along the edges.

A teddy and a curious cat are in the room. The cat investigates every new opening as soon as it is cut: head through a window, tail out of a door. The teddy can be carried inside. When the child flicks the room's light switch off, the fort glows from within: light spills from exactly the windows and doors that were cut, the marker drawings show as silhouettes, and the shapes of the openings fall across the wall and ceiling. It stays like that, with the rain on the window. Switching the light back on returns the afternoon, the fort as built.

The hand's work is stacking, cutting and decorating. The payoff is the light through the holes you cut.

### tiny-island
**Tiny Island** · 🏝️ · a wooden toy village set on a little island you can turn

A small green island sits on a turntable in calm water. Drag sideways anywhere on the sea or sky to turn it and look from every side. Beside it is a tray of wooden toy pieces: cottages, trees, a lighthouse, a windmill, a bridge, a jetty and boat, sheep, a well, path stones. Touch a piece and then a place on the island, or drag it there: it settles onto the ground where it is put, the right way up, and stays. Pieces can be picked up and moved, or carried back to the tray.

What is placed comes quietly to life: the windmill turns in the breeze, smoke rises from a cottage, sheep drift toward the nearest tree, the boat makes slow circles from the jetty, a tiny figure walks any path of stones from door to door. When the child pulls the sun down behind the sea, the windows warm, the lighthouse beam sweeps across the water and the island, and stars come out. It stays. Lifting the sun brings morning with the island as it was built.

The hand's work is placing and turning. Nothing is scored and no layout is asked for.
