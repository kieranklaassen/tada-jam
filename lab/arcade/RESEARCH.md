# Arcade round: what the research said

Four parallel web-research passes on 2026-09-30 (ages 2 to 6, ages 6 to 12, game feel, evergreen one-mechanic hits). Reach numbers are all-age downloads or concurrent players as reported by the named source, not counts of children. The ranking and the choice of 30 are judgement.

## Why the first round fell flat

The first 30 prototypes were invented, abstract loops drawn as flat shapes, silent, and scored by a simulated persona panel for "depth on repeat play". 2 of 30 passed that gate and the owner liked none. The research names the same failure several ways: no recognisable fantasy, goal before toy, silent touches, and nothing a watcher could read in three seconds.

This round inverts it: every loop is borrowed from something children already play in huge numbers, every touch answers with sound and motion, and a person rates them by playing.

## What children actually play

**Ages 2 to 6.** The US iPad "Kids 5 & under" chart (fetched 2026-09-30) is, outside the curriculum apps, dollhouses, kitchens, doctor play, rescue vehicles, colouring and licensed characters: Toca Boca World, Bluey: Let's Play, Toca Boca Jr, Toca Jr Kitchen, PAW Patrol Rescue World, Hospital Games, Sago Mini World. The patterns with the strongest evidence:

1. Feed a character and get a face back (Toca Kitchen).
2. Cook, mix, transform (Toca Jr Kitchen, Bluey's pizza oven).
3. Hair salon and makeover (Toca Hair Salon, called Toca's most popular early app by EdSurge).
4. Tap to reveal a surprise (Sago Mini; surprise-egg videos, 5.1B views).
5. Doctor and caring: sad turns happy (Hospital Games on the chart).
6. Rescue vehicles with a job (PAW Patrol Rescue World, DUPLO).
7. Things that roll, fall and pop (Pok Pok Marble Machine, Bluey keepy-uppy).
8. Mess and mischief: in Toca Tea Party playtests the favourite part was spilling.
9. Slapstick and wrongness: a bewildered, never hurt, victim.

**Ages 6 to 12.**

| Loop | Evidence |

| Plant, wait, harvest, sell, buy a weirder seed | Grow a Garden, 22.3M concurrent |

| Drop and merge two of the same | Suika Game, Coolmath top 10 |
| Idle collect, upgrade, numbers go up | Tiny Fishing and Cookie Clicker in Coolmath's top 10 |
| Precision run with instant retry | Run 3 is Coolmath #1; Geometry Dash 17.4M monthly |
| Serve orders | Papa's Pizzeria, Monkey Mart |
| Launch and upgrade, physics stunts | Coolmath top 10 (Eggy Car, Moto X3M) |

What this age wants that little children do not: competition and mastery, transgression without consequences (the two biggest hits of 2025-26 are about stealing), rarity and surprise, building and owning, numbers going up.

**Evergreen one-finger verbs.** Swipe to slice (Fruit Ninja, 1B), pull back and fling at a stack (Angry Birds), steer a hole that swallows and grows (Hole.io, 420M), rub to clean (PowerWash Simulator), draw a line that turns solid (Brain Dots, Crayon Physics), dig a path for water (Where's My Water), tap to hop lanes (Crossy Road, 200M), aim a ball into pegs (Peggle), tap what pops up, time a claw drop, five-second microgames (WarioWare, Dumb Ways to Die), break bricks with a paddle.

Cut on purpose: Flappy Bird and Getting Over It (the hook is frustration), 2048 and Tetris (abstract and numeric, the first round's failure mode), tower defence (the fun is unit variety, not one verb), anything that needs other people (hidden-role tag, roleplay towns).

## What makes a small prototype feel fun

The builder's checklist, from Sesame Workshop's touch guidance, Nielsen Norman Group's children's UX work, Voodoo and Supersonic publishing guidance, "Juice it or lose it", Eiserloh's and Nijman's screen-shake talks, Swink's Game Feel, Koster's Theory of Fun, and Toca Boca, Sago Mini and Pok Pok on their own design:

1. Respond on touch-down, in the same frame, with sound and motion and a particle.
2. Every action has a sound; vary the pitch a little each time; climb a scale on a streak.
3. The basic verb is fun in an empty room before any goal exists (Miyamoto tuned Mario alone until moving was fun).
4. Toy first: poking anything does something.
5. Readable in three seconds with no words.
6. One satisfying payoff that repeats every few seconds.
7. Forgiving: little or no fail state; if there is one, retry is instant.
8. Fudge everything in the child's favour: hit areas larger than the drawing, partial drags accepted.
9. Tap and drag only. No double tap, pinch, or required second finger.
10. Targets at least 2 cm (about 100 logical pixels) for under-sixes; nothing important on the bottom edge.
11. Touchable things idle-animate and look touchable; nothing else does.
12. A hint after five or so seconds without a touch.
13. Something new to learn every 30 to 60 seconds.
14. Mess and mischief are allowed.
15. Leave marks: debris, splats and trails stay on screen.

Five tests for the first 30 seconds: the empty-room test (is the verb pleasant alone?), the mash test (does random tapping always delight, never punish?), the three-second read, the again test (does the hand go back?), and the second-try-better test (does attempt two differ from attempt one?).

Fun killers: silent touches, abstract shapes with no face, wrong-answer buzzers and fail screens, any wait before retry, linear tweens and objects that stop dead, the same sound every repeat, precision gestures and small targets, a pattern exhausted in 20 seconds, and juice poured over a dull verb.
